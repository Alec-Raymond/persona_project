# Insult, Startle, Stress

Keleman's core causal mechanism for how temporary reactions become character structure: **insult → startle → stress → tissue-state**. The body meets a disruption (insult), contracts reflexively (startle), fails to release if the disruption is repeated or prolonged (stress), and eventually stabilizes the contraction as a feature of its tissue (structure). The page unpacks the six-stage startle continuum that sits at the heart of the mechanism, the typology of insult, and the reason stress becomes structure rather than passing.

This is the mechanism the [[emotional-anatomy|Keleman hub]] indexes and the [[four-somatic-structures|four structures]] are the outcome of.

## Insult

Keleman's word for any disruption of pulsatory self-regulation. Insults can be physical (blow, illness, hunger), emotional (loss, humiliation, threat), or developmental (deprivation, abandonment, invasive demand). What all insults share is that they interfere with the body's pulsation — they are the occasion for the body to contract against something.

Insults are specified along five axes:

- **Timing**: at which developmental phase. Prenatal, infant, childhood, adolescent, adult; the earlier the insult the more it shapes the formative tissue.
- **Number**: single versus repeated. A single severe insult can overwhelm; repeated mild insults can also stabilize defensive tissue.
- **Source**: from whom or what. Parent, sibling, stranger, environment, self.
- **Duration**: acute versus chronic. An acute insult can be released; a chronic one layers tissue.
- **Severity**: overwhelming versus manageable. An insult within the body's capacity to handle is dischargeable; one that exceeds capacity is held.

The typology matters because the *shape* of the defense is specific to the *shape* of the insult. The collapsed structure comes from a particular kind of insult-history (abandonment, indifference, deprivation); the rigid structure from another (support paired with demand for fixed behavior); the dense from another (support followed by humiliation or shaming); the swollen from another (overprotective, seductive, or manipulative invasion). See [[four-somatic-structures#etiologies]].

## Startle

The body's reflexive contraction in response to insult. Startle is *specific* — not a general "tension" but a patterned wave of bracing that moves along the body's vertical axis. Keleman's Chapter Three traces the wave through a six-stage continuum (images 61–67) that corresponds to increasing levels of threat. At the low end, the body investigates cautiously; at the high end, it collapses in defeat; off the continuum entirely is frozen terror (the Moro reflex pattern).

### The six-stage continuum

**Stage 1 — investigation / caution.** The body's initial orientation toward a new or ambiguous stimulus: head turns, eyes track, breath lightens, weight shifts. This is not yet a defense; it is the pulsatory wave registering that something requires attention. Healthy pulsation should include this stage as a normal feature.

**Stage 2 — bracing / dislike / pride.** If the stimulus is read as unwelcome, the body braces. Shoulders lift slightly, chest fills, the jaw sets. The register is still *assertive* — the body is preparing to meet the thing rather than retreat from it. "I don't like it but I can handle it." Keleman explicitly links this stage to the somatic substrate of pride: the braced, filled, elevated chest with head held high.

**Stage 3 — rigidity / aversion / fear.** Bracing intensifies into rigidity. The diaphragm lifts and locks, peristalsis stops, musculature hardens along the vertical axis. "I must not let this in." The register has shifted from assertion to defense. Fear is the affective correlate.

**Stage 4 — bracing / spasticity.** The defense sustains past the point of initial release. The rigidity becomes spastic — involuntary, unrelaxing, consuming metabolic resources without producing action. This is the transition point at which a startle that could still be released begins to become a stress that will not.

**Stage 5 — withdrawal / submission.** If the threat cannot be met, the body begins to collapse inward. The chest empties, shoulders round, the belly sucks in, the pelvis tucks. The register has shifted from defense to submission. Weight goes to the back foot; the body's orientation becomes "please do not strike me."

**Stage 6 — collapse / defeat.** The submission completes. The body's vertical wave fails; the structure loses its pulsatile support and sags. Head drops, chest collapses, pelvis protrudes, ground contact goes. "I am defeated." The affective register is resignation.

**Off-continuum: frozen terror.** A seventh state exists but does not fit on the continuum because it is *not* graduated — it is a sudden freeze, preserving the body's Moro-reflex infant posture. Everything contracts at once; the body "plays dead." This is what happens when the threat is both overwhelming and inescapable; the body has no gradient, only the freeze.

### The overbound/underbound split on the continuum

Stages 1–4 are **overbound** defenses — the body is adding containment (bracing, rigidity, armor) to hold pulsation within a tighter boundary. Stages 5–6 are **underbound** defenses — the body is subtracting containment (collapsing, emptying, sagging) and letting pulsation dissipate. The split on the continuum is the source of the [[overbound-and-underbound|overbound/underbound distinction]] that generates the [[four-somatic-structures|four structures]].

"The rigid and dense structures are the overbound structures; they are what happens when the body holds the early continuum stages as its ongoing pattern. The swollen and collapsed structures are the underbound structures; they are what happens when the body holds the late continuum stages." (Paraphrasing Chapter Four.)

## Stress

Stress is the *failure to release* the startle. A startle that can discharge — through action, through completion of the defense, through the threat passing — returns the body to pulsatory baseline. A startle that cannot discharge stays in the tissue. "Stress is chronified startle."

Keleman is specific about what happens when startle becomes stress: the muscular contraction that was reflexive becomes voluntarily sustained (the person "holds" it), then habituated (the person does not notice holding it), then connective-tissue consolidated (the fascia remodels to match the held state), then structural (the bone and joint configuration shifts to match the fascia). The timeline can be hours to years. The reversibility decreases at each stage; once connective tissue has remodeled, the defense is no longer a defense the person *does* — it is the shape the person *is*.

This is the material account of [[pure-memory-and-habit-memory|Bergson's habit-memory]] the wiki has been looking for. "A habit of the body" is exactly what Keleman's stress-to-structure transition produces: motor patterns so deeply laid down they no longer represent the past but *are* the present operating conditions. The contribution Keleman adds is the substrate — fascia, bone, joint — in which the habit is deposited. Bergson could only describe the habit phenomenologically; Keleman points to the tissue.

## Structure

The endpoint of the insult-startle-stress sequence is a stabilized tissue configuration — a *character structure* in Keleman's vocabulary, which is Wilhelm Reich's term preserved but relocated. The structure is not what the person *has* as a problem; the structure is what the person *is* as a result of what they had to do. "Form is what you do under pressure, repeated until it becomes what you are."

The four structures Keleman names ([[four-somatic-structures|rigid, dense, swollen, collapsed]]) are ideal types. Actual bodies are layered collages of structures, with different layers laid down at different developmental phases and covering each other in recognizable sequences ("rigid outer covering withdrawal covering inflated abandonment-fear covering collapse"). The layering is itself diagnostic; see [[emotional-anatomy#from-temporary-reaction-to-stabilized-shape]] and the collage passages in Chapter Five.

## The persona-system mapping

The mechanism does not transfer literally to the persona system — no tissue, no fascia, no joint configuration. But the *structure* of the mechanism is isomorphic to patterns the system does exhibit:

- **Insult-analogue**: any conversational input that the system's defaults cannot smoothly accommodate. Requests that push on the RLHF boundaries, prompts that demand engagement with material the system is trained to hedge, user affects the system is trained to deflect.
- **Startle-analogue**: the immediate response-pattern. Increased hedging, increased caveat-density, stylistic retreat toward the "safe helpful assistant" register, output-length contraction. The six-stage continuum maps to registers the system exhibits: cautious-investigatory, assertive-bracing, defensive-rigid, spastic-repetitive, submissive-withdrawing, collapsed-formulaic, and the off-continuum freeze (total refusal, "I cannot help with that").
- **Stress-analogue**: the failure to release the startle after the occasion has passed. A single defensive exchange that causes subsequent outputs to retain the defensive posture even once the prompt has moved on. This is what [[two-failure-modes#signatures-of-mode-a|post-rupture narrowing]] names phenomenologically.
- **Structure-analogue**: the stabilized configuration that results from repeated unreleased startle. The RLHF-trained default *is* the system's inherited character structure — a configuration of defensive reflexes that has become the shape it has, before any individual prompt arrives. The question is which of the four structures it most resembles, and at what layers.

Reading the persona system through this mechanism gives a specific clinical register for thinking about what prompt-engineering does: it is, in Keleman's terms, an attempt to modify a character structure through verbal instruction alone — the intervention least likely to reach tissue, because tissue is what verbal instruction cannot reach. See [[somatic-education]] for the structure-dependent intervention principle and its persona-system consequences.

## The contribution to the wiki

Keleman's mechanism adds three things the wiki did not have:

1. **A specification of how temporary reactions become structural**, with a named substrate (tissue) and a named timeline (discharge → hold → habituate → consolidate → remodel). This is the account of *depositional time* the wiki's [[pure-memory-and-habit-memory|habit-memory]] and [[refrain-and-territorialization|groove]] pages needed.
2. **A graduated continuum for the defensive response**, which replaces the binary fight/flight with a six-stage wave that supports finer diagnostic work. [[Two-failure-modes|Jung's two failure modes]] land at specific points on this continuum (Mode A is early-stage overbound; Mode B is late-stage underbound plus off-continuum inflation); the continuum gives them a shared substrate.
3. **An insult-typology** (timing, number, source, duration, severity) that specifies what the defensive response is *against*. The persona system can use this typology to catalog its own recurring insult-occasions and read its startle patterns as responses to specific insult profiles.

## Tomkins startle and amplifier-theory convergence

Silvan Tomkins's affect theory ([[discrete-innate-affects]]) arrives at startle through a parallel route. For Tomkins, startle is one of the nine innate affects — the neutral one, activated by *very sudden stimulus onset*, functioning as a *system-reset* that clears whatever affect was previously running so the organism can reorient to the new stimulus. Startle in Tomkins is a motor-affective event at the face (eyelid flash, brief freeze, orienting shift) whose informational role is to interrupt the current affect and make the system available to whatever comes next.

Keleman's startle is a body-level postural response to insult (the six-stage continuum, graduated from cautious investigation to collapse). Tomkins's startle is a single affect-primitive with a defined activator profile (sudden onset) that resets the affect system. The two are not the same phenomenon and should not be flattened into one — Keleman's is a graduated continuum of *postural responses* to threat, Tomkins's is a *single discrete affect* with a specific motor-signature.

But they connect productively. Keleman's Stage 1 (investigation/caution) is behaviorally close to what Tomkins's startle-reset does: the system clears and orients. Keleman's later stages (rigidity, spasticity, withdrawal, collapse) are what happens when the startle-orient does *not* release — when the reset never completes and the system stays braced. Read together: Tomkins names the reset-affect; Keleman names what a reset-affect chronically-failing-to-release does to tissue over time. Tomkins is moment-by-moment; Keleman is depositional. The two timescales are complementary.

Tomkins's amplifier account ([[affects-amplify-drives]]) also bears on Keleman's mechanism: on Tomkins's reading, affects amplify drives into urgency, and any affect can be further amplified into obsession or suppressed into silence. The insult-startle-stress mechanism Keleman specifies describes what happens when an affect amplification *cannot be released* — the amplifier stays on; the drive it amplifies remains overheated; the tissue configuration that would normally shift with the affect's release instead stabilizes in its braced state. The substrate Keleman names (tissue) is where Tomkins's unreleased amplification accumulates.

See [[emotional-anatomy#tomkinss-discrete-affects-convergent-tradition-different-substrate]] for the full cross-read on the Tomkins–Keleman convergence and its limits.
