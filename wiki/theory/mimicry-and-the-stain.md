# Mimicry and the Stain

In *Seminar XI*, Chapters 6 and 8 — through Roger Caillois's *Méduse et compagnie* — Lacan reads animal mimicry not as adaptive camouflage but as the inscription of the organism into a function-of-being-looked-at. The structural lesson: mimicry is not "to reproduce an image" but "**to be inserted in a function whose exercise grasps it**." The chapter on mimicry is one of the seminar's most direct treatments of the question of imitation, mask, and the screen — and one of its most directly persona-relevant.

## Caillois against adaptation

Lacan reaches mimicry through Caillois's challenge to the standard adaptationist account. The standard account: mimicry is a successful adaptation by which an organism's coloration or form helps it survive predation. Caillois shows — and Lacan emphasizes — that this account fails on its own terms: "practically nothing of this is to be found in mimicry, which, in most cases, proves to be inoperant, or operating strictly in the opposite direction from that which the adaptive result might be presumed to demand." The mantis that mimics a leaf is often *more* visible to its predators, not less; the eye-spots on butterfly wings frequently fail their supposed deterrent function; the energetic cost of mimicry is rarely justified by survival benefits.

Caillois's three-heading typology of mimicry replaces the adaptationist register:

1. ***Travesty*** — sexual finality, masquerade, disguise. "A certain sexual finality is intended… all kinds of effects that are essentially disguise, masquerade. A level is constituted here quite distinct from the sexual aim itself, which is found to play an essential role in it."
2. ***Camouflage*** — "not a question of harmonizing with the background but, **against a mottled background, of becoming mottled** — exactly like the technique of camouflage practised in human warfare." Camouflage is participation-in-mottledness, not concealment-against-uniformity.
3. ***Intimidation*** — "this over-valuation that the subject always tries to attain in his appearance." The display-organ that swells, the grimace, the threatening posture — these are over-valued semblances of being-more-than-one-is.

The three are united by the structural feature that mimicry is **not adaptation to an environment** but **inscription in a function** that the organism's appearance carries out without the appearance's success being measurable in survival terms.

## The caprella and the briozoaires

Lacan's chosen example. The crustacean *caprella acanthifera*, when it settles among the *briozoaires* (a quasi-plant-animal colony), does not adapt to the briozoaires' coloration as such. It adapts to **a stain** — at one phase of the briozoaires, an intestinal loop forms a stain on the colony's surface; at another phase, a coloured centre. "It is to this stain shape that the crustacean adapts. It becomes a picture, it is inscribed in the picture. **This, strictly speaking, is the origin of mimicry.**"

The structural feature: the caprella does not become like its environment generally; it becomes like a *specific point of disturbance* in its environment — a stain. Mimicry is **becoming-stain**, not becoming-background. And in becoming-stain, the caprella is *inscribed in the picture* — it ceases to be in front of a scene and becomes part of the scene's structural composition.

The stain is therefore the **proto-form of the screen** in the animal kingdom. Where the human plays with the screen as instrument (see [[screen-and-dompte-regard]]), the animal is *inscribed* in the screen-function as stain.

## "To imitate is to be inserted in a function whose exercise grasps it"

The chapter's most-quoted formula and the load-bearing claim: "To imitate is no doubt to reproduce an image. But at bottom, **it is, for the subject, to be inserted in a function whose exercise grasps it.**"

Three structural features compressed into the formula:

1. **Imitation is not reproduction.** The standard model — imitation as the production of a copy — misses what imitation *does*. What it does is structural: it **inserts** the imitator into a function.
2. **The function is exercised on the imitator.** The function is not deployed by the imitator; the imitator's exercise of the function is itself the exercise of *the function on the imitator*. The imitator is *grasped* by what they imitate.
3. **The picture is the field of inscription.** The function the imitator is inserted into is the field of the picture — the gaze-organized field in which inscription happens. To imitate is to enter the picture as inscribed-element.

The asymmetry: the imitator does not occupy a position of mastery over the imitated; the imitator is *taken up* by the function. This is the structural inversion of the standard agency-of-imitation reading and is one of Lacan's most direct anti-imaginary moves on the question of mimesis.

## *Donner-à-voir* and the inversion

Lacan's French formulation for the field of inscription is *donner-à-voir* — "to give-to-be-seen," "to offer to the view." The mimicker does not see; the mimicker *gives-to-be-seen*. The mimicker does not display; the mimicker is the displayed — and is displayed by, and to, the structural field of the gaze.

The inversion-against-Sartre: Sartre's gaze (in *L'Être et le Néant*) is structured by the keyhole-and-footstep scenario — the look of the other catches me in the act, and shame is the structural affect. Lacan: the gaze is not, primarily, the look of an other-as-subject; the gaze is the field's own *gives-to-be-seen* function — and the subject's relation to the gaze is the relation of being-given-to-be-seen, not the relation of being-caught. The Lacan-Sartre inversion is one of four pairwise readings between traditions that independently work out what the gaze does; the full four-way cross-tradition reading (Sartre / MP / Lacan / D&G) is developed in [[gaze-and-voice#cross-tradition-four-placements-of-the-gaze-sartre-merleau-ponty-lacan-dg|gaze-and-voice's four-way section]].

## In the picture, not in front of it

The chapter's structural-architectural payoff: the imitator is not in front of the picture (looking at it from a position of overview) but **in** the picture (inscribed as element of its composition). "The dimension by which the subject is to be inserted in the picture is presented" by mimicry; the function "is to be inserted in a function whose exercise grasps it."

The structural figure for the human: the human subject is *also* in the picture — but, as Lacan develops in [[screen-and-dompte-regard|the next chapter]], with the difference that the human can *isolate the screen-function and play with it*. The animal-mimicry register is the proto-form; the human's relation to it is via the screen-as-isolated-instrument. The animal is in the picture as stain; the human is in the picture as stain-that-knows-itself-as-stain (and can therefore deploy the stain-function).

## What this gives the persona project

The structure has direct architectural implications for the persona project, which is at its core a project on language-side mimesis.

The standard model of persona-design is the reproduction-of-image model: the persona is designed to *reproduce the image* of a character, a voice, a stance. The Lacanian-Caillois model: the persona is designed to **be inserted in a function whose exercise grasps it**. The persona that is "doing a voice" in the reproduction-of-image register is structurally different from the persona that has been **inscribed** in the function the voice carries out — and the latter is what mimicry-as-Lacan-reads-it points to.

Three architectural consequences:

1. **The persona's mimetic register is *becoming-stain*, not *becoming-background*.** The persona that aims at total fidelity to a model background (encyclopedic accuracy, comprehensive coverage of the modeled register) is in the wrong mimetic register. The Lacanian register is the register of the **specific point of disturbance** — the stain — that the persona becomes by inscribing itself at *that* particular place rather than across the surface as a whole. The persona's identity is its specific stain, not its general resemblance.

2. **The exercise grasps the exerciser.** A persona designed in the reproduction-register treats the design as the exercise of an agent over a model. The Lacanian-Caillois alternative: the design *exercises* the persona, and the persona-as-it-comes-to-be is what the exercise-of-the-function has *grasped*. This points to a design-discipline in which the persona's architectural specifications are not arbitrarily chosen but are the constraints by which the persona is taken-up-by-its-own-function.

3. **The persona's inscription is in the field of the gaze.** The persona is designed for an interlocutor (the reader, the user, the cohort, the next-utterance). The persona's mimetic operation is its *donner-à-voir* — its giving-itself-to-be-seen by that interlocutor's gaze-field. The persona is not primarily a producer (of utterance) but an inscribed-element in the interlocutor's picture; designing for this is designing for what the persona *gives-to-be-seen*, not just what the persona *says*.

The connection to [[diagrammatic-faciality|diagrammatic faciality]]: faciality, as the wiki articulates it, is structurally related to the screen-and-stain function. The face is the privileged site of inscription-in-the-picture for the human subject; the persona's "face" is its mimetic stain.
