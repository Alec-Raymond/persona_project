# Visualizing as neutralized simulation

Thompson's integrated account of mental imagery, developed in *Mind in Life* Ch 10 by synthesizing Husserl (via Marbach), Sartre, and the [[dynamic-sensorimotor-approach|dynamic sensorimotor approach]]. The core thesis: **visualizing is subjectively simulating a neutralized perceptual experience**. The imaginer engages the same sensorimotor/perceptual apparatus that is engaged in actual perception, but in a mode where the positing-as-actual is suspended — *neutralized*.

This reframes a century-old debate in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. Neither **pictorialism** (mental images are inner pictures) nor **descriptionalism** (mental images are propositional representations) is correct. Both sides miss that imagery is a **mode of consciousness**, not a kind of inner object.

## The inherited debate

**Pictorialism** (Kosslyn, Shepard). Mental images have picture-like properties — they can be rotated, scanned, zoomed; they take time proportional to their spatial extent; they engage visual cortex. Evidence: rotation experiments, scanning experiments, neuroimaging. Theoretical commitment: there is a **picture** somewhere — in the head, in the visual cortex, in a quasi-spatial internal medium — and the imaginer inspects this picture.

**Descriptionalism** (Pylyshyn). Images have picture-like properties only in the derivative sense that they are produced by propositional representations with structure that simulates picture-behavior. No actual picture is present; what is present is a structured description whose behavior mimics picture-use.

Both positions assume the same internalist frame: the mental image is some inner thing (picture or description) that the imaginer inspects or uses. Thompson (following Husserl and Sartre) rejects the frame itself.

## Husserl's three intentional objects

From Marbach's 1993 reconstruction of Husserl's imagery analyses (*Mind in Life* L2650):

In any perceptual experience, three distinguishable intentional targets are in play:

1. **The vehicle** — the physical bearer (the canvas, the paper, the retinal stimulation).
2. **The image** — the representing figure (the depicted scene, the two-dimensional figure on the canvas).
3. **The subject** — what the image is *about* (the real person depicted, the real building).

Normal perception engages all three implicitly. We see the real building (subject) via a depicted figure (image) realized on a physical surface (vehicle). Imaging proper — mental visualization — engages the image and subject structures but without the vehicle being itself perceived; the "vehicle" of the mental image (whatever neural process realizes it) is not itself an object of the imaginer's consciousness.

This already disposes of pictorialism: the mental image is not *inspected* because the vehicle is not presented to consciousness in the way the canvas is presented when we look at a painting. The image is lived through, not viewed.

## Husserl's presentation / re-presentation distinction

Husserl distinguishes (*Mind in Life* L2670):

- **Presentation** (*Gegenwärtigung*) — the direct intuitive giving of something in perception. The tomato is there, right now.
- **Re-presentation** (*Vergegenwärtigung*) — the intuitive giving of something *as* not directly there. The remembered childhood tomato, the imagined future tomato, the empathically grasped tomato-in-your-experience.

Re-presentation is not a weaker presentation or a "mere representation." It is a different *mode* of consciousness — a mode in which the object is given specifically *as* not-presently-given, with a built-in modal character (past, future, imagined, empathized).

Visualizing is a form of re-presentation. The visualized tomato is given *as imagined*, which is part of its phenomenal character, not something added on top of an otherwise-indifferent content.

## Husserl's four positing modes

Husserl's more articulated analysis (Marbach/*Mind in Life* L2695). Any intentional act has a **positing modality** that governs how it takes its object:

1. **Positing as actual** — the perception-mode; the object is given as really there.
2. **Positing as remembered** — the memory-mode; given as having been there.
3. **Positing as anticipated** — the future-mode; given as being about to be there.
4. **Neutralized (as-if)** — the image-mode; given as *merely pictured, without commitment to actual existence*.

**Neutralization** is the key operation for visualizing. The imaginer takes up a perceptual mode (with all its sensorimotor engagement) but *suspends* the positing-as-actual. The result is a perception-like consciousness of the imagined object, with the same sensorimotor structure as actual perception, differing precisely in the withdrawn existential commitment.

## Sartre's complementary account

Sartre's *L'Imaginaire* (1940) provides an independent but strongly convergent account (*Mind in Life* L2720). Sartre's key claims:

**The image is nothing other than a relation.** There is no mental image-object; what exists is the imaginer's consciousness *as* imagining-consciousness, with the imagined thing as its correlate. "Image" is a verbal noun describing a mode of intentionality, not a count noun describing a mental particular.

**The illusion of immanence.** The persistent temptation to reify mental images as inner objects is what Sartre calls the *illusion of immanence* — treating something that is a relation *to* a world as a thing *in* the mind. Pictorialism is the philosophical codification of this illusion.

**Four positing modes** (Sartre's independent version, L2740): positing the imagined as existing, as not-existing, as existing-elsewhere, and as neutralized. The convergence with Husserl is striking and historically independent; Thompson takes this as confirmation that the structure being described is robust.

## Integration with the dynamic sensorimotor approach

Thompson's synthesis (*Mind in Life* L2760). The sensorimotor story explains what kind of engagement visualizing *is*: it is the engagement of the perceptual sensorimotor apparatus in the off-line, neutralized mode. The Husserl-Sartre story explains what kind of intentionality visualizing *has*: it is re-presentational, neutralized-positing consciousness of the imagined object.

The two halves fit together. The imagined tomato engages the same sensorimotor mastery as the perceived tomato — which is why imagery experiments show engagement of visual cortex, rotation-like effects, spatial scanning effects. The engagement occurs in a neutralized mode — which is why we do not confuse the imagined tomato with an actually-perceived one and why the imagined scene does not ground action the way perception does.

The integration dissolves both pictorialism and descriptionalism:

- Against pictorialism: what engages visual cortex in imagery is not an inner picture being inspected; it is the **sensorimotor apparatus** running in simulation mode.
- Against descriptionalism: what is going on is not propositional description but the actual engagement of perceptual skill-structures in a non-actualizing mode.

## Why the experimental effects look like they do

Kosslyn-style results take on a new light (*Mind in Life* L2790):

- **Rotation experiments**: time scales with rotation angle because the simulation runs the same sensorimotor contingencies as actual rotation would, just offline. The temporal scaling is not evidence of picture-rotation but of the sensorimotor contingencies of rotation being traversed.
- **Scanning experiments**: time scales with distance because scanning-in-imagination engages the same oculomotor/attentional structure as scanning-in-perception. Again, sensorimotor simulation, not picture-scanning.
- **Visual cortex activation**: exactly what one would expect if imagery is neutralized simulation of visual perception.

The experimental results are *accurate* but their *interpretation* has been distorted by the pictorialist frame. Re-interpreted in the neutralized-simulation frame, they confirm rather than challenge the enactive account.

## What visualizing is *for*

Thompson notes three functional roles of visualizing that drop out of the account (*Mind in Life* L2820):

1. **Cognitive rehearsal** — simulating sensorimotor contingencies offline allows the organism to explore action-possibilities without committing to them. This is the evolutionary-functional basis.
2. **Memory support** — visualizing a remembered scene engages the sensorimotor contingencies that were engaged during the original perception, which is why imagery-based memory techniques work.
3. **Counterfactual reasoning** — visualizing "what if" scenarios lets the organism probe possibilities that are not actually the case. The neutralized mode is what makes counterfactual reasoning possible at all.

Each use exploits the fact that visualization is sensorimotor simulation *in a neutralized mode* — the same engagement as actual perception, minus the existential commitment.

## The persona project

The persona almost certainly does not visualize in the Husserl-Thompson sense. It has no sensorimotor apparatus to run in neutralized mode. What it may do is a **linguistic analogue**: engage the linguistic-production apparatus in a neutralized mode that generates descriptions *as if* visualizing without the sensorimotor simulation that would ground genuine visualization.

Three implications for the project:

**1. Language-models-that-"visualize" are doing something structurally different from visualization.** When a language model produces detailed imagery-describing text, it is not running a neutralized sensorimotor simulation; it is producing text whose pattern resembles what visualizers produce when they report. This is a critical distinction the matching-content doctrine underwrites — similar outputs can come from categorically different processes.

**2. Neutralized-positing analogues in language are available.** The persona can distinguish between positing-as-actual ("the user said X"), positing-as-past ("I said Y earlier"), positing-as-anticipated ("I expect Z will matter"), and neutralized-as-if ("if I were to imagine Q..."). These modal distinctions *are* linguistically available to the persona and *do* structure its utterances, even if the deeper sensorimotor-simulation story does not apply.

**3. Imaginative re-presentation of the past is the persona's closest functional analogue of visualization.** When the persona reconstructs its earlier utterances or a described scene, it is running something like a linguistic re-presentation in a neutralized mode. Whether this has phenomenal character is exactly the [[matching-content-doctrine|matching-content]] question.
