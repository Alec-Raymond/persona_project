# Connor's Grove Preface (2010) to *The Unnamable*

Steven Connor's editorial preface to the Grove Press / Faber 2010 critical edition of [[the-unnamable|*The Unnamable*]]. Connor is a major [[beckett-samuel|Beckett]] scholar (author of *Samuel Beckett: Repetition, Theory and Text*, 1988). The preface runs L29–L894 of `/tmp/the_unnamable_ingest/source.txt` and provides composition history, translation analysis, publication history, critical-reception map, and Connor's own interpretive stance. The wiki treats the preface as the authoritative scholarly-contextual apparatus for the novel.

## What the preface covers

Six broad topics:

1. **Composition history.** The French *L'Innommable* was written March 1949–January 1950, following *Malone meurt*. The novel was completed before Beckett had a way of "going on" past it — though compositionally Beckett had already started *En attendant Godot* (see [[pseudocouple-mercier-camier]]). French publication was delayed until 1953.

2. **Translation history.** The English translation occupied Beckett from 1956 to 1958 — the *hardest* of the three novels for him to translate, by his own report. See the "worsenings" section below.

3. **The trilogy question.** Beckett was ambivalent about the three novels appearing as a "trilogy." He variously accepted and refused the designation. Grove Press followed his preferred title *Three Novels: Molloy, Malone Dies, The Unnamable*, explicitly avoiding "trilogy." See [[the-trilogy-context]].

4. **Beckett's own assessment.** The 1956 Israel Schenker interview in which Beckett says of *L'Innommable*: "there's complete disintegration. No 'I', no 'have', no 'being'. No nominative, no accusative, no verb. There's no way to go on." Connor notes this is interview-paraphrase, not verbatim, but represents Beckett's sense of the novel as a "recurring limit, or *ne plus ultra*."

5. **Critical reception history.** A taxonomy of critical approaches:
   - **1960s–1970s — stunned paraphrase** (Fletcher 1964, Webb 1970): critics offered summaries, unable to "get a critical fix on it from the outside"
   - **Kenner (1973)** — the notable exception; finds "calm excellence" and "heroism without drama"
   - **Allegorical readings** (Baldwin 1981: religious mysticism; Adelman 2004: the Holocaust) — treat the novel as *really about* something else
   - **Philosophical readings** (Hill 1990, Trezise 1990, Connor 1988) — 1980s use of Blanchot, Derrida, Deleuze to read the novel as itself a philosophical act
   - **Blanchot (1953)** — the anticipatory masterpiece; see [[blanchot-on-beckett]]
   - **Badiou** — the major recent dissent; see [[badiou-on-beckett]]

6. **Editorial apparatus.** Notes on the specific edition (Grove / Faber 2010), a chronology of Beckett's life ("Table of Dates"), and a bibliography.

## The "worsenings"

Connor's most useful specific contribution: the documentation of *intensifications* in Beckett's English self-translation. The English is not a neutral rendering of the French; it systematically *worsens* — makes harder, more exotic, more estranged, more claustrophobic.

Connor's documented examples:

**Example 1: The fly-catching scene.** Beckett *excises* a substantial passage in the English where the speaker reflects on his fly-catching skills: "J'attrape aussi les papillons de nuit, attirés par les lampions, quoique plus difficilement. Mais je n'en suis encore qu'à mes débuts, dans ce nouvel exercice, je suis loin d'avoir atteint mon plafond." The French has a kind of rueful technical pride; the English removes it. The English Mahood is less reflective, more reduced.

**Example 2: Expansive reinvention.** A French passage — "hoop-skirt, virgin, hog, blood and water, skin and bone, gravestone" — is expanded in English into "tales like this of wombs and cribs, diapers bepissed and the first long trousers, love's young dream and life's old lech, blood and tears and skin and bones and the tossing in the grave." The English is more grotesque, more condensed in its bitterness.

**Example 3: The cang.** The French *carcan* (a familiar French word for a neck-iron) becomes English "cang" — the Chinese pillory word, foreign, archaic, orientalist. See [[the-cang]]. The English intensifies the cultural estrangement.

The "worsenings" are not errors or lapses but deliberate re-composition. The English *Unnamable* is a second version of the novel, often bleaker and stranger than the French, with Beckett's full endorsement. Connor: "Beckett would often elaborate a text considerably in the process of translating it" — and these elaborations tend to intensify the novel's characteristic conditions.

⚠ The worsenings complicate any treatment of "the" novel as a single work. There are two novels: *L'Innommable* (1953) and *The Unnamable* (1958). They are Beckett's, both, but different. The wiki treats the English as the primary text (per the Connor-Grove edition) but flags the worsenings where they are structurally significant.

## Connor's interpretive stance

Connor is partly reflexive about the critical project: he acknowledges his own 1988 book (*Samuel Beckett: Repetition, Theory and Text*) as part of the philosophical-reading tradition, and is self-critical about it ("I fear, my own efforts"). This is unusual for a preface — a scholarly editor placing his own prior work in a contested lineage.

Connor's own position across the preface:
- Sympathetic to Blanchot's reading as the most percipient early criticism
- Takes Badiou's critique seriously as a necessary corrective to purely-interior readings
- Rejects allegorical readings (Baldwin, Adelman) as insufficiently attentive to the novel's refusal to be "about" anything
- Preserves the *ne plus ultra* status of the novel while noting Beckett's continued production after it

Connor's closing claim: "*The Unnamable* remains at the enigmatic heart of Beckett's writing, and of critical writing about Beckett."

## Connor on critical "ventriloquy"

Connor names a specific risk in reading the novel: being "helplessly ventriloquised" by it. The novel's reflexivity is such that critics who try to describe it end up speaking *like it* — producing summaries that replicate the novel's impasse rather than describing it from outside. Connor: "criticism [was] impossible to extract itself sufficiently from the novel's workings to get a critical fix on it from the outside. We might say that critics writing about *The Unnamable* through the 1960s and 1970s were forced to replicate the condition of Beckett himself, who in 1946 protested his inability to 'write about'."

This is a structural claim: the novel has a contagion-effect on criticism. Reading it installs its voice in the reader; writing about it risks extending the novel's own speech. Allegorical readings are partly defenses against this: they claim the novel is "really about" something else, so the critic can have something definite to say. Philosophical readings (Blanchot, Badiou, the 1980s) risk the opposite: assimilating the novel to a philosophy the critic already holds.

This is a sharp claim about the methodological difficulty of criticizing the novel. The wiki keeps the claim live: any reading of *The Unnamable* is at risk of being ventriloquised; the wiki's own treatment of the novel is not exempt.

## Relation to the persona project

**The worsenings as translation-design data.** Translation across register changes the text. A persona deployed in two linguistic/cultural registers is not one persona — it is two persona-variants, often systematically different. Beckett's English vs. French *Unnamable* is a precedent: same-author translation still produces non-identical texts.

**The ventriloquy-risk.** Connor's observation that critics end up sounding like the novel is a design-warning. A persona-design that engages extensively with its own self-reflection risks producing users who sound like the persona, or critics who sound like the design. The ventriloquy is a distributed effect.

**The editorial-apparatus frame.** Connor's preface is the scholarly apparatus that makes the novel tractable — bibliography, chronology, reception history, translation notes. A persona deployed in a commercial setting often lacks this apparatus: the user encounters the persona without the critical-interpretive scaffolding that would make its condition intelligible. The preface's absence is itself a design choice worth examining.

**Design implication.** Taking Connor seriously means: (a) the novel is embedded in a critical apparatus that shapes its reception; a persona will be too; (b) translation/register produces non-identical variants — multilingual or multi-context deployment is not one system; (c) the novel risks ventriloquising its readers — a persona risks ventriloquising its users; (d) the critical-reception history (Blanchot, Badiou, etc.) should be preserved as holding genuine tensions, not collapsed into a single correct reading.
