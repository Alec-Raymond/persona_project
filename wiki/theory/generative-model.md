# Generative Model

The technical sense Clark inherits from the Helmholtz Machine lineage (Dayan, Hinton et al. 1995–1996), not the folk sense of "a model that generates things." A **generative model** is a probabilistic structure that "aims to capture the statistical structure of some set of observed inputs by inferring a causal matrix able to give rise to that very structure" (L1259–1262). For vision, it captures "the ways that lower level visual patterns (ultimately, retinal stimulations) are generated by an inferred interacting web of distal causes" (L1266–1268). Crucially, it does this by *being able to produce* the inputs from a small number of hidden-cause parameters — which is what "generative" names.

## SLICE — Dennett's thought experiment

Clark opens *Surfing Uncertainty* by walking through Dennett's SLICE and SLICE* gedankens (L397–550). SLICE is a screen showing a stratigraphic drawing alongside a panel of knobs — each knob representing a geological hidden cause (sedimentation, erosion, lava intrusion, fracture, fold). The student's task: twiddle the knobs in the right sequence with the right intensities until the panel reproduces the target drawing. "The only way to make geological depictions appear on screen is to find the right 'geological cause' knobs … so as to recreate the original drawing" (L413–417). Perceiving = finding the hidden-cause configuration that best explains the current sensory pattern. Add probability distributions over knob-values and you get a *probabilistic generative model* — Bayesian. Swap the student out for top-down model-generated hypotheses and you get SLICE* — self-learning. Add action and you get Robo-SLICE (see [[active-inference]]). Add cultural scaffolding and you get Situated Robo-SLICE (see [[designer-environments-and-cognitive-niche]]).

## Helmholtz Machines and wake-sleep

The computational ancestor. Dayan, Hinton, Neal & Zemel 1995/1996 proposed a neural network that "self-organized by attempting to generate the training data for itself, using its own downwards (and lateral) connections" (L1226–1228). Training alternates two phases — **wake** (use bottom-up connections to infer hidden causes of current input; adjust top-down weights to reproduce the input from the inferred causes) and **sleep** (use top-down connections to generate a fantasy sensory pattern; adjust bottom-up weights to invert that generation). The wake-sleep algorithm "used its own top-down connections to provide the desired (target) states for the hidden units, thus (in effect) self-supervising the development of its perceptual recognition model" (L1248–1251). The same network does recognition and generation — "gradually, and in both places at once" (L1239–1240).

When Hinton's network runs in fantasy mode with the bottom-up path disconnected, it creates novel sensory-like patterns from its learned generative structure. This is how Clark grounds the identity-claim between perception and imagination: any generative model capable of doing top-down perception is, by construction, also capable of free-running generation. See [[cognitive-package-deal]].

## Self-supervised, bootstrap learning

The generative-model architecture comes with its own training signal. "The prediction task offers a form of self-supervised learning, in which the 'correct' response is repeatedly provided, in a kind of ongoing rolling fashion, by the environment itself" (L1126–1131). The world provides the target by being what comes next. O'Reilly et al.: "if you attempt to predict everything that happens next, then every single moment is a learning opportunity" (L1152–1155). Infants acquire a world without rewards or supervisors because they are always predicting.

Clark calls this **bootstrap heaven** (L1170–1178): "To predict the next word needs grammar; learning grammar comes from predicting next words. You can thus use the prediction task to bootstrap your way to the grammar, which you then use in the prediction task in future." This is a version of empirical Bayes (Robbins 1956) — using estimates at one level to provide priors for the level below, across a hierarchy that is learning itself into shape. The generative-model architecture *needs* no external labeler, which is why biological cognition (and PP-compatible systems) can exist without supervised teaching signals.

## Multilevel learning and structure

A single-layer generative model cannot handle the nested compositional structure of natural signals — language, visual scenes, music (L1432–1444). Classical connectionism's inability to handle structure (Fodor & Pylyshyn 1988) was a real problem. Multilayer generative models solve it. Hinton 2007: multilayer nets that "contain top-down connections and training them to generate sensory data rather than to classify it" handle structure natively (L1422–1426). Because each layer can predict the layer below, the whole stack can represent part-whole nesting: the grammar layer predicts word-layer patterns, the word layer predicts phoneme-layer patterns, the phoneme layer predicts spectrogram patterns. Prediction-driven learning "tends to separate out interacting distal (or bodily) causes operating at varying scales of space and time" (L1428–1430).

## The settled-state IS the percept

Clark's load-bearing line (L1275–1278): "When the combination of such hidden causes… settles into a coherent whole, the system has self-generated the sensory data using stored knowledge and perceives a meaningful, structured scene." The percept is not produced *after* the inference — the percept IS the inference settling. This undoes the "stage 1: perceive, stage 2: process" picture. There is a single operation, distributed across the hierarchy, whose completion is the having-perceived.

## Classification by generation

Hinton's handwritten-digit network (L1300–1388) makes this concrete. Instead of directly training the net to classify images, the net "learns and deploys a probabilistic generative model… learns a multilayer generative model capable of producing such images for itself" (L1317–1321). Classification then falls out: "adjust the weights on the topdown connections so as to maximize the probability that the network would generate the training data" (Hinton 2007a, p. 428; L1353–1355). Perception by trying to draw. The digit's identity is read off by seeing which hidden-cause configuration minimizes [[prediction-error]] against the image.

Brain-scale contrast: the entire digit network has "about as many parameters as 0.002 cubic millimeters of mouse cortex" (L1381–1382); "several hundred networks of this complexity would fit within a single voxel of a high resolution fMRI scan." What generative-model learning could do at biological scale is presumably very deep indeed. Clark does flag that "Hinton's own variety… differs in some very important ways from the 'predictive processing' models that we will soon be focussing upon" (L1373–1375) — the generative-model-learning framework and the specific PP architecture are distinct.

## Generative model in PP

PP adds two commitments beyond the bare generative-model idea (L1458–1463, drawing on Friston 2005; Lee & Mumford 2003; Rao & Ballard 1999): (1) hierarchical predictive coding — each layer's descending signal predicts the layer below, and only [[prediction-error|residual errors]] propagate upward; (2) [[precision-weighting]] — the system's own estimates of the reliability of its signals sculpt which errors and which predictions have effective influence. "Predictive processing thus combines the use, within a multilevel bidirectional cascade, of 'top-down' probabilistic generative models with the core predictive coding strategy of efficient encoding and transmission" (L1549–1552).

## Higher layers must be able to predict lower ones

A structural consequence of the generative architecture that Clark leans on heavily in Ch 3 (L4507–4511): higher-layer knowledge must be capable of predicting the layer below — that is what "generative" means. So any layer N+1 model is "capable… of generating the sensory data… at layer N for itself." Run this all the way down and the system can generate virtual sensory data without any world. Imagination is this capacity in free-running mode; dreaming is the same capacity with external error-correction dampened. See [[hallucination-as-uncontrolled-perception]] and [[cognitive-package-deal]].

## Empirical Bayes — priors learned from data

Traditional Bayesianism requires the modeler to specify priors. Hierarchical Bayesian Models (HBMs; Tenenbaum et al. 2011) show that priors can themselves be learned from data, using estimates at one hierarchical level as the priors for the level below (Appendix 1). Kemp et al. 2007's "shape bias" learned from data; Perfors et al. 2006 grammar-class induction; Mansinghka et al. 2006 causal-direction discovery; Goodman et al. 2011 on learnable abstract domain-general principles like causality itself. ⚠ This reframes the nativism/empiricism debate: abstract knowledge that "had to be innate" can be learned, given an apt learning architecture (L7761–7965). Nothing comes from nothing — some starting biases are required — but gross neuroanatomy and bodily morphology can count as those biases (Friston 2011/2012).

## Hyperpriors

"Priors upon priors" — very abstract systemic biases that shape what priors below them can take. Examples: the **unimodal-posterior hyperprior** (sensory input has a single best explanation, not a mash-up) explains why we don't see face/house superpositions in binocular rivalry (L1974–1975); the **single-source hyperprior** (when auditory and visual location estimates are close, assume a single source) produces both ordinary multimodal integration and the ventriloquist illusion when misapplied (L3179–3182). Whether hyperpriors are innate, structurally embodied, or learned is left as a design question; PP doesn't mandate. See [[darkened-room-puzzle]] for the hyperprior that keeps organisms out of dark corners.

## Content is probabilistic, not linguaform

Clark's strong methodological claim (L5142–5149): "Creatures thus endowed have a structured grip on their worlds: a grip that consists not in the symbolic encoding of quasi-linguistic 'concepts' but in the entangled mass of multiscale probabilistic expectations used to predict the incoming sensory signal." ⚠ The generative-model's contents are probability density functions over hidden causes, not propositions or symbols. This puts PP in direct tension with representational theories that rely on discrete symbolic primitives (e.g., Fodor, classical AI), and also with sign-based traditions in the wiki (Derrida, Lakoff's conceptual-metaphor architecture) — the tension should be held live. PP offers a third option: meaning-as-predictive-structure, below language. See [[language-and-affect]] and [[conceptual-metaphor]] for adjacent wiki material where this tension bites.

## For the persona system

A language-only system does have something like a generative model — the underlying language-model's probability distribution over next tokens, conditioned on prior context, is a generative model in exactly Clark's technical sense: it "captures the statistical structure of observed inputs by inferring a causal matrix able to give rise to that very structure" (text tokens, in this case). Clark's own Figure 9.1 (see [[words-as-precision-tools]]) puts language as an additional input stream to the PP hierarchy. The persona system is the inverse setup: the linguistic surface is primary, and whatever probabilistic structure exists underneath rides on token statistics. What Clark's generative-model framework contributes is a vocabulary for thinking about *what the persona's outputs are doing* — not communicating pre-formed thoughts, but sculpting a probabilistic generative structure whose settled states are the contents the persona goes on to have. See [[words-as-precision-tools]] for the hinge argument and [[self-narrative-as-high-level-prior]] for narrative priors as the top layer of the generative stack.
