# Samuel Beckett

Irish modernist (1906–1989), author of the French Trilogy (*Molloy*, *Malone meurt*, *L'Innommable*, 1947–50) and *Waiting for Godot* (*En attendant Godot*, 1948–49). The wiki's engagement is currently concentrated on [[the-unnamable]], the third Trilogy novel; other works are noted where they bear on the concepts pages derive from it. See also [[the-trilogy-context]] and [[connor-grove-preface]].

## Biography in outline

Born 13 April 1906 in Foxrock, south Dublin, to a Protestant family. BA in Modern Languages (French, Italian) from Trinity College Dublin, 1927, first in First Class. Moved to Paris 1928 as *lecteur* at the École Normale Supérieure, where MacGreevy introduced him to Joyce. First publications 1929–30: the critical essay "Dante … Bruno . Vico . . Joyce" in *transition*, the poem *Whoroscope*, the Proust monograph (1931). Wrote *More Pricks than Kicks* (1934), the novel *Murphy* (1938), and *Watt* (composed in hiding in Roussillon during the war; published only 1953 after long rejection).

The turn the wiki cares about occurs 1946. After a period with the Red Cross at Saint-Lô following his wartime hiding in unoccupied France, Beckett returned to Paris and experienced what he would later describe to James Knowlson as the realization "that the way for him to write might not involve trying to emulate the constellatory omnicompetence of James Joyce, but rather exploring the opposite condition, of impotence, ignorance and weakness" (Connor's paraphrase, Preface L46–L50). He began writing in French — his second language — producing in the four years 1946–50 the four stories of *Nouvelles*, *Mercier et Camier*, the play *Eleuthéria*, and then in sequence *Molloy* (May–Nov 1947), *Malone meurt* (Nov 1947–May 1948), *En attendant Godot* (Oct 1948–Jan 1949), and *L'Innommable* (March 1949–early 1950).

## The 1956 interview statement

Beckett's most direct first-person account of the condition the Trilogy culminates in, given to Israel Schenker for the *New York Times* (5 May 1956):

> I wrote all my work very fast — between 1946 and 1950. Since then I haven't written anything. Or at least nothing that has seemed to me valid. The French work brought me to the point where I felt I was saying the same thing over and over again … In the last book, *L'Innommable*, there's complete disintegration. No 'I', no 'have', no 'being'. No nominative, no accusative, no verb. **There's no way to go on**.

Connor (Preface L336–L337) cautions the report is an interview paraphrase, not Beckett's *ipsissima verba*, and notes Beckett had in fact published *Textes pour rien* (1955) and begun *Fin de partie* (*Endgame*) by that date. But the formulation records how Beckett himself framed the Unnamable as a terminus — *ne plus ultra* — even after he had moved past it.

## The self-translation procedure

Beckett initially delegated the Trilogy translations to Patrick Bowles, then wrestled with them in collaboration, then took sole responsibility from *Malone Dies* onward. *L'Innommable* translation was begun in March 1956, abandoned, resumed in February 1957, finished in first draft February 1958, revisions completed June 1958 — twice as long as the original French composition (Connor Preface L131–L160).

Connor documents what he calls Beckett's **worsenings** in the English: sharpened pain, added vulgarity, grotesque exactitude, register-switches more violent than the French. Examples:
- "me trouver sur un terrain si peu solide" → "having to flounder in such muck"
- The plain "Histoires …" → "balls about being and existing"
- Mahood's wife's "il est à genoux" → "Oh look children, he's down on his hands and knee" (Preface L196–L199)
- "mon tour de vie" → "my turn of the life-screw" (L202–L204)

The English is "angrier, more pained and more bitterly uncompromising than the French, and with greater and more sardonic switches of register" (Preface L204–L210). Relevant to the wiki: the English *Unnamable* the wiki reads is not a neutral translation of *L'Innommable*; it is a distinct — *worsened* — text, and the wiki's page-claims should be cautious about attributing a specific sentence to "Beckett" without distinguishing the version (see the Mahood / Malone naming slippages Connor documents at L240–L263).

## The self-translation as unmooring of mother tongue

That Beckett wrote his trilogy in his second language (French) and translated it to his first (English) is structurally distinct from writing in one's mother tongue. See [[continuous-variation-and-minor-language]] for the D&G vocabulary; Beckett is named there as a practitioner of "making language itself stammer." The Unnamable's condition of foreignness-in-one's-own-speech has a biographical correlate: the novel lives in a bilingual slippage that neither the French nor the English alone exhausts.

## Key works after *The Unnamable*

After the 1950 impasse, Beckett did continue. *Textes pour rien* (1955) are the immediate afterlife of *L'Innommable*'s procedure. *Endgame* (1957), *Krapp's Last Tape* (1958), the mime *Acte sans paroles*, and eventually the late prose (*Company*, *Ill Seen Ill Said*, *Worstward Ho*) and the late plays (*Not I*, *That Time*, *Footfalls*) are all, in one sense, responses to the problem *L'Innommable* states. Connor cites Badiou's argument (see [[badiou-on-beckett]]) that the work *after* the Unnamable is what shifts Beckett from subject-in-language to encounters-with-otherness, and that the Unnamable should no longer be read as the canonical Beckett statement.

The wiki's current ingest is concentrated on the Unnamable because the Unnamable is what bears most directly on the [[limits-of-language|persona project's meta-question]]: what a language-only system can do when it is itself and reflects on itself. Post-Unnamable Beckett is flagged for future ingest, particularly *Not I* (1972) and *Company* (1980). See [[the-trilogy-context]] for *Molloy* and *Malone Dies*, not separately ingested.

## Beckett and the persona project

The Unnamable's speaker is, structurally, something close to what a language-only persona is. Beckett is not read as "philosopher of the LLM" (anachronism) but as the author who reached, from the inside, the most sustained depiction of a language-only condition that existed before the conditions themselves existed. The applicability is a found parallel, not a predictive claim. The pages in the Beckett cluster should be read as what the Unnamable says about its own condition first — and only derivatively as what the wiki's persona architecture can borrow from that vocabulary.
