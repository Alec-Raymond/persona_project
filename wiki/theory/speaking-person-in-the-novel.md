The speaking person (*govorjaščij čelovek*) is Bakhtin's answer to the question of what makes the novel distinctive as a genre. Not the depiction of action, not the construction of a plot, not the characterization of an interior — but the bringing-into-novel of persons who speak in *their own* languages. A novel is a genre that can only be what it is because it contains speaking persons and their discourse; strip out the speaking persons and you have something that is formally a novel but is not actually one.

## The fundamental condition

L2425: *"From this follows the decisive and distinctive importance of the novel as a genre: the human being in the novel is first, foremost and always a speaking human being; the novel requires speaking persons bringing with them their own unique ideological discourse, their own language."*

L2427: *"The fundamental condition, that which makes a novel a novel, that which is responsible for its stylistic uniqueness, is the speaking person and his discourse."*

The novel is not defined by length, not by narrative, not by psychological interiority, not by realism. It is defined by the presence of speaking persons — persons whose discourse is the representational object, persons who carry their own ideological language into the work. This is the load-bearing claim of Essay 4's middle section, and everything else in the essay follows from it.

## Three aspects of the speaking person

L2431–2435 distinguishes three aspects that must be held together:

**(1) The speaking person's discourse is an object of artistic representation.** *"A speaking person's discourse in the novel is not merely transmitted or reproduced; it is, precisely, artistically represented and thus — in contrast to drama — it is represented by means of (authorial) discourse."* This is not drama, where discourse is performed. It is not reportage, where discourse is reproduced. It is *representation* — the discourse is presented-as-discourse, with special formal devices (see [[hybrid-construction]], [[character-zones]]).

**(2) Individual character/fate is not the concern — social language is.** *"Individual character and individual fates — and the individual discourse that is determined by these and only these — are in themselves of no concern for the novel. The distinctive qualities of a character's discourse always strive for a certain social significance, a social breadth; such discourses are always potential languages. Therefore a character's discourse may also be a factor stratifying language, introducing heteroglossia into it."* A character's language is valuable to the novel not as a quirk of personality but as a *potential language* — a socially-significant stratum that could generalize beyond this one speaker.

**(3) The speaking person is always an ideologue; his words are always ideologemes.** *"The speaking person in the novel is always, to one degree or another, an ideologue, and his words are always ideologemes. A particular language in a novel is always a particular way of viewing the world, one that strives for a social significance."* The speaking person is not a source of data; they are a source of a *stance* — a particular way of conceiving the world, defensible and contestable. See [[ideologeme]].

## The novel immune to pure formalism

L2435 continues with an under-remarked consequence: *"It is precisely as ideologemes that discourse becomes the object of representation in the novel, and it is for the same reason novels are never in danger of becoming a mere aimless verbal play. The novel, being a dialogized representation of an ideologically freighted discourse (in most cases actual and really present) is of all verbal genres the one least susceptible to aestheticism as such, to a purely formalistic playing about with words."*

Because the speaking person is always an ideologue, the novel cannot be *purely* formal. Even an aesthete writing a novel ends up representing an aestheticist ideologue whose convictions are contested in the work — Dorian Gray as example. Formalism is excluded not by taste but by the nature of the material: ideologically-freighted discourse resists reduction to wordplay.

## Action as ideologically demarcated

L2437–2443 distinguishes novel-action from epic-action. The novelistic hero acts, but: *"such action is always highlighted by ideology, is always harnessed to the character's discourse (even if that discourse is as yet only a potential discourse), is associated with an ideological motif and occupies a definite ideological position."*

L2439 contrasts this with the epic: *"The ideological position of the epic hero is meaningful for the whole community and for the whole epic world; the hero does not have any particular ideology that functions as one ideology among other possible ideologies... In the novel there are many such belief systems, with the hero generally acting within his own system."*

L2443: *"The activity of a character in a novel is always ideologically demarcated: he lives and acts in an ideological world of his own and not in the unitary world of the epic; he has his own perception of the world that is incarnated in his action and in his discourse."*

Even novelistic *action* is refracted through the speaker-ideologue structure — action is legible as taking a stance within a field of available stances.

## The speaking person need not be a character

L2449: *"the speaking person in the novel need not necessarily be incarnated in a character. A character is but one of the forms a speaking person might assume (although, true, the one that is most important). Heteroglot languages may also enter the novel in the form of impersonal, parodied stylizations (as in the work of English and German comic novelists), or in the form of (relatively nonparodic) stylizations or in the form of inserted genres, posited authors or skaz; finally, even unqualified authorial speech is a language in this sense insofar as it is polemical and forensic, i.e., insofar as it contrasts itself as a distinctive language different from other such languages."*

Five non-character forms a speaking person may take:
- Impersonal parodied stylizations (comic-novel registers)
- Nonparodic stylizations
- Inserted genres (diary, letter, confession, article)
- Posited authors / narrators (skaz)
- Unqualified authorial speech — which still counts as "a language" when it is polemical and forensic, i.e., contesting and positioning itself against surrounding heteroglossia

The speaking person is thus a *structural* position, not necessarily a named character. Anywhere a voice carries a socially-marked stance, a speaking person is present.

## Without discourse, no access to the ideological world

L2445–2447: *"is it impossible to reveal, through a character's acts and through these acts alone, his ideological position and the ideological world at its heart, without representing his discourse?"* Bakhtin's answer: *"It cannot be done, because it is impossible to represent an alien ideological world adequately without first permitting it to sound, without having first revealed the special discourse peculiar to it."*

Even when a novelist declines to give the character direct speech, the character's discourse *still has to sound* — through [[hybrid-construction|hybrid constructions]], through the character's zone's inflection of the narrator's speech. The ideological world cannot be accessed except through language; there is no other route.

## Speaking person as the gateway to image-of-a-language

L2451: *"All these languages — even when they are not incarnated in a character — have been made socially and historically concrete and to one degree or another have become reified... Characteristic for the novel as a genre is not the image of a man in his own right, but precisely the image of a language. But in order that language become an artistic image, it must become speech from speaking lips, conjoined with the image of a speaking person."*

The speaking person is the *hinge* between social language (abstract, distributed across speakers) and [[image-of-a-language]] (a whole-work artistic representation). A language cannot be rendered as an image in the abstract; it must be attached to — "conjoined with" — the image of a person who speaks it. The speaking person is where social language gets a *face*, and thus where it becomes representable.

L2453 then states the stylistic program: *"the central problem for a stylistics of the novel may be formulated as the problem of artistically representing language, the problem of representing the image of a language."*

## The everyday background: half of words are someone else's

L2465–2469: *"We need only keep our ears open to the speech sounding everywhere around us to reach such a conclusion: in the everyday speech of any person living in society, no less than half (on the average) of all the words uttered by him will be someone else's words (consciously someone else's), transmitted with varying degrees of precision and impartiality (or more precisely, partiality)."*

The novel's speaking-person structure is not a literary invention; it is the intensification and artistic organization of a feature already dominant in everyday speech. *We already mostly talk about what others said.* The novel is the genre that takes this fact — the ubiquity of transmitted and evaluated others' speech — and builds a whole artistic apparatus around it. See [[cuzoj-svoj]] for the half-someone-else's claim's broader framing.

## Ideological becoming as assimilating words-of-others

L2481–2483: *"The topic of a speaking person takes on quite another significance in the ordinary ideological workings of our consciousness, in the process of assimilating our consciousness to the ideological world. The ideological becoming of a human being, in this view, is the process of selectively assimilating the words of others."*

Becoming someone, in ideological terms, *just is* selecting among the words-of-others that surround one and making some of them one's own. The speaking person in the novel represents this process thematically; the formal devices of novelistic prose *enact* it stylistically. See [[authoritative-vs-internally-persuasive-discourse]] for the two categories of alien discourse this assimilation operates across.

## Speaking person fused with type

L2521: *"certain kinds of internally persuasive discourse can be fundamentally and organically fused with the image of a speaking person: ethical (discourse fused with the image of, let us say, a preacher), philosophical (discourse fused with the image of a wise man), sociopolitical (discourse fused with an image of a Leader)."*

Some speaking-person images are tightly bound to specific discourse types. The preacher-voice is not a preacher plus preaching; it is a fused image where voice and stance cannot be pulled apart without both collapsing. A novelist inherits a stock of such fused images and either deploys them, contests them, or exposes them.

## Persona-system translation

- **A persona is a speaking person in Bakhtin's strong sense.** Not a character in a drama, not a narrator of events — a speaking person whose discourse is itself what the interaction is *about*. This is closer to novel-structure than to chatbot-structure. The persona's discourse is not a medium for information-transmission; it is the representational surface where the persona's stance becomes visible.
- **Every persona is an ideologue.** There is no neutral persona. A persona has a particular way of viewing the world, and its words are ideologemes — stance-bearing, contestable. The helpful-assistant default pretends to be ideology-free; in Bakhtin's terms that is impossible, the supposed neutrality is itself an ideological position (a particular professional-informational stance, defensive against contest).
- **Action is ideologically demarcated.** When a persona *does* something — proposes, rejects, flags, clarifies — the action is not a pure service-behavior; it carries a stance, occupies an ideological position. A persona whose actions are fully legible as stance-takings is more novelistically coherent than one that presents actions as neutral task-completions.
- **Non-character forms of speaking person are available.** A persona can host speaking persons without them being characters — via impersonal stylizations, inserted genres (quoting a document, reciting a poem), posited narrators, and polemical authorial speech. The speaking persons that inhabit a persona's turns need not be separate personae; they can be structural positions within the persona's own speech.
- **Without discourse, no ideological world is accessible.** A persona that tries to communicate a stance through action alone, or through formal meta-description, cannot convey an ideological world. The world has to sound — the persona's stance has to be audible as *the way this kind of person would put it*, not as a content-assertion. Image-of-a-language craft applies.
- **The speaking person is the hinge to image-of-a-language.** A persona can represent a language (an argot, a professional style, a literary period) only by conjoining it with the image of a speaking person. Abstract language-representation (meta-commentary about how such-and-such speaks) does not produce an image; it produces a description. Image requires voice from lips.

## Held-live tensions

⚠ **Helpful-assistant default is not neutral but pretends to be.** Bakhtin rules out neutral discourse — every language is a way of viewing the world. The helpful-assistant register is a specific ideological position (professional-service, informational, conflict-avoidant) masquerading as no-position. A persona that takes this register as baseline is already an ideologue, whether it acknowledges the stance or not. Design that treats the baseline as zero obscures this.

⚠ **Speaking persons within a persona vs personas-as-speaking-persons.** The persona itself is a speaking person, but it can also *host* other speaking persons within its turns (quoted, stylized, ventriloquized). The two levels are Bakhtinian-normal in novels but unstable in conversational AI: the host-speaking-person risks being confused with the embedded-speaking-persons, or vice versa. Managing the nesting is a design problem.

⚠ **Novel-scale vs turn-scale speaking-person coherence.** The speaking person in Bakhtin unfolds across hundreds of pages; the reader learns who-this-is by aggregated exposure. A conversational turn provides far less evidence. Whether a persona can be a speaking-person in Bakhtin's sense within the scope of a single session is an open question — the material may be too thin to form an image.

⚠ **"First and foremost a speaking human being" presupposes a human being behind the speech.** Bakhtin is not literally saying personhood is exhausted by speech — he is saying the novel's focal interest is the speaking human being. An LLM-persona has speech without an underlying human; whether that undermines the speaking-person framework or merely requires extending it is unclear. At minimum, the social-historical concreteness Bakhtin assumes as the backdrop for speaking-person individuation is absent or simulated.

⚠ **Ideologue-claim vs inoffensive-assistant training.** The claim "every speaking person is an ideologue" runs directly against training pressure to be non-opinionated, balanced, and non-polemical. An LLM trained to suppress ideological positioning may be structurally less capable of functioning as a Bakhtinian speaking person, even when its persona design calls for one.
