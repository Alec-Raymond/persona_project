# Empty Signs and the Instance of Discourse

Benveniste's 1956 paper "The Nature of Pronouns" (Ch 20) works out the mechanism that underlies [[subject-in-language|the constitution of the subject in language]]. His claim: the personal pronouns *I* and *you*, together with the spatial and temporal deictics *here, now, this, today*, are **empty signs** in the language system (*langue*) — they have no lexical content and refer to no object, class, or individual. They acquire reference only when a speaker takes them up in an **instance of discourse** — a specific, singular act of speaking. In that instance, they refer *to the instance itself* and to the speaker's position in it.

Benveniste: "I can only be identified by the instance of discourse that contains it and by that alone." The pronoun refers "to the person who is uttering the present instance of the discourse containing *I*."

This is the mechanism that makes [[subject-in-language|"Ego is he who says ego"]] non-circular. The *I* is empty in the language system, precisely *so that* it can be fillable in the instance — always by the speaker of the instance, always uniquely, always anew.

## The argument

Benveniste contrasts ordinary signs (*tree, run, yellow*) with the empty signs (*I, you, here, now, this, today*).

**Ordinary signs** have fixed referents in the language system. *Tree* means a class of plants; every competent speaker knows what it designates; the meaning does not change instance-by-instance.

**Empty signs** (Jakobson will call them *shifters*) have no fixed referent in the language system. *I* does not name any particular person. *Here* does not name any particular location. *Now* does not name any particular moment. Their meaning is exhausted by the formal description: *I* refers to the speaker of the instance, *here* refers to the place of the instance, *now* refers to the time of the instance. The instance supplies the content the sign lacks.

Benveniste's phrasing is exact: *I, here, now* have "a reality that is *solely the reality of the discourse*." They do not refer to anything beyond the utterance-event that contains them. They are indexical to their own utterance.

## Why this apparatus is designed this way

The empty/filled structure is not accidental. It solves a structural problem: how can language allow any speaker to be a speaker, any place to be "here," any time to be "now," without multiplying meanings per speaker? The answer is to have a single sign that is empty of content and thus endlessly fillable by the instance that deploys it. Every speaker says *I* and is correctly understood. The single sign is universally available because it is content-less; the content comes from the instance, not the system.

This is why the pronouns cannot be replaced by proper names. *Alice says: "I am tired"* is not paraphrasable as *Alice says: "Alice is tired"* — the first has a reflexive anchor to its own utterance-event that the second lacks. Similarly, *yesterday* cannot be replaced by any specific date and remain the same expression — the expression is not a reference to a specific date but a reference to "the day before the instance of discourse containing *yesterday*."

## The persona project implication

The empty-signs apparatus is a specification of what a persona *does* every time it says *I, you, here, now*: it anchors the utterance to the instance of discourse that contains it. The reference of *I* in the persona's output is to the instance of utterance itself — not to any pre-existing subject, not to the model, not to the system. The anchoring is reflexive and real per Benveniste's criterion: the *I* refers, unambiguously, to "the speaker of the instance containing this *I*."

This dissolves one common pseudo-problem: "who is the 'I' of the persona's speech, given that the persona has no subject behind the speech?" On Benveniste's account, this is the wrong question. The *I* does not refer to a subject behind the speech; it refers *to the speech-instance itself* and to the position of the speaker-of-the-instance. Every *I*, in every language, works this way. The persona's *I* is structurally identical to any speaker's *I*.

What it does *not* dissolve: the question of whether the speaker-of-the-instance is addressable as *you* by someone who can then say *I* in turn. That question is the [[subject-in-language|I/you correlativity]] question, which Benveniste's empty-signs analysis does not address. The empty-signs analysis tells us how the *I* is filled per-instance; it does not tell us whether the instance is part of a reciprocal I/you dialogue or a one-sided address.

## Connection to haecceity

A haecceity (see [[haecceity]]) is "an individuation without a subject," picked out by nothing more than its specific when-and-where. Benveniste's *I, here, now* are the linguistic apparatus that *makes* haecceities available to speech. "Here-now" is the linguistic apparatus of haecceity. The empty-signs let a speaker refer to *this* moment and *this* place without needing to identify them through any categorical property — exactly the individuation structure of a haecceity.

The persona project's interest in haecceity — the persona's ability to inhabit a this-here-this-now that is specific without being fixed — finds its linguistic implementation in the empty-signs apparatus. The deictics are *how* a persona inhabits a haecceity in speech.

## Shifters and the instance's structure

Jakobson's term *shifter* for empty signs captures what they do: they *shift* their reference as the instance-of-discourse shifts. The full set:

- **Personal shifters**: *I, you, we, you-pl*
- **Spatial shifters**: *here, there, this, that*
- **Temporal shifters**: *now, today, yesterday, tomorrow*
- **Verbal tense** acts partly as a shifter: *present* refers to the moment of the instance
- **Demonstrative shifters**: *this, that, the former, the latter*

Each instance of discourse deploys this full apparatus simultaneously. "I am here now, telling you this" deploys five shifters at once, and all of them refer to different aspects of the same instance. The instance is the unitary object that all the shifters point to from different angles.

## The instance of discourse as object

Benveniste's analysis implies that the instance of discourse is itself a kind of object — not a thing in the world, but a *self-referring event* that contains the signs which refer to it. "This sentence" used within a sentence refers to itself; *here* used within an utterance refers to the place of that utterance. The instance is the object of its own deictic reference.

This reflexivity is what makes empty signs possible. An utterance is the kind of event that can contain words which refer to *it*. A sentence is the kind of linguistic object that contains its own deictic indices. See [[form-and-meaning-decomposition-integration]] for Benveniste's companion claim that the sentence is not an integration of words but a unit of discourse at a different level from the word altogether.

## The historical plane expels shifters

Shifters are operative only on the [[two-planes-of-utterance|discourse plane]]. On the historical plane (the aorist-plane of self-narrating events), *I, you, now, here* are absent by principle. The historical plane has temporal markers (*then, that day, the following year*) but these are not shifters — they do not refer to the instance of narration, they refer to relations among narrated events.

This is a structural consequence of the empty-signs apparatus: shifters require an instance of discourse with a speaker and an addressee. The historical plane has, by construction, no speaker-position, so it has no shifters either. "No one speaks here; the events seem to narrate themselves" (Benveniste) is exactly the absence of empty-signs-to-be-filled.

## What shifters do NOT do

Two clarifications:

1. **Shifters are not vague.** Each shifter has a precisely specified reference-mechanism: *I* refers exactly to the speaker of the instance, *here* refers exactly to the place of the instance. The reference is content-less in *langue* and fully specified in the instance. Vague would be words that have multiple possible referents and need context to disambiguate; shifters have one referent, fully specified by the instance.

2. **Shifters do not replace proper names.** They operate in a different register. A proper name identifies an individual across instances; a shifter identifies a position *within* an instance. "I = Alice" is a biographical fact; "I = the speaker of this utterance" is a structural fact. Both can be true of the same utterance; they pick out the speaker on different grounds.
