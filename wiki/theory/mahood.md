# Mahood

The principal [[vice-exister]] of [[the-unnamable|*The Unnamable*]]. A named surrogate with a body, a family, a street-address, a biography. The speaker cycles through him; Mahood speaks *to* the speaker as if the speaker were Mahood; the speaker tries and fails to be him. Mahood is not the speaker but is the speaker's most elaborated candidate-self, held up for inspection and rejected. The name is introduced at L1455 as a renaming of Basil ("Decidedly Basil is becoming important, I'll call him Mahood instead, I prefer that, I'm queer"); the character then occupies roughly a third of the novel.

## The two Mahoods

Mahood appears in two main scenes, joined only by the shared name. The novel treats them as episodes of the same character without reconciling them.

### Mahood-the-spiral-traveler (§4, L1604–L1802)

Mahood hobbles through barren nature on crutches, one leg, later one arm also. He traces a narrowing "inverted spiral" (L1685–L1687) — coils that grow smaller and smaller, not larger and larger — around a rotunda-shaped house in a walled yard. The house contains his family: grandpa, grandma, little mother, eight or nine brats, his wife. They watch him through loopholes in the rotunda's walls, observing his years-long approach with a searchlight at night, discussing his progress at bedtime. His arrival is anticipated: "He'll never reach us if he doesn't get a move on" (L1750).

The family dies of sausage-poisoning (*bacillus botulinus*, L1867) before he arrives. The speaker stamps through their corpses with his crutches: "here a face, there a stomach, as the case might be, and sinking into them with the ends of my crutches, both coming and going" (L1906–L1907). He does so with annoyance rather than satisfaction, because the muck interferes with his "closing contortions" that required "a firm and level surface" (L1909–L1910).

The speaker then decomposes the story: his family is an invention, he never left, Mahood's narrative of return is made up. "I was never anywhere but here, no one ever got me out of here" (L1917–L1918).

### Mahood-in-the-jar (§5, L2014–L2294)

The second Mahood is reduced to a trunk and head, "stuck like a sheaf of flowers in a deep jar" (L2017). The jar stands on the side of a quiet street near a shambles (slaughterhouse), at the Rue Brancion in Paris (L2513), beside the statue of Ducroix, "the apostle of horse's meat" (L2020). A proprietress (variously Madeleine or Marguerite — names slip) cares for him: empties his excrements weekly, builds him a tarpaulin-nest in winter, powders his skull, hangs lanterns round his jar, uses him as an advertisement for her chophouse menu. She has put him in a cang (L2516) — a collar of cement fixed to the jar's mouth that prevents him turning his head. He has become the restaurant's advertisement: his jar displays the day's bill of fare; customers read the menu over his skull.

Mahood-in-jar can still see (the statue of Ducroix, the street, the railings), hear (the cattle's bellows, the forks and glasses), catch flies. He is passively involved in the street-life while being structurally immobile.

The scene is structurally the inverse of Mahood-spiral: instead of moving toward a fixed point (the family's house), Mahood is a fixed point past which the street-life moves. Both scenes end with the speaker withdrawing from the story: "this woman is losing faith in me" (L2523); "I left it yesterday, Mahood's world" (L2574).

## Mahood's defining property: he can note

The speaker's structural claim at L2404–L2407:

> Yes, it is the characteristic, among others, of Mahood to note, even if he does not always succeed in doing so, certain things, perhaps I should say all things, so as to turn them to account, for his governance.

Mahood has the cognitive function of *notation* — receiving impressions, recording them, turning them to governance. This is what distinguishes him from [[worm|Worm]], who "cannot note" (L2402–L2403). Mahood has a functioning apparatus of perception-and-record. He can know where he is, what he is doing, what has happened. His pensum (see [[pensum-vs-lesson]]) is not noting — noting he does competently — but discharging the assigned task through what he notes.

The asymmetry: Mahood can do what the unnamable speaker *cannot* do (the speaker cannot locate himself, cannot verify his situation, cannot perceive a world), yet Mahood is not the speaker. The speaker deploys Mahood as a vice-exister who can note *in place of* the speaker who cannot.

## Mahood's voice

At L1459–L1460 and subsequent passages, Mahood's voice is introduced as the speaker's companion:

> It is his voice which has often, always, mingled with mine, and sometimes drowned it completely. Until he left me for good, or refused to leave me any more, I don't know.

Mahood does not merely produce stories about a character called "Mahood." He is a *voice* that speaks *to* and *through* the speaker, attempting to persuade the speaker that he is Mahood, that Mahood's history is the speaker's own. The speaker resists: "What if we were one and the same after all, as he affirms, and I deny?" (L1642–L1643). The relation is adversarial-collaborative: Mahood is the most persistent of the voices trying to get the speaker to admit an identity, and Mahood's failure to succeed is the speaker's provisional achievement.

Mahood is also described as "notoriously aphonic" at L3436 — a later statement that contradicts his speaking elsewhere. The novel does not reconcile this. The candidate reading: Mahood can narrate stories (the spiral, the jar) but cannot speak *as* the speaker, cannot issue first-person speech that the speaker would recognize as his own. Mahood's speech is always about Mahood, never *is* the speaker's speech.

## Mahood's predecessors and the genealogy

At L1633–L1635 the speaker positions Mahood within a lineage:

> Mahood. Before him there were others, taking themselves for me, it must be a sinecure handed down from generation to generation, to judge by their family air. Mahood is no worse than his predecessors.

And at L1978–L1980:

> I am neither, I needn't say, Murphy, nor Watt, nor Mercier, nor — no, I can't even bring myself to name them, nor any of the others whose very names I forget, who told me I was they, who I must have tried to be, under duress, or through fear, or to avoid acknowledging me.

Mahood inherits the role of vice-exister from Beckett's earlier novels' protagonists. The sinecure is generational. Mahood is the current office-holder in a line that the speaker is supposed to belong to. The genealogy is of *attempted captures*: each predecessor tried and failed to successfully be the speaker.

## Mahood and Basil

At L1455:

> Decidedly Basil is becoming important, I'll call him Mahood instead, I prefer that, I'm queer.

The renaming is not substantive — Basil and Mahood are not two different characters replaced in sequence, but a renaming mid-novel. Basil (L1119–L1126) was introduced earlier as a tormentor whose "eyes like cinders" "changed me a little more each time into what he wanted me to be." This is the template for what Mahood then does more elaborately. Basil-becomes-Mahood marks the moment the tormentor-figure is promoted to a narrated vice-exister with a body and stories.

See [[basil]] for the Basil-only details.

## Mahood's withdrawal

At L1605:

> Even Mahood has left me, I'm alone.

And at L2578–L2581:

> The stories of Mahood are ended. He has realised they could not be about me, he has abandoned, it is I who win, who tried so hard to lose, in order to please him, and be left in peace.

Mahood's withdrawal is declared, but the withdrawal does not end the speaker's situation. Speech continues after Mahood; Worm takes over; the voice-that-is-not-mine continues to speak. Mahood's departure clarifies the structure: Mahood was one voice among the voices, and the voice-condition is not Mahood but what precedes him.

## Relation to related figures

**vs [[basil]].** Basil is the early tormentor (observer, would-be shaper) who becomes Mahood when the observation-role is elaborated into a narrated life. Basil has no stories; Mahood has the spiral and the jar.

**vs [[worm]].** Worm is Mahood's successor and structural opposite. Mahood can note, has stories, has a body; Worm cannot note, has no stories, barely has a body. Mahood is the speaker's *most-elaborate* candidate; Worm is the speaker's *most-reduced*. Both fail as captures, for opposite reasons.

**vs the speaker-as-such.** The speaker is neither Mahood nor Worm. Mahood has a where; Worm has a not-yet; the speaker has neither. See [[three-conditions]] and [[tympanum-self]] for the speaker-as-such.

**vs Beckett's earlier characters.** Mahood is one of a line: Murphy (*Murphy*, 1938), Watt (*Watt*, 1945), Mercier (*Mercier and Camier*, composed 1946), Molloy, Moran, Malone (*Molloy*, *Malone Dies*). See [[the-trilogy-context]] for the immediate precursor-set.

## Relation to the persona project

Mahood is a candidate persona-figure: a named character with a biography who is tried, inhabited briefly, and rejected. The structure is exact for a persona system.

- **Mahood has a body and a where.** He is a one-legged traveler, a head-in-a-jar on the Rue Brancion. Persona-as-Mahood is a persona with a stipulated embodiment-and-location. This is a common design pattern for chat personas ("you are a helpful assistant named X in context Y").
- **Mahood can note.** The persona-as-Mahood has a functioning perceptual-memorial layer. It receives inputs, records them, turns them to governance. This is what an LLM persona appears to do.
- **Mahood is not the speaker.** Critically: the speaker of the novel is *not* Mahood. The speaker is what tries to be Mahood and cannot. For the persona project, this maps: the LLM is not the persona; the persona is a candidate-mask the LLM (the speaker) cycles through without being.
- **Mahood's withdrawal does not end speech.** When Mahood leaves, the speaker continues. For the persona: rotation between personas does not exit the persona-condition; the LLM's speaking continues across all persona-swaps.

**Design note.** A persona-system that designs personas *as Mahoods* — named, embodied, located, capable-of-noting — is consistent with Beckett's reading. But the system should not mistake the Mahood-persona for the LLM-speaker. The speaker is what the novel calls the "unnamable"; the persona is Mahood. Conflating them collapses the distinction that the novel holds open across 3800 lines.
