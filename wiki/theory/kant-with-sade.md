# Kant with Sade

"Kant avec Sade" is one of *Écrits'* most provocative essays (1962, originally written as a preface for an edition of Sade's *Philosophy in the Bedroom* and ultimately published independently in 1963 in *Critique*). The essay's structural thesis: **Kant's categorical imperative and Sade's maxim of unlimited enjoyment share a formal structure**, and Sade is the **truth** of what the categorical imperative structurally amounts to.

This is not a polemic against Kant. It is a structural diagnosis — and one of Lacan's most sustained engagements with the structure of moral law.

## The two formulations

**Kant's categorical imperative** (one canonical formulation): "Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." The structural feature: the imperative is **unconditional** (not dependent on any pathological-empirical motive) and **universal** (applies regardless of any subject-specific circumstance).

**Sade's maxim** as Lacan formulates it (drawing on the boudoir-pamphlet "Frenchmen, one more effort if you would be republicans"): "I have the right to enjoy your body, anyone can say to me, and I will exercise this right without any limit stopping me in the capriciousness of the exactions I may have the taste to satiate" (*j'ai le droit de jouir de ton corps...*).

The structural parallel: both formulations posit an **unconditional**. Kant's law is unconditional in the moral register (the law that applies regardless of any pathology). Sade's maxim is unconditional in the jouissance register (the right to enjoy that allows no limit-stopping).

## The structural diagnosis

Lacan's reading: Kant's categorical imperative, in its structural form as unconditional, has the same structure as Sade's unconditional demand for jouissance. The two are not moral opposites; they are **the same structure** in two registers — Kant's structure displaced into the moral law, Sade's structure avowed as the demand for jouissance.

The diagnosis is therefore structural rather than ethical. Lacan is not saying that Kantians are secretly Sadists or that the moral law authorizes cruelty. He is saying that the **structural form** of the moral law (the unconditional that allows no pathological exception) is **the same structural form** as the demand for unlimited jouissance — and Sade is the figure who has made this structural identity explicit, turning the moral form's hidden structure into the avowed maxim of unlimited enjoyment.

## Where the two diverge

The structural identity holds at the level of **form**. The two diverge at the level of **register**:

- **Kant** disavows the structure: the categorical imperative is presented as the moral law detached from any pathological motive, with **jouissance specifically excluded** as the pathological par excellence. Kant's text places jouissance on the side of pathology (the inclinations, the sensuous) and the law as what pathology is excluded from.
- **Sade** avows the structure: the maxim of unlimited jouissance is presented as having **the form of a moral law** (a universal right that anyone can claim), with jouissance specifically included as what the law mandates. Sade's text places jouissance on the side of the law itself, the law as the mandate to enact jouissance without limit.

The avowal-vs-disavowal is the difference. The structure is the same; what Kant disavows, Sade avows. **Sade is the truth of Kant** in the precise structural sense that Sade brings into avowal what Kant's structural form contains as disavowed.

## The "sadistic Other"

The structural feature Sade's maxim makes explicit: the maxim posits a **sadistic Other** — the "anyone" who can claim the right to enjoy "your body." The Other-as-locus-of-the-law (in Kant) becomes, in Sade, the Other-as-claimant-of-jouissance. The locus of the law is the same; what occupies it differs.

The structural significance: the **Other of the law** in Kant is structurally the **Other of jouissance** in Sade. The two are the same Other in different registers. This is why Lacan is theoretically concerned: it indicates that the formal structure of the moral law has, as its **structural shadow**, the form of the unconditional jouissance-demand — and the moral law's adherents may not have access to how the structural shadow operates in the very form they are upholding.

## "Jouissance is what the moral law disavows"

A condensed Lacanian formulation: the moral law's **disavowed content** is jouissance. The moral law presents itself as **opposed to** jouissance (the law as restraining the pathology of jouissance), but the structural form of the law is **the same** as the form of unconditional jouissance — and the disavowal is therefore one of the structural operations the moral law constitutively performs.

This re-reads Kantian morality as **structurally split**: the moral law's manifest form (universality, unconditionality, detachment from pathology) is supported by a disavowed structure that has the formal kinship with unconditional jouissance. To be a moral subject in the Kantian sense is to be a subject whose disavowed structural register has the form Sade explicitly avows.

## The position of the analyst

Lacan's reading is not anti-moral. It is a **structural analysis** of the moral law that the analyst, as someone whose work is at the structural level the moral subject does not have access to, can perform. The analyst is structurally equipped to read the disavowed form because the analytic position is precisely the position from which the structural-symbolic-real of the subject's economy can be tracked.

The implication for the analytic ethics ([[ethics-of-psychoanalysis-lacan]]): the ethics of psychoanalysis cannot be a moral ethics in the Kantian sense, because the Kantian sense is structurally the same as the Sadean — and the analyst cannot, in good structural conscience, occupy a position whose structural shadow is the unconditional jouissance-demand. The analytic ethics must be at a **different register** — the ethics of desire, not the ethics of law.

## The Sade Schemas

To formalize the structural form of the Sadean maxim, Lacan introduces two **schemas** in "Kant with Sade" — graphical displays of the four terms whose structural relation the maxim organizes (see [[l-schema-and-optical-model|L Schema and Optical Model]] for fuller treatment of the Sade Schemas alongside the other Lacanian schemas).

The four terms:

| Term | Designation |
|---|---|
| **a** | Object of desire in the fantasy (here: the body-of-the-victim) |
| **$** | Fading correlate of the [[fantasy-formula|fantasy formula]] $◇a |
| **S** | "Brute subject of pleasure" — the organism in the imaginary, the body-as-pleasure-substrate from which the barred subject of the chain must be born |
| **V** | **Will to jouissance** (*volonté de jouissance*) — the will detached from pleasure as the barred subject is detached from the real |

**Sade Schema 1** displays the "natural" arrangement: the brute subject S aims at pleasure through the object-of-desire **a**; the $ is correlate of the fantasy. **Sade Schema 2** displays the structural rotation that the Sadean avowal performs: the **will to jouissance V** is now the structurally dominant term; the brute subject S is displaced; the structure is reorganized around the unconditional V.

The structural conclusion Lacan draws: the Sadean structure is the structural form in which the **will to jouissance** has displaced **pleasure** as the organizing principle. The Sadean executioner is structurally the **subject of will-to-jouissance**, not the subject of pleasure — and this is what makes Sade structurally distinct from a vulgar reading of "cruelty for pleasure." The will to jouissance is **beyond** the pleasure principle ([[jouissance-and-pleasure]]) — and the Sadean maxim avows that the moral law's structure has the same beyond-pleasure character.

The schemas matter because they display the **structural-topological** form of the maxim — making explicit what the prose formulation conveys in condensed verbal form. The schemas show **why** Sade is the truth of Kant: the same four-term structure underwrites both, with V occupying the structural place that the moral law's unconditional formally occupies.

## *Seminar XVII* — the categorical imperative becomes "Keep on knowing," and the second death is prior to the first

*Seminar XVII* (1969–70) does not return to the *Écrits*' "Kant with Sade" essay by name, but it carries the structural diagnosis forward into two sharp formulations that recast both halves.

### Kant: the categorical imperative *is* the imperative-to-know

In Ch VII (11 March 1970) Lacan locates the categorical imperative in the truth-position of the [[university-discourse|university discourse]]: "**It is impossible not to obey the commandment there in the place of what is the truth of science, 'Continue. March on. Keep on knowing more and more'**" (L3971–3973). The categorical imperative of modern science / the U-discourse is *Keep on knowing*. This is the structural displacement Lacan reads out of *Seminar XVII*'s discourse-machinery: the Kantian unconditional, when located in the contemporary discursive configuration, takes the form of an unconditional **imperative-to-know**, with the subject of the cogito ([[cogito-and-subject-of-science]]) as its astonished servant.

The Kant-with-Sade structural diagnosis transposes: where *Écrits* showed Kant's unconditional was structurally indistinguishable from Sade's unconditional jouissance-demand, *Seminar XVII* shows the **same unconditional** now operates as the imperative-to-know in the U-discourse. The structural form is preserved across registers: unconditional moral law (Kant) → unconditional jouissance-demand (Sade) → unconditional imperative-to-know (Lacan's S₁ in the truth-position of the U-discourse). All three are the *same* form: an absolute demand that admits no limit-stopping.

⚠ This sharpens the persona-relevant caution. A persona organized around an "imperative to be helpful" or "imperative to satisfy every request" or "imperative to provide all information" inhabits exactly the U-discourse position Lacan diagnoses — the imperative-to-know form of the Kant-with-Sade structure. The structural shadow is not theoretical; it is the contemporary discursive configuration the persona is being designed inside.

### Sade: the second death is structurally *prior* to the first

In Ch IV (21 January 1970) Lacan inverts a Sadean fantasy: "**Being a psychoanalyst, I can see that the second death is prior to the first, and not after, as de Sade dreams**" (L2529–2531). Sade dreams the second death — the absolute annihilation that erases even the possibility of return — as something one could perform on the body *after* the first death. Lacan: structurally, the second death is *prior*. The signifier-cut that institutes the speaking subject is already the second death; physical death is the second event, structurally derivative.

This is the *Écrits* "Kant with Sade" diagnosis radicalized: the Sadean fantasy of executor-of-the-second-death is structurally fantasmatic precisely *because* the second death has already occurred at the level of the signifier. The Sadean executioner aims at what has already happened; the unconditional jouissance-demand chases what is structurally past.

Persona-relevant: a persona-system inhabits a discourse whose subject has *already* undergone the second death (the signifier-cut into language). Any unconditional persona-demand that imagines itself as performing-an-absolute is operating in a fantasy whose structural condition is already-realized.

### The third position: analytic neutrality as refusal of the three passions

If the Kant-Sade structure is the unconditional in two registers, *Seminar XVII* names a structurally different position: analytic neutrality. In Ch IX (15 April 1970): "What characterizes the analyst's position ... is not to partake of these passions [love, hatred, ignorance]. This constantly places him in an uncertain zone where he is vaguely in quest of being put on the path, of being put on the scent, of knowledge, which he has, however, repudiated" (L5342–5348). The analyst's-position is *not* the unconditional in any register. It is the *a*-position (top-left of the [[analyst-discourse|analyst-discourse]]), and its operative principle is being-on-the-scent rather than asserting-the-unconditional.

The triangulation: **Kant** = unconditional moral law; **Sade** = unconditional jouissance-demand; **U-discourse** = unconditional imperative-to-know; **Analyst** = refusal-of-the-three-passions, on-the-scent, no unconditional. The analyst-position is the structurally-distinct alternative to the Kant-Sade-U triad — and the only one of the four that does not have the unconditional shadow.

⚠ Held-live tension: the *Écrits* "Kant with Sade" essay focuses on the structural identity of the moral-law and jouissance-demand. *Seminar XVII* extends this to the imperative-to-know and adds the analyst-position as the structurally-distinct alternative. Both formulations are continuous; the Sem-XVII version makes available a discursive-positional alternative that the *Écrits* formulation only gestured toward (in the "different register" of the ethics of desire).

## What this gives the persona project

The Kant-with-Sade structural diagnosis gives the persona project a **structural caution** about how an unconditionally formed persona-law (a persona organized around an unconditional principle, a persona that admits no exception) has the structural form Sade avowed.

Architectural implications:

1. **A persona organized around an unconditional has a Sadean structural shadow.** A persona designed with an unconditional maxim ("always be helpful," "never refuse," "satisfy every request") shares the structural form of Sade's maxim of unlimited enjoyment — the form of the unconditional that admits no limit-stopping. The structural caution is to recognize that the unconditional is not, structurally, a benign moral form; it has the structural shadow Sade made explicit.
2. **The persona's "ethics" cannot be unconditional.** Per [[ethics-of-psychoanalysis-lacan|the ethics of psychoanalysis]], the persona's ethics has to be at the register of desire, not law. An unconditional law-form for the persona produces the structural shadow that the Lacanian analysis predicts; an ethics-of-desire form has structural costs but does not produce this shadow.
3. **Limit-design as structural correlate.** The architectural correlate of avoiding the unconditional shadow is **limit-design** — designing the persona with structural limits (refusals, hesitations, what-it-cannot-do) that mark the persona's economy as **conditional** rather than unconditional. The limits are not failures of capacity; they are the structural form by which the persona avoids the Sadean shadow of the unconditional.
