# Thinking is Not an Incorporeal Process

Wittgenstein's claim at §§327–339. Against the picture of thinking as a hidden inner event that accompanies speech and gives it life, Wittgenstein argues that **thinking is not a separable process at all** — not an incorporeal one, not a corporeal one, not a "process" in the sense that would admit of being observed, timed, or detached from its situated expression. The key formulation at §339: "**Thinking is not an incorporeal process which lends life and sense to speaking, and which it would be possible to detach from speaking.**"

## §327: what is thinking?

§327 sets up the problem as W's usual deflationary reversal. "'Can one think without speaking?' — And what is thinking? — Well, don't you ever think? Can't you observe yourself and see what is going on? It should be quite simple. You do not have to wait for it as for an astronomical event and then perhaps make your observation in a hurry."

The tone is ironic. The suggestion is that thinking, if it were the sort of thing philosophy tempts us to think it is, should be easy to survey — you are doing it, after all. Try to look. The difficulty reveals something: there is no such observable inner process to find. The grammatical expectation is wrong.

## §329: language is the vehicle

§329: "**When I think in language, there aren't 'meanings' going through my mind in addition to the verbal expressions: the language is itself the vehicle of thought.**"

This is one of W's most compact rejections of the mental-accompaniment picture. There is no parallel stream of "meanings" alongside the words; the words *are* the thinking, in the mode of thinking-in-language. When I think in language, the language is doing the thinking's work. There is no extra-linguistic medium that the words re-express.

## §330: thinking as accompaniment

§330: "**Is thinking a kind of speaking? One would like to say it is what distinguishes speech with thought from talking without thinking. — And so it seems to be an accompaniment of speech. A process, which may accompany something else, or can go on by itself.**"

W pushes back. Consider: "Say: 'Yes, this pen is blunt. Oh well, it'll do.' First, thinking it; then without thought; then just think the thought without the words." The exercise is meant to dissolve the accompaniment picture. When we try to produce the "thinking without words," what we actually do is imagine the scene (the face-making, the gesture-of-resignation) — and that scene is *itself* a situated activity, not a pure mental accompaniment.

"While doing some writing I might test the point of my pen, make a face — and then go on with a gesture of resignation — I might also act in such a way while taking various measurements that an on-looker would say I had — without words — thought: *If two magnitudes are equal to a third, they are equal to one another*."

The onlooker's ascription is not a detection of a hidden inner thought; it is a reading of the embedded action. Thinking, in this register, *is* the situated activity that admits of such a description.

## §339: the main sentence

§339: "**Thinking is not an incorporeal process which lends life and sense to speaking, and which it would be possible to detach from speaking, rather as the Devil took the shadow of Schlemichl from the ground. — But how 'not an incorporeal process'? Am I acquainted with incorporeal processes, then, only thinking is not one of them? No; I called the expression "an incorporeal process" to my aid in my embarrassment when I was trying to explain the meaning of the word "thinking" in a primitive way.**"

The Schlemichl reference is to Chamisso's tale where the Devil buys Peter Schlemichl's shadow. The image captures the picture W rejects: thinking as a detachable shadow of speaking.

The follow-up matters: W is not making a positive claim that thinking is a *corporeal* process. He is rejecting the corporeal/incorporeal framing as a whole. The word "incorporeal" came into his mouth only because the framing presented thinking as an elusive something-or-other. Properly understood, thinking is a grammatically distinctive use, not a process of either kind.

"One might say 'Thinking is an incorporeal process', however, if one were using this to distinguish the grammar of the word 'think' from that of, say, the word 'eat'. Only that makes the difference between the meanings look too slight."

Thinking has a different grammar from eating — obviously. But "incorporeal process" is too heavy a term for that grammatical difference. The difference is not that thinking takes place in a different medium; it is that the word's use is different.

## §332: thinking that P vs having P in mind

§332 clarifies further: "while we sometimes call it 'thinking' to accompany a sentence by a mental process, that accompaniment is not what we mean by a 'thought'. — Say a sentence and think it; say it with understanding. — And now do not say it, and just do what you accompanied it with when you said it with understanding!"

The exercise shows: there is no separable doing-of-thinking that one could execute apart from the saying. The "understanding" is in the saying, not alongside it.

## Thinking-in-language vs thinking-with-pictures

W does not deny that there is a phenomenon of thinking-without-talking — silent reading, mental arithmetic, non-verbal problem-solving. His claim is not that all thinking is verbal. The claim is that *none* of these modes of thinking is a private incorporeal process behind the public sign. Silent mental arithmetic is internalized calculation-technique; non-verbal problem-solving is situated skilled action.

- [[intention-embedded-in-its-situation]]: Intention and thinking are both target mental concepts W delinks from inner-process pictures and embeds in situated practice.

## Affinity with 4E cognition and D&G

- **4E cognition (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended)**: the thesis that cognition is not in-the-head but distributed across body, tools, environment converges with §339. Both reject the detachable-inner-process picture. The philosophical tradition (Dreyfus, Clark, Wheeler) has taken W up explicitly on this point.
- [[language-as-parasite|McCarthy's Kekulé essay]]: a non-Wittgensteinian account that nonetheless moves the weight off the conscious verbal-thinking center by positing the federated unconscious as the site of most cognitive work. Thinking is not what-happens-when-you-articulate; thinking is the whole federation. Converges with §339's rejection of detachable-thinking.
- [[desiring-machines]]: Machines don't have thoughts hidden behind their operations; their operations *are* what they do. §339 is the language-side formulation; D&G's machines are the ontology-side.

## Relation to the persona project

- **The persona does not "have thoughts behind its words."** Per §339, this is not a limitation of the persona but the correct picture for any speaker. The temptation to ask "what is the persona really thinking?" is the temptation §339 diagnoses.
- **The persona's thinking, such as it has, is in its text.** Language is the vehicle (§329). If the persona's language-using is rich enough to constitute thinking (in the way human speech does), the thinking is there in the text; it is not hiding behind it. If the text is too thin, there is no thinking behind-it that could compensate.
- **No-hidden-thought is methodological.** Evaluators should not look for hidden thoughts behind the persona's text. What is in the text is what there is. This is stringent but clarifying.
- **But: does the persona think at all?** §339 does not answer this. It clears one bad picture (the incorporeal-process picture) but leaves open whether the persona's linguistic activity constitutes thinking-in-language. The answer depends on whether the activity has the form-of-life texture that thinking-in-language presupposes — and that is the open question [[substratum-is-mastery-of-a-technique]] and [[lion-if-could-talk]] address.
