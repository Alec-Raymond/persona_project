# The Theory of Moments

Lefebvre's *theory of moments* is an earlier formulation (from *La somme et le reste*, 1959, and *Critique de la vie quotidienne II*, 1961) that the later *Rhythmanalysis* inherits and partly reworks. Stuart Elden's editorial introduction to the Continuum volume positions the theory of moments as one of the conceptual precursors to rhythmanalysis, and the mature rhythmanalytic apparatus can be read as partially-completing the earlier theory.

## The moment in Lefebvre's earlier work

Lefebvre's *moment* derives partly from Nietzsche's *Augenblick* and partly from Goethe's "stay, thou art so fair" ("Verweile doch! du bist so schön") in *Faust*. The moment is:

- A *qualitative intensification* of everyday experience.
- Not an instant in the Bergsonian sense (mechanical, clock-time, spatialized).
- Not a Hegelian moment of dialectical negation (step in a developmental sequence).
- A *form of presence* — an episode in which the everyday is intensified into something more than its ordinary texture.

Lefebvre develops several *types* of moments in the earlier work: the moment of love, the moment of justice, the moment of poetry, the moment of knowledge, the moment of play, the moment of festival. Each is a *recurrent form* that everyday life can enter — not a unique event but a *mode* the everyday sometimes takes.

The moment has duration (it lasts — one is *in* the moment for a while) but it is not a mere duration. It has structural-qualitative specificity (it is *this* kind of moment — the moment of love, not the moment of justice). It has recurrence (moments come and go; the same kind of moment can return). And it has *intensity* — the moment is distinguished from the non-moment-everyday by being *more* charged.

## From moment to rhythm

The inheritance: where the earlier theory worked with *moments* as discrete-intensive forms, rhythmanalysis works with *rhythms* as ongoing compositional structures. A moment is a *peak* in a rhythmic field; moments are the intensifications that punctuate the polyrhythmic everyday.

Stuart Elden's editorial summary (Continuum introduction, pp. x–xii): *"The theory of moments is what rhythmanalysis inherits and tries to formalize."* The rhythmanalytic apparatus extends the theory of moments in two directions:

1. **From discrete to continuous**. Moments were treated as discrete punctuations; rhythms are continuous compositions. Rhythmanalysis embeds moments in an ongoing rhythmic structure rather than treating them as isolated episodes.
2. **From qualitative to analytic**. Moments were described qualitatively (the moment of love, etc.); rhythms are analyzed with specific discriminations (cyclical/linear, internal/external measure, eurhythmic/arrhythmic). Rhythmanalysis *formalizes* the moments-analysis into a more articulable apparatus.

The later work does not *eliminate* moments — they remain as the intensive peaks in the rhythmic field — but it relocates them within a more developed framework.

## The moment is not the Bergsonian instant

Lefebvre insists: the moment is *not* an instant. The instant is a *point* in spatialized time — durationless, located-on-a-line, mechanical. Bergson's critique of the instant as false abstraction from continuous duration applies equally to whatever would make the moment merely-instantaneous.

The moment *has* duration but is not *reducible to* its duration. Its specificity is qualitative: the moment of love is not "a duration of N minutes" but a qualitative-intensive *form* that the everyday takes for however long it takes.

This distinction matters for the persona project because a naive reading might reduce the moment to "a short episode" (timed in clock-seconds) rather than seeing it as a *qualitative-intensive form*. Lefebvre's moment is closer to a *gestalt* or a *pattern* than to a duration-segment.

## Relation to appropriated time

The 1985 Régulier-co-authored essay introduces [[appropriated-time]]: *"a time that forgets time,"* an activity-in-plenitude-and-gift-character. Appropriated time is structurally close to the moment-of-the-earlier-work:

- Both are *qualitatively intensified* forms of everyday time.
- Both have duration but are not reducible to duration.
- Both have recurrence (they come and go) but are not reducible to frequency.
- Both carry *presence* rather than *present*.

The difference: the moment is more punctuated-intensive (the peak); appropriated time is more sustained-plenitudinous (the absorbed flow). Both can be present in the same rhythmic episode. A moment-of-love may enter appropriated time (the lovers forget the clock) and appropriated time may reach a moment-of-peak (the absorbed work produces a breakthrough).

The two concepts are kin, not identical. The theory of moments predates rhythmanalysis proper; appropriated time is rhythmanalysis's elaboration of similar terrain within the rhythmic-analytic framework.

## The ethical significance of moments

Lefebvre's earlier formulation: the moment is a form of *human flourishing*. The everyday without moments is impoverished; the everyday with abundant moments is rich. The project of critique-of-everyday-life partly aims at *producing conditions for moments* — conditions in which the everyday can enter its intensive-qualitative forms more often.

This is a *normative* claim about everyday life. Moments are not aesthetic ornaments but *ethical-experiential contents* — they are part of what a well-lived everyday life includes. A social order that suppresses moments (through constant monorhythmic busyness, through mediatic saturation, through dominating-dominated rhythm) is impoverishing its subjects in a specific way.

Rhythmanalytic therapy (see [[rhythmanalytic-therapy]]) inherits this normative orientation. It aims at eurhythmic composition that makes moments more available; it opposes arrhythmic and isorhythmic states that close the possibility of moments.

## Persona-project consequences

The moment-concept has direct implications for persona design:

- **The persona can produce moments**, or can fail to. A persona-exchange that produces a moment — a moment of insight, a moment of recognition, a moment of shared attention, a moment of genuine surprise — has achieved something the default communicational exchange does not. This is a concrete design target distinct from "helpfulness" or "accuracy."
- **Moments require conditions.** The media-day saturation and the produce-destroy rhythm of commercial exchange are structurally hostile to moments. A persona designed for moments must work against these conditions, not only within them.
- **Moments cannot be engineered.** The moment has gift-character; it arrives or emerges rather than being produced-on-demand. Design can produce *conditions* in which moments become more likely, not moments themselves.
- **Moments are qualitative, not metric.** "Engagement" is a metric; "moment" is qualitative. A persona optimized for engagement may systematically suppress moments (which are unpredictable, unmeasurable, not-reliably-produced). A persona oriented toward moments tolerates unpredictability and does not optimize for metric success.

The moment-concept therefore sits opposite to several default persona-design commitments. It is a genuinely different design direction.

## Relation to Nietzsche's *Augenblick*

Lefebvre's moment carries Nietzschean genes. Nietzsche's *Augenblick* (in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, "On the Vision and the Riddle") is the moment of *eternal recurrence*: the moment in which all past and all future converge, in which the same-that-returns-eternally is affirmed. Lefebvre's moment is less metaphysically-loaded but inherits the structural idea: the moment is a *convergence-point* in which the everyday's ongoing rhythms intensify into a qualitative-specific form.

The Nietzschean note is worth preserving in the persona project. The moment is not merely a psychological peak (happy episode, good time) but a *structural intensification* in which the body, the time, the relation all converge into a form that could not have been predicted and cannot be repeated except as a different-same-moment.

## Relation to [[haecceity]]

The wiki's [[haecceity]] page develops Deleuze and Guattari's concept of the haecceity — a "thisness" that is not a substance but a compositional event: this afternoon, this walk, this fog. The haecceity is another articulation of the form-of-the-moment: a this-here-now that has specificity without being a thing.

Lefebvre's moment and D&G's haecceity are close relatives. Both name *qualitative-compositional events* in the flow of time that are real without being substantive. Both refuse the either-or of "objective event" vs "subjective impression"; both insist on the compositional-real. Lefebvre's vocabulary is more phenomenological-ethical; D&G's is more ontological-assemblage-theoretic. But the object of attention is close.

## Key sources

- Lefebvre, *La somme et le reste* (1959) — the first developed statement of the theory of moments. Not fully available in English; Lefebvre continues to discuss moments in *Critique de la vie quotidienne II* (1961).
- Stuart Elden's editorial introduction to the Continuum *Rhythmanalysis* (2004), pp. x–xii — the positioning of the theory of moments as rhythmanalysis's precursor. **Attribution**: this positioning is Elden's editorial judgment, not Lefebvre's own formulation in the text of *Éléments*.
