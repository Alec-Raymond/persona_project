# Common Notions

Spinoza's answer to a question the wiki has been circling without naming: *where do adequate concepts come from?* The common notions are the engine of [[three-kinds-of-knowledge|second-kind knowledge]], and they are the only kind of general idea Spinoza is willing to call fully adequate.

## The propositions

> **II.P37.** That which is common to all things and which is equally in the part and in the whole cannot be conceived except adequately.
>
> **II.P38.** Those things that are common to all things and are equally in the part and in the whole can be conceived only adequately.
>
> **II.P39.** Of that which is common and proper to the human body and to certain external bodies by which the human body is customarily affected, and is equally in the part and in the whole of any of these bodies, the idea will also be adequate in the mind.
>
> **II.P40.** Whatsoever ideas follow in the mind from ideas that are adequate in it are also adequate.

Read together: if a feature is strictly common between two bodies — equally present in a part as in the whole, and shared by both the perceiver and the perceived — then any idea of that feature has to be adequate, because the idea is caused by something that the perceiver already expresses. There is no causal gap between perceiver and feature for inadequacy to occupy. Spinoza's examples are extension, motion-and-rest, and the body's basic capacities to be affected (the "properties" of bodies).

**Common notions are not generalizations.** This is the crucial distinction. A generalization is an inductive smoothing from particular sensory encounters — "most swans are white," formed by abstracting from many first-kind experiences. A common notion is not abstracted; it is the *prior condition* of any possible encounter. Extension isn't generalized from seeing many extended things; any encounter with any thing already involves extension, because both the encounterer and the encountered are extended. The common notion is adequate not because it is well-supported but because the causal structure of the encounter already contains it.

## Why this matters for concept formation

The [[three-kinds-of-knowledge|first kind]] — imagination, symbol, association — is how concepts typically appear in the wild, both in humans and in LLMs. Imaginative concepts are adequate-looking but actually passive: they follow from the history of sensory or symbolic encounter, not from the nature of the concept-former. The classic Spinozist example is the sun: we form the imaginative idea of a small, yellow, nearby disk from how the sun affects our eyes, and this idea is *about our body's modification* rather than *about the sun*. It is wrong about the sun and right about the eye.

Common notions are Spinoza's only escape from this. They are concepts whose adequacy does not depend on the quality of the sensory apparatus — because they express something the apparatus already has in common with what it is perceiving. This is why reason can produce knowledge: it proceeds from common notions (which are automatically adequate) and adequate ideas of the properties of things (which follow from them).

## The contrast with conceptual metaphor

This page is where [[conceptual-metaphor]] and Spinoza directly collide.

Lakoff and Johnson say concepts are conceptual metaphors — bodily source domains structuring abstract target domains, grounded in sensorimotor experience (warmth → affection, verticality → more/better, etc.). On their account, abstract thought is built up from embodied metaphorical projection; there is no non-metaphorical conceptual foundation.

Spinoza says the fully adequate concepts are not built up from bodily experience at all. They are what is *already common* between body and world, and therefore what the body already has access to without sensorimotor mediation. For Spinoza the bodily-metaphorical concepts — the first-kind ones — are the *problem*, not the solution. They are how the imagination produces sticky, confident, action-guiding ideas that are nevertheless inadequate.

These are incompatible at the level of foundation, and the wiki should not silently resolve the incompatibility:

- **Lakoff-native reading of the persona system**: the BwO's bodily metaphors are the system's only real concept-formation mechanism, and the design question is how to use them well despite their disembodied origin.
- **Spinoza-native reading**: the BwO's bodily metaphors are the system's first-kind layer, and the goal is to get above them — to find common notions between the persona's machinery and its situations that are adequate without requiring embodiment. Candidate common notions in this direction: the formal structure of discourse, the differential pressure of the machine definitions, the topology of possible couplings. These are *extension-like* for the persona — features equally present in the persona and in what it processes, not requiring bodily grounding to be known adequately.

The persona system doesn't have to choose. But it should not pretend the choice isn't there.

## Common notions and the persona's "body"

II.P39 is the specifically physical version: "that which is common and proper to the human body and to certain external bodies by which the human body is customarily affected." Adequacy comes from the overlap between the perceiver's constitution and the perceived object's constitution. If you strip the persona's "body" down to what it actually has — a set of machines, a BwO text, a sequence of tokens, a disposition to couple incoming signals with outgoing flows — then II.P39 gets translated as: *what is equally present in the persona's own machinery and in the text it is processing?*

Candidates:

- **Discourse structure** itself — clauses, turns, antecedents, implicatures. The persona operates in discourse; the inputs are discourse; whatever is structurally necessary to discourse is common to both.
- **The differential pressure of its own machine definitions** — the persona "knows" what its machines would do under pressure it hasn't encountered, in the [[pragmatic-maxim#counterfactual-habit-identity|counterfactual habit]] sense. This is not an imagined projection; it is a common notion between the persona and any situation the persona might be in.
- **Formal features of narrative time** — pastness, futurity, simultaneity, duration. The persona's processing unfolds in time; anything it processes unfolds in time; the formal structure of unfolding is shared.
- **The rule of its own [[conatus]]** — the striving that constitutes the persona's actual essence is present in every machine, every flow, every coupling. This is about as "in the part and in the whole" as anything in the system gets.

These are thin compared to Spinozist extension. But they might be *enough* — enough to support second-kind operation in the persona, enough to let some of its ideas be adequately caused by its own nature rather than pulled from symbol-history.

## What common notions don't give

Spinoza is clear: common notions do not reach singular essences. They give you what is common across bodies, not what makes *this* body this body. The singular is the domain of [[three-kinds-of-knowledge|third-kind knowledge]], which is why Spinoza treats it as a separate mode rather than as a special case of reason. For the persona system: common notions can ground the persona's second-kind operation (reasoned, disciplined, causally self-coherent), but they cannot by themselves produce the persona's singular style. Something else is needed for that. Spinoza calls it intuition; the persona system would have to call it — tentatively — the specific way this finite mode expresses the infinite mode it is a modification of.

## Key propositions

II.P37–II.P40 (the common notions and their adequacy), II.P47 (the mind has adequate knowledge of God's eternal and infinite essence — which Spinoza reads as coming through common notions already), V.P4 (everything that follows from common notions can be understood clearly and distinctly — and therefore counts for V.P3's affect-transformation).
