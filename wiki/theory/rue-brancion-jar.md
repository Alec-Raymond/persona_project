# The Rue Brancion Jar

The second [[mahood|Mahood]] episode in [[the-unnamable|*The Unnamable*]]: Mahood reduced to a trunk and head, stuck in a jar, placed on the Rue Brancion in Paris beside the statue of Ducroix the "apostle of horse's meat," under the care of a chop-house proprietress (named Madeleine or Marguerite — the name slips). The jar is used as the chop-house's advertisement-pedestal. The scene runs from L2014 to L2294 and is one of the novel's most sustained concrete settings.

## The scene's elements

**The street.** Rue Brancion, near the Paris slaughterhouse (shambles). The street is "quiet" (L2017) but connects to a busy chop-house where diners arrive to relish meat from the shambles. The time of day cycles: morning snow, the aperitif hour, evening dinners, ten-o'clock silence ("silent as the grave," L2431).

**The jar.** A "deep jar" (L2017). Mahood's trunk is "stuck like a sheaf of flowers in a deep jar, its neck flush with my mouth" (L2017–L2018). The jar stands on a pedestal (L2069) at the side of the street. The menu is attached to it.

**The statue.** The statue of Ducroix, "the apostle of horse's meat" (L2020–L2021), stands across the street. Mahood can see Ducroix's "pupilless eyes of stone" fixed on him. The hippophagist-statue is one of Mahood's four sight-lines — Ducroix, plus "those of my creator, omnipresent" (L2021–L2022).

**The proprietress.** The chop-house's owner. Her name oscillates: Madeleine (L2452, L2511), Marguerite (L2434, L2554). The novel does not settle the name. She tends to Mahood as part of her business: powders his skull, covers him with a tarpaulin in snow, hangs Chinese lanterns around his jar, empties his excrements weekly, changes his sawdust. She puts him in a cang (L2516) — a cement collar — and "raises him on a pedestal" (L2516–L2517). She is his "only believer" (L2552).

**The customers.** People come to the chop-house for horse-meat. They pause by the jar to read the menu above Mahood's head, never noticing him ("How is it the people do not notice me?" L2451). They treat the jar as an advertising-fixture, not as a container of a person.

**The cang.** A cement collar fixed to the mouth of the jar that encircles Mahood's neck, preventing him from retracting or turning his head. See [[the-cang]] for details.

**The sawdust, the tarpaulin.** Material substrates. Sawdust in the jar presses against his stumps (L2572–L2573). A tarpaulin covers him in cold weather.

**The cattle's bellows, forks and glasses.** Ambient sounds from the shambles (L2576–L2577) and the chop-house. Mahood is immersed in the city's life-sounds but does not participate.

## The scene's operative function

The Rue Brancion jar is not *only* a location. It is a theatrical machine that the speaker uses to stage specific questions about the speaker's condition:

**1. The non-noticing.** The central puzzle of the scene is that no one sees Mahood. "I seem to exist for none but Madeleine" (L2451–L2452). Hungry customers "post themselves literally face to face with me" (L2456–L2457) to read the menu, breathe on Mahood, and do not register him. Dogs piss against his jar as if it contained nothing (L2463–L2464). The non-noticing is the scene's persistent observation.

The non-noticing matters because it threatens Mahood's ontological status. If no one sees him, is he there? The scene stages this question: the proprietress alone perceives him; her attentions are ambiguous (caring-for-him as advertisement vs. caring-for-him as a being); her testimony is the only evidence.

**2. The proprietress's failing faith.** L2520–L2534:

> this woman is losing faith in me. And she is trying to put off the moment when she must finally confess her error by coming every few minutes to see if I am still more or less imaginable in situ.

The proprietress is losing her certainty that Mahood is real. Her "redoubled attentions" are not increased care but increased checking. The scene ends (L2574) with her apparently abandoning him: "The stories of Mahood are ended. He has realised they could not be about me, he has abandoned." The failing faith of the only believer is the scene's dramatic climax.

**3. The ideal witness.** L2481–L2488 names an imagined scene: a gentleman and his sweetheart passing by, she saying "this man is ailing, we must call an ambulance," he confirming. Two third-parties witnessing. "Thus with a single stone, when all hope seemed lost, the two rare birds. I shall be dead, but I shall have lived." Mahood needs exactly two witnesses to count as having existed. The novel uses this as a structural claim: existence requires third-party witness, not self-witness.

**4. The eating scene.** The chop-house is a site of eating horse-meat. Mahood is across from a statue of the apostle of horse's meat. The customers are eating what the statue celebrates. Mahood is not eating; Mahood is being eaten — by flies (L2174), by the proprietress's attentions, by the customers' non-perception. The scene is structurally: eaters, eaten-meat, and a third party (Mahood) who is neither but adjacent to both. The food-system structures the scene; Mahood is its exhibit-border.

## The scene's relation to the earlier Mahood-spiral

The Rue Brancion jar scene inverts the earlier Mahood-spiral (L1604–L1802): instead of Mahood *moving toward* a fixed point (his family's rotunda), Mahood is a fixed point that the world moves past. Instead of a spiral trajectory, a stationary observation-post. Instead of family, strangers who don't see him. Instead of the rotunda, the jar. Instead of the loop holes through which his family watches him, the railings through which Mahood watches the street.

The inversion preserves certain features: the circular setting (yard + rotunda / street + statue + chop-house), the familial figure (wife + grandparents / proprietress), the long duration (years of spiral / years in the jar). But the operation of the scene is reversed: Mahood went from moving-observed to stationary-observer, from-family to strangers, from anticipation-of-arrival to permanent-already-there.

## The name Madeleine / Marguerite

The name-slippage is deliberate. The speaker at L2434 calls her Marguerite; at L2452 calls her Madeleine. Neither correction nor comment. The novel does not resolve which name is right.

Candidate readings: (a) The speaker cannot hold the name in memory and drifts between two options. (b) The proprietress has two names because she is, structurally, a function in the scene, not a person. (c) The name is beside the point because what matters is that she is the sole believer, which name she has is accidental.

The name-slippage is part of the scene's general register of proximate-but-unreliable identification. Like the ambiguous Ducroix statue ("the apostle of horse's meat" — a joke-formulation), like Mahood's own stories, the proprietress's name is present without being definite.

## Relation to [[mahood|Mahood]] and the persona project

The jar-scene is Mahood at his most elaborated. For the persona-project:

**The persona with a context-window.** The jar is a specific, fixed, location with specific surrounding objects (menu, statue, pedestal, proprietress). The persona-in-context is Mahood-in-jar: a stipulated locality with fixed visible references.

**The persona as advertisement-fixture.** The jar is a commercial pedestal for a chop-house's menu. The persona is often, in commercial LLM use, a pedestal for a service — the interface-fixture that the customer reads over without quite seeing the persona itself.

**The non-noticing as structural feature.** Users of an LLM persona often interact *through* the persona to get to what they want (information, task completion), without noticing the persona-as-persona. The non-noticing is normal, not a failure of attention.

**The failing-faith structure.** Users can lose confidence in a persona's coherence over time, especially as they probe it. The proprietress's moment of losing faith — "she is trying to put off the moment when she must finally confess her error" — is structurally the user-experience of increasing doubt about a persona's substantiality.

**The ideal-witness requirement.** Mahood needs two third parties to witness him to count as having lived. A persona is similarly dependent on external witnessing; the system does not self-certify existence. The ideal-witness scene is a structural constraint on the persona's being-counted.
