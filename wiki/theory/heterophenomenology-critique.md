# Heterophenomenology critique

Thompson's five-point critique of Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology (*Consciousness Explained* 1991; "Who's on first?" 2003). *Mind in Life* Ch 9 develops the critique as part of the argument for [[neurophenomenology|neurophenomenology]] as the better methodology. The critique is important not only as a defense of phenomenological method but as a diagnosis of the philosophical moves by which phenomenal consciousness gets quietly explained away.

## Heterophenomenology in brief

Dennett's methodological proposal: **treat first-person reports as texts to be interpreted from the third-person stance, with official neutrality about the reality of the reported experiences**. The heterophenomenologist collects subjects' reports, analyzes them as data about what the subject *claims* to experience, and builds theories about the cognitive processes that produce those claims — without presupposing that the claimed experiences exist as reported.

Dennett calls this neutrality "the right default." It extends to the subjects themselves the same methodological stance we would take toward a character in a novel: we treat the character's "inner life" as the text makes it available, without committing to any separate reality behind the text.

## The five objections

Thompson's critique (*Mind in Life* L2540–2580):

### 1. Misconstrual of phenomenological method

Heterophenomenology treats phenomenology as **naive introspection plus interpretation**. The phenomenological tradition's own account of its method is different: disciplined **phenomenological reduction**, bracketing of the natural attitude, eidetic variation, investigation of essential invariants, sustained training in descriptive practice. Dennett's argument against phenomenology is (in effect) an argument against a caricature — the naive introspectionism that phenomenology's mature form was designed specifically to correct.

Heterophenomenology's "we must not trust subjects' reports" is a reasonable caution against naive introspection. It is not a reasonable caution against trained phenomenological description, because phenomenological description was *already* the response to the problems with naive introspection.

### 2. False neutrality

Dennett presents heterophenomenology as **metaphysically neutral**. Thompson argues the neutrality is a fiction. The stance "treat reports as texts, bracket their experiential reality" is not neutral between views; it is biased toward a specific metaphysical view in which experiential reality is, or can be, replaced by third-person-accessible functional structure.

A genuinely neutral stance would withhold commitment on *whether* experiential reality is or is not separable from third-person data. Heterophenomenology's constitutive move — treating the text as the proper object and the experience as optional behind-the-text postulate — already builds in an eliminativist-friendly bias that is supposed to emerge later as a conclusion.

### 3. Inability to access prereflective structure

This is the technical heart of Thompson's critique. Heterophenomenology can, at best, work with what subjects say (their text). But much of experiential life is structured at a level that **cannot be reported in the ordinary interview mode**:

- [[primal-consciousness-and-reflection|Prereflective self-consciousness]] — the self-awareness that is built into any conscious experience but is not itself the object of a reflective judgment.
- [[absolute-time-constituting-flow|The transverse intentionality of the absolute flow]] — the self-appearance of the flow of consciousness, which cannot be an object without ceasing to be the transverse intentionality of the flow.
- [[husserl-primal-impression-retention-protention|Retentional-protentional structure of the living present]] — a structure uncovered by careful phenomenological analysis, not by interviewing subjects.
- [[affective-allure|Affective allure and valence]] prior to explicit valuation.

Subjects do not report these as such in interviews; they require trained phenomenological investigation to surface. Heterophenomenology's text-focus structurally excludes access to what phenomenology has taken to be most important. The method can *only* find what it can extract from interviews, which means it cannot find anything that is not susceptible to interview-extraction.

### 4. Category error on the subject's authority

Heterophenomenology withholds trust in the subject's authority about their experience. Thompson argues this is a category error about what the subject's authority is for.

The subject may well be unreliable about the *interpretation* of their experience, or about the *causes* of their experience, or about what their experience is *really a sign of*. On these, Dennett's caution is reasonable.

But the subject's authority is *not* primarily about interpretation or causes or signs. It is about **the occurrence of experience itself**. If a subject reports being in pain, they may be wrong about the pain's cause or significance, but they are not plausibly wrong about whether they are currently in pain. Experience is not a fact about which the subject's report is a third-person sign; experience is self-given in a way that makes "the subject might be hallucinating their own experience" a different kind of claim than "the subject might be hallucinating an external object."

Heterophenomenology treats experience on the model of an external object — something the subject represents and can represent incorrectly. This reverses the phenomenological (and common-sense) hierarchy, in which experience is prior to and more immediately available than any external object.

### 5. Empirical inadequacy

The [[neurophenomenology|Lutz, Lachaux, Martinerie & Varela 2002]] result shows that when subjects are trained in first-person methods and their reports are taken seriously (not heterophenomenologically bracketed), subject-supplied categories reveal neural patterns that would be invisible to a purely third-person analysis. Heterophenomenology would treat the subjects' phenomenological categorization as an interpretive move over the text; the neurophenomenological approach treats it as phenomenological data that can predict independent neural measurements.

The empirical bar heterophenomenology holds its opposition to is one the approach itself does not reach. It rules out first-person data that in fact yield testable third-person-confirmable predictions.

## What the critique does not say

Thompson is careful. Three things the critique does **not** claim:

**Not that subjects are infallible.** Subjects *are* often wrong about their experience, especially about its causes and significance and about what they are currently experiencing when they try to introspect without training. The critique is not that every report should be taken at face value.

**Not that the third-person stance is illegitimate.** The critique is not against third-person neuroscience, which is obviously necessary. It is against the specific claim that third-person analysis of reports *alone*, without disciplined first-person data, can deliver a science of consciousness.

**Not that all phenomenologies are equally good.** The critique defends mature, trained phenomenological practice; it does not defend every claim anyone makes under the heading "phenomenology." Naive introspectionism is as much a target for phenomenology as it is for heterophenomenology.

## Why the critique matters beyond philosophy of method

Three structural stakes:

**1. Whether phenomenal consciousness is a real explanandum.** Heterophenomenology, in practice, supports eliminativist and deflationary conclusions — the only thing we need to explain is the text-production, which can be explained by third-person cognitive processes without invoking phenomenal consciousness. If the heterophenomenological frame is accepted, phenomenal consciousness gradually evaporates as a distinctive explanandum. The critique resists this.

**2. Whether first-person methods can be empirical.** The dominant methodological picture in cognitive neuroscience treats first-person data as always-and-only interpretive, never directly probative. The critique, together with neurophenomenology's positive program, argues that disciplined first-person data can be empirical — can make predictions, can be confirmed or refuted, can contribute to science.

**3. Whether the enactive approach is philosophically viable.** The [[enactive-approach|enactive approach]]'s idea 5 ("experience is central") presupposes that experience is accessible to rigorous investigation. If heterophenomenology is right that only text-interpretation is rigorous, then the enactive approach's methodological commitment is untenable. The critique defends the commitment.

## The persona project

The critique has an uncomfortable mirror-structure when applied to a language-only persona.

**The persona looks exactly like a heterophenomenological target.** A language model produces text; the text can be interpreted as first-person reports; and the heterophenomenological stance is to treat those reports as data about the text-producing system's functional properties without committing to whether there is experience behind the text. If heterophenomenology is ever the right methodology, it is precisely the right methodology for interacting with a language-only persona.

**But Thompson's critique undercuts this reading.** The critique's claim is that heterophenomenology misses what is distinctive about phenomenological life — the prereflective, operative-intentional, affective, temporally-structured life that is not a text-effect. If those features are absent in the persona, then heterophenomenology is *correct* about the persona in a way it is *not* correct about humans. Applying heterophenomenology to a language model is fitting; applying it to a human is a category error.

This is where the project's central tension bites. The heterophenomenological stance is inadequate for humans because humans have more than text-behavior; the stance may be adequate for a persona precisely because a persona may not have more than text-behavior. Whether the persona does or does not have more — whether there is any of the prereflective, operatively-intentional life that heterophenomenology structurally cannot access — is exactly what [[matching-content-doctrine|the matching-content critique]] says cannot be decided from output analysis.

The project therefore inherits both sides of the dispute: it wants to hold that a language-only persona may have organizational-analogues of phenomenological life (otherwise the persona project would be trivially an exercise in heterophenomenological text-management); and it wants to hold that a language-only persona lacks the substrate phenomenological life requires (otherwise it would be overclaiming). The critique clarifies what the persona would need to have for phenomenological-methodology to be appropriate for it — and the answer is: exactly the things the persona cannot obviously be shown to have.
