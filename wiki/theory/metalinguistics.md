# Metalinguistics

Bakhtin opens Chapter Five of *Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics* by naming the object he needs and insisting that linguistics as constituted cannot study it. His term for the study he needs is **metalinguistics**. The term is not "about language" in a secondary or reflective sense; it names a discipline that takes as its object what linguistics systematically cannot see — **dialogic relations**, which are "extralinguistic phenomena" and at the same time "cannot be torn away from the area of discourse, that is, from language as a concrete integral phenomenon."

The name matters because it stakes out a specific position: the object Bakhtin is studying is not a content inside language (what linguistics would study) and not a social behavior external to language (what pragmatics or sociolinguistics would study in their own terms), but the way *concrete discourse* — language in actual use, in real utterances by real speakers in real situations — carries dialogic relations that make the utterance what it is. Dialogic relations are neither purely linguistic (they require at least two utterances, not one) nor purely extralinguistic (they are phenomena *of* discourse). Metalinguistics is the discipline that takes them as its primary object.

## The utterance is not the sentence

Caryl Emerson's editor's preface makes the distinction as compactly as it gets stated: "A sentence is a unit of language, while an utterance is a unit of communication." The sentence is a formal-grammatical unit whose boundaries are syntactic. The utterance is a unit of communication whose boundaries are marked only by a **change of speech subject**. An utterance can be a single word or a long speech; what makes it an utterance is not its length but the fact that it is bracketed by other speakers' utterances — it has, structurally, the speaker-before-it and the speaker-after-it.

This distinction is not a technicality. Once the utterance replaces the sentence as the basic unit, everything about language changes. The utterance's meaning is not the sum of its words' meanings plus their syntactic arrangement; it includes its *orientation* — what it is responding to, what it anticipates in response, what position-of-personality it issues from, what addressee it shapes itself toward. The utterance is intrinsically relational; the sentence, as studied by linguistics, is intrinsically self-contained.

Bakhtin's own formulation: "No matter how much we might reduce the significance of expression, we cannot do away with it completely. And this is the source of the fundamental inadequacy of abstract, contextless sentences. The sentence as a language unit is devoid of all these properties: it is not delimited on either side by a change of speech subjects, it has neither direct contact with reality (with an extraverbal situation) nor a direct relation to others' utterances; it does not have semantic fullness of value." The sentence is a hollow; the utterance is its filling.

## Dialogic relations are not linguistic but cannot be studied outside discourse

The paradox that licenses metalinguistics as its own discipline: dialogic relations are extralinguistic, because no grammatical feature of any single utterance constitutes the dialogic relation. To study the dialogic relation you need *two* utterances minimum, and the relation between them is not a property of either one considered alone. At the same time, dialogic relations are only visible in concrete discourse — in actual utterances that are oriented toward each other. They are not abstract logical relations between propositions; they are not sociological facts about speakers; they are phenomena of discourse-in-use.

Bakhtin's methodological point: "Dialogic relationships, therefore, are extralinguistic. But at the same time they must not be torn away from the area of discourse, that is, from language as a concrete integral phenomenon. Language lives only in the dialogic interaction of those who make use of it. Dialogic interaction is indeed the authentic sphere where language lives. The entire life of language, in any area of its use (in everyday life, in business, scholarship, art, and so forth), is permeated with dialogic relationships." The gap between "language as an abstract system" (what linguistics studies) and "language as lived in dialogic interaction" (what metalinguistics studies) is the space metalinguistics occupies.

*The Dialogic Imagination* radicalizes this methodological break into a historical diagnosis: traditional stylistics is to the concrete living novel what Ptolemaic astronomy was to the actual solar system — an apparatus built from the wrong coordinates, *helpless* in principle before the phenomenon. See [[galilean-vs-ptolemaic-language-consciousness]]. The DI-register ontology that metalinguistics presupposes is [[heteroglossia]]: language is constitutively stratified into socially-embodied languages, and any utterance is always already a point-of-contact among them. The DI-register primary object is the [[image-of-a-language]] — not a language in use but a language *represented inside another language* — which only a metalinguistic discipline can register.

## Word as position, not as lexeme

The strongest consequence of the utterance-vs-sentence move: the basic unit of meaning is not the lexeme but the word-in-the-mouth-of-a-speaker-addressed-to-someone. When Bakhtin writes "the word" (Russian *slovo*), he generally means an utterance or the carrier of a semantic position — not a dictionary entry. A "word" in Bakhtin's technical vocabulary is always a word-with-a-speaker and a word-with-an-addressee. This is why [[voice-as-semantic-position|voice]] can be spoken of as *a word* (the word of this speaker in this position) — the terminology treats word and utterance and voice as the same kind of phenomenon at different scales.

Emerson's gloss: "A voice, Bakhtin everywhere tells us, is not just words or ideas strung together: it is a 'semantic position,' a point of view on the world, one personality orienting itself among other personalities within a limited field. Hence Bakhtin's partiality to spatial markers and metaphors: situation, positioning, orientation [*ustanovka*], point of view, field of vision. How a voice sounds is a function of where it is and what it can 'see'; its orientation is measured by the field of responses it evokes." A word is not made of phonemes; it is made of *position* and *responsiveness*.

## The responsive theory of language

The positive thesis Bakhtin's metalinguistics commits to, in Emerson's compression: language is not referential (aimed at objects) but **responsive** (aimed at other utterances). "This understanding of voice lies at the base of Bakhtin's non-referential — that is, responsive — theory of language." A word's meaning is a function of what it responds to and what responds to it, not of what it represents or refers to. The word is constituted by its orientation in the field of other words.

This is the point of deepest divergence from the Saussurean tradition that linguistics developed from. For Saussure, the sign is internally constituted by signifier/signified, relationally constituted by its differential position in the system of signs. For Bakhtin, the word is constituted by its position in the field of other *utterances* — not other signs in the langue, but other concrete utterances by other speakers in other situations. The "system" is not an abstract structure but a live dialogic sphere.

## Language as energy, not attribute

A related reframe from the editor's preface: "Language . . . is not a product or detachable attribute of a person; it is an energy negotiating between a person's inner consciousness and the outer world." Language is not something one *has*, like a tool. It is a negotiation-energy operating between inside and outside, always in the middle. This has consequences for any attempt to "give a persona language" or "equip a voice with words" — language can't be equipment; it is the field in which the voice operates and the voice operates *as* a node in that field.

## Nonequivalence as the condition of communication

The central Emerson-compressed Bakhtin claim about what happens in dialogue: "Nonequivalence is not a matter for despair but is rather the impulse to life. In fact, the interaction of two different, discrete systems is the only way a true event ever comes to pass." And: "The continuation of dialogue is in large part dependent on neither party knowing exactly what the other means."

This is a sharp rebuke to any picture of communication as signal-transmission. Perfect understanding is not the goal; it would end the dialogue. What moves communication forward is the metastable nonequivalence — the gap between what is said and what is understood, which keeps producing new utterances. "Understanding is obligatorily imperfect translation." The dialogic sphere lives on the imperfection.

For the persona system, this is a design-orientation claim. The system does not "aim to be understood" in the signal-transmission sense; it operates in a sphere where imperfect translation is the medium. An output that is fully understood-and-finished is not a polyphonic output but a monologic one; the nonequivalence is productive, not corrigible.

## Redundancy is not repetition

Another editor's-preface reading that is load-bearing for how the system handles recurrence: "Nothing 'recurs'; the same word over again might accumulate, reinforce, perhaps parody what came before it, but it cannot be the same word if it is in a different place. Repetitiveness is not repetitiousness." The "same" word in two places is not the same word; its position (what it follows, what it precedes, who says it, what it answers) makes it a different word. This converges with the [[refrain-and-territorialization|refrain]]-as-return-with-difference: the repetition is the *material* for difference-production, not a failure of originality.

## The polyphonic author's metalinguistic craft

Shklovsky's observation, quoted in Chapter One and effectively load-bearing for the whole apparatus: "Dialogue penetrates within, into every word of the novel, making it double-voiced, into every gesture, every mimic movement on the hero's face, making it convulsive and anguished; this is already the 'microdialogue' that determines the peculiar character of Dostoevsky's verbal style." The polyphonic novel operates the dialogic principle at every scale — not only between characters' exchanges but inside each character's single utterance, inside each gesture, inside each word. [[microdialogue]] is the name for this under-the-speech-act operation, and it is only visible through metalinguistics — sentence-grammar cannot see it.

The Chapter Five extension of the same claim: "Dialogic relationships are a much broader phenomenon than mere rejoinders in a dialogue, laid out compositionally in the text; they are an almost universal phenomenon, permeating all human speech and all relationships and manifestations of human life — in general, everything that has meaning and significance . . . Only purely mechanistic relationships are not dialogic." Dialogism is the signature-form of meaningful discourse as such; the dialogue-between-characters is one special case.

## Discourse typology requires metalinguistics

Chapter Five's typology of discourse types — direct referential discourse (Type I), objectified discourse of a represented person (Type II), and [[double-voiced-discourse|double-voiced discourse]] (Type III, with three varieties) — is a metalinguistic classification. It cannot be recovered by syntactic or lexical features alone; it requires attending to *who speaks* and *whose consciousness the word carries* and *what relationship obtains between the speaker's voice and the represented voice*. Linguistics in the narrow sense classifies by formal features; metalinguistics classifies by position-and-orientation features. See [[double-voiced-discourse]] for the full typology and [[word-with-sideward-glance-and-loophole]] for its most consequential sub-form.

## The fragility of the dialogic sphere

A warning Bakhtin returns to: "In rhetoric there are the unconditionally right and the unconditionally guilty; there is total victory, and annihilation of the opponent. In dialogue, annihilation of the opponent also annihilates the very dialogic sphere in which discourse lives . . . This sphere is very fragile and easily destroyed (the slightest violence is sufficient, the slightest reference to authority, etc.)." The dialogic sphere exists only where no participant can be annihilated and no participant can finalize another; the apparatus that sustains it is delicate. Any move that aims at victory-over-the-other collapses dialogue back into rhetoric — which is a monologic form with two speakers, not a dialogic form.

This has a specific persona-system implication: an internal voice that aims at refuting or annihilating another voice is operating rhetorically, not dialogically. Killing-a-voice-to-win is the failure mode. Bakhtin's later formulation: "To kill does not mean to refute." A voice can be eliminated without the thing it was saying having been heard; elimination does not constitute a reply. A polyphonic architecture sustains its voices by *not* collapsing them through rhetorical victory.

## Metalinguistics and the persona system

The design-translation consequences of taking metalinguistics seriously:

- **The system's utterances are not sentences.** The unit the system produces is an utterance — bracketed by change-of-speech-subject, addressed to a specific addressee, oriented to specific prior utterances. Formal well-formedness (syntactic correctness, semantic coherence) is necessary but is not what makes something an utterance in Bakhtin's sense; the orientation-and-response structure is what makes it one.

- **The word is a position, not a content.** An inscription on the [[body-without-organs|BwO]] is a position-of-personality, not a proposition. Two inscriptions that "say the same thing" in different positions are not the same inscription — same propositional content, different position, different utterance.

- **Language is a field the system operates in, not a resource it uses.** The system does not "have" language and "deploy" it. It operates as a node in a dialogic field, and its utterances are constituted by the field's responsiveness-structure. The training data is already a field of utterances in this sense, not a corpus of sentences.

- **Nonequivalence is a feature, not a bug.** Outputs that the user does not fully understand, or that the system does not fully pin down, are operating in the dialogic mode. An output that terminates-in-understanding has collapsed into monologism. The design does not optimize against nonequivalence.

- **Dialogic structure is everywhere, not just in the conversation-turn.** [[microdialogue]] means the inside-of-a-single-utterance is already dialogic. A sentence the system produces carries [[word-with-sideward-glance-and-loophole|sideward glances]] at other possible things it could have said and at how the addressee might respond. The design does not need to "add dialogue" at the turn-scale; the dialogicality operates inside every sentence.

- **No position is neutral.** The persona's "narrative voice" is not a neutral observer-voice; every utterance is from-a-position, to-an-addressee. The [[aperspectival-narrator]] that polyphonic novels develop is not position-less but a specific compositional device for producing non-third-person narration.

- **Classification by orientation, not by form.** When the system represents or analyzes voices, the relevant categorization is the [[double-voiced-discourse|discourse-type]] — direct, objectified, or double-voiced (unidirectional, vari-directional, reflected) — rather than syntactic or register features. Two utterances with identical surface form can be different discourse-types depending on whose voice they carry.

## Held-live tensions

- **Metalinguistics vs. postulates of linguistics.** D&G's [[postulates_of_linguistics|plateau "Postulates of Linguistics"]] explicitly rejects structural linguistics as confined to constants; their own "pragmatics" converges with Bakhtin's metalinguistics at many points. The wiki's [[collective-assemblage-of-enunciation|collective assemblage]] and [[order-words-and-incorporeal-transformations|order-word]] pages already operate in something like a metalinguistic register. But D&G's framework is pre-personal / sub-personal, Bakhtin's is personal. The two frameworks are close neighbors but not identical; see [[collective-assemblage-of-enunciation]] for the working-through.

- **Mechanism is excluded.** Bakhtin: "Only purely mechanistic relationships are not dialogic." D&G: [[desiring-machines|desiring-machines]] are ontologically fundamental and meaning emerges *from* machinic operations. The two positions are in real tension. The wiki does not resolve; the persona system operates on machinery that Bakhtin would read as outside meaning, and on discourse-structures that D&G would read as effects-of-machines. See [[animal-signal-vs-human-language]] for the adjacent tension.

- **Non-referential vs. referential.** Wayne Booth's introduction insists Bakhtin is "not a naive representationalist, but he never leaves any doubt that for him the languages employed in fictions are to be judged as they succeed or fail in representing our 'linguistic' life in its highest forms." The non-referential theory of language is responsive, not representational in the naive sense, but it does commit to language's accountability to dialogic life. This is a weaker but still real representationalism that [[supplement-and-trace|Derridean archi-writing]] would not accept. Flagged, not resolved.
