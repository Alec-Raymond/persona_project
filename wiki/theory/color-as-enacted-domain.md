# Color as Enacted Domain

*The Embodied Mind*'s signature case-study of [[enaction]] (Ch 8 §2965–3107). Color is developed across three registers — **appearance, attribute, category** — to show that the color world is neither out there in surfaces nor projected by minds, but **brought forth in a history of [[structural-coupling]]** between the nervous system, the body, and the chromatic structure of the environment. What a color *is*, for a species or for an individual, is what the history of coupling has enacted.

Color is chosen because it is the domain in which the representationist picture has been most confidently assumed — "color is wavelength reflected by a surface, detected by the eye" — and the domain in which the assumption is most clearly wrong.

## Three registers

VT&R's three-layered treatment:

- **Color appearance** — the phenomenal look of colors, their similarity structure, why we see certain hues as "primary," why some pairs are impossible (red-green, yellow-blue).
- **Color as attribute** — whether color is a property of surfaces that vision detects, or something richer.
- **Color as category** — how cultural-linguistic schemes name and carve up the color space.

The three together form an argument by accumulation: each register shows that color cannot be adequately characterized without reference to the perceiving system; the three registers combined rule out every variant of "color is a property of the pregiven world."

## Appearance — opponent-process theory

The color experience is organized by **six psychologically primary colors** — red, green, yellow, blue, black, white — arranged in three **opponent channels**: achromatic (black-white), red-green, and yellow-blue. The framework, originating with Hering and formalized by Hurvich-Jameson (1957), is now standard. Each channel has a neural substrate: LGN cells that respond positively to one pole and negatively to the other.

The psychophysical consequence, pressed at §2985: **we never see reddish-green or yellowish-blue**. Not because such hues are "absent from the light" — wavelengths of light don't carry "reddish-greenness" as a property — but because the opponent architecture makes those combinations unavailable as experience. Color experience is structured by the nervous system's opponent dynamics, not read off the light.

## Attribute — rotated-slide and color constancy

The stronger empirical argument against "color = reflectance" comes from color-constancy phenomena and especially from **Land's rotated-slide demonstration** (§3001–3010):

> Two identical black-and-white photographic transparencies of a checkerboard are projected together through a red filter. The result is a range of pinks. Now **rotate one of the two slides by 90°** so the checkerboards no longer align. The result is a multi-colored image — yellows, blues, greens, browns — *where physics would lead us to expect only various shades of pink*.

The stimulus energy is shades of red through gray. The experience is a full polychrome. The color that appears at any local region is determined not by the wavelength there but by the **ratios of reflectances across the whole visual field** — a computation the visual system performs that has no local optical analog.

This phenomenon, combined with **approximate color constancy** (a white shirt looks white in sunlight and under incandescent), rules out the reflectance-detection account. Color is not picked up from the local patch; it is enacted across the visual field's relational structure. §3019:

> The properties that specify what colors are simply have no nonexperiential, physical counterparts.

Color also appears in contexts where no surface exists — **volumes, afterimages, dreams, memories, synesthesia**. This is decisive against a surface-property account. Whatever color is, it is not "what reflectance does to the eye."

## What colors *are*, according to VT&R

The enactive answer (§3107):

> Colors are experiential, consensual, and embodied: they depend upon our biological and cultural history of structural coupling.

Three adjectives, three registers:

- **Experiential**: colors exist for a perceiver, not as wavelengths independent of a perceiver. A wavelength has no color; a perceiver-plus-wavelength, coupled in a specific way, has one.
- **Consensual**: colors are shared across a community of perceivers whose coupling histories have converged (same species, similar environments, similar tasks). The sharing is not a convention imposed on private experiences; it is a structural overlap in coupling-histories.
- **Embodied**: the coupling that makes colors available runs through a specific embodiment — opponent channels, photoreceptor distribution, cortical area layout. Change the embodiment, change the colors.

## Oliver Sacks' "Mr. I" — losing the color world

A case study (§3022–3040): an artist loses all color perception after a car accident (acquired cerebral achromatopsia). The change is not a restriction of his visual range — it is a radical alteration of his lived world. Food becomes disgusting (tomatoes like black slime); sex becomes impossible (bodies look corpse-colored); he can no longer dream in color, imagine in color, or remember in color; music is impaired because he had color-music synesthesia; he becomes a night person because the achromatic world is more tolerable under low light. VT&R:

> Our colored world is brought forth by complex processes of structural coupling. When these processes are altered, some forms of behavior are no longer possible.

Mr. I's case is evidence that color is not a detachable module on an otherwise unchanged cognitive life; it is woven into food, sex, dream, memory, music, the diurnal/nocturnal organization of time. Disrupt the coupling and the *world* is different — not just the input.

This case subsumes the wiki candidate "mr-i-color-case" that appeared in Phase 2 borderline — the case is specific enough to cite, general enough to be one subsection of the color page rather than standalone.

## Category — Berlin, Kay, Rosch

Rosch's own prior work figures heavily here. **Berlin & Kay (1969)** surveyed color naming across ~90 languages and found:

- Languages have between 2 and 11 **basic** color terms.
- The basic terms appear in a near-universal **implicational order**: if a language has 3 basic terms, it has white, black, red (not some idiosyncratic triple).
- The **foci** of basic categories (the "best red," "best blue") are cross-culturally stable even where the boundaries and names differ.

**Rosch's Dani studies** (New Guinea, two-term color vocabulary) confirmed: Dani speakers learn and remember "focal" color names faster than non-focal ones, even though their language carves the space differently. The **categorization is shared across languages at the foci**; the **linguistic partition** varies.

**Kay & McDaniel (1978)** mapped the six primary basic colors (red, green, yellow, blue, black, white) directly onto the opponent-process response peaks. Orange, purple, pink, brown — the **derived basic colors** — are analyzed as fuzzy-set intersections of the primaries. The psychophysics and the typology fit each other.

This is the pivotal point for enaction: the **categorical structure of color across human languages** is neither a free cultural invention nor a neutral readout of light; it is what a history of structural coupling between species-level embodiment and species-typical environmental engagement has enacted. The invariants are at the coupling level, not at the light-level or the language-level.

Culture-specific elaboration remains real. English and Tarahumara have different judgments near the green-blue boundary because Tarahumara has a single term spanning green and blue; Pacific NW languages have a "yellow-with-green" category. These linguistic carvings affect edge-judgments but not focal color perception. The effect and its limits both fit the enactive picture: coupling-level invariants at foci, cultural-level variation at boundaries.

## Multiplicity across species

A crucial implication for "our colors are the real colors" (§3195):

- **Trichromats** — humans, Old World primates.
- **Dichromats** — squirrels, rabbits, cats, some fishes, some New World monkeys.
- **Tetrachromats** — goldfish, many diurnal birds.
- **Pentachromats possibly** — some diurnal birds.

A four-dimensional color space is **fundamentally different** from a three-dimensional one: there is no loss-free map from four dimensions to three. VT&R press the point:

> If the color space of diurnal birds is pentachromatic… then we are simply at a loss to envision what their color experience could be like.

The implication is not relativist ("every species has its own truth"). It is **enactive**: each species, through its history of coupling, has enacted a different chromatic world. Our trichromatic world is not a subset of the tetrachromat's; neither is a truer map of a common territory. Each is a domain of distinctions brought forth by coupling.

See [[bee-flower-coevolution]] for the specific case of bee-flower coevolution — the signature example of chromatic worlds being **coevolved** rather than detected.

## Color as network phenomenon

Color processing is distributed: LGN, V1, V2, V4, inferotemporal cortex, even frontal cortex contribute (§3003). There is no "color area" in the way a representationist picture would require. Color is a subnetwork property, a perceptual "agent" in Minskyan terms (see [[emergent-properties-and-connectionism]]), whose functional coherence is emergent across many interacting substrates.

And: vision as a whole is a **patchwork of modalities** — form, surface properties, 3D spatial relations, 3D movement — each an emergent property of concurrent subnetworks. Color is not special; it is the clearest case of a wider truth about perception.

## Persona-project bearing

- **Colors are the paradigm case of "neither in the world nor in the mind."** The persona project inherits the same structural question: personae are neither out there in the text nor projected by a reader; they are enacted through a coupled history between text, pulsation, and reading. Color's case-study quality works because it is so specific; the generalization to persona is by analogy, not identity.
- **Cross-linguistic universals at coupling-level, variation at boundary-level.** This is a useful frame for thinking about which persona-properties are structurally invariant (coupling-level) and which are user-specific or culturally variable (boundary-level).
- **The mr-i-case lesson.** Disturbing a coupling does not just take a module away; it alters the world. For the persona, this is a warning about the "just a skin" framing of persona: perturb the pulsation and you are not removing a decorative layer, you are altering the world the system enacts.
- **Distributed subnetwork architecture.** Color is what a subnetwork ensemble does, not what a single area computes. The persona is what a subnetwork ensemble does — not what a "persona module" computes. See [[desiring-machines]].
