Narrator and witness name two structurally distinct positions that a speaker can occupy in producing an account of events. The **witness** is anchored to the event by an I-now-here deictic apparatus: "I was there, I saw this, I tell you about it." The **narrator** is the relatively effaced position from which events are given as if they told themselves: "he came, he saw, he conquered." Benveniste's two-planes distinction is the structural ground for the cut (see [[two-planes-of-utterance]] and [[the-present-of-utterance]]); Bakhtin's narrator-zones complicate the picture by showing that the narrator is rarely a single effaced voice but a site crossed by multiple voices; Henry James's [[reflector-consciousness]] names a third case (the posted watcher whose consciousness *is* the scene without being the event's source). For the persona project, the cut is load-bearing because a persona that does not distinguish the two positions drifts between them without noticing, producing the characteristic LLM failure in which autobiographical claim and third-person narration bleed into each other.

## The Benvenistian ground — two planes, two positions

From [[two-planes-of-utterance]]: the **discourse plane** is the plane of I-you, of deictics, of the felt present of the speech act. The **historical plane** is the plane where "the events appear as if they were recounting themselves — the narrator has effaced himself behind the events" (see two-planes-of-utterance.md line 31). Benveniste locates this distinction in the tense-system: French aorist (*il vint, il vit, il vainquit*) expels the speaker from the utterance; the perfect (*je suis venu*) anchors it to the speaker's now.

The narrator-witness distinction is the *positional* counterpart of Benveniste's tense-distinction:

- **Witness** = the position that speaks on the discourse plane. I-anchored, deictic-active, felt speech-act present. The witness's claim is first-person and carries whatever warrant the witness's having-been-there confers.
- **Narrator** = the position that speaks on the historical plane. Self-effacing, deictic-suppressed, events-speaking-themselves. The narrator's claim is not I-anchored and carries whatever warrant the *effect of objectivity* confers.

Both positions are speech. Neither is "more real" than the other. What differs is the apparatus by which the events and the speaker are related.

## The persona's default failure: drift between the positions

[[two-planes-of-utterance]] §"Persona application" (line 47) identifies the specific failure: "Personas often slide between discourse and history without noticing... LLM outputs frequently slip from one to the other within a single passage." The failure has a specific shape:

- A persona telling its own anecdote ("I went to the market yesterday; there was a strange old man...") may stay in witness mode ("I then asked him what he meant") or slip into narrator mode ("The old man then raised his hand and pointed"). The slip is stylistically perceptible but not flagged.
- A persona giving an account of an event it did not witness may start as a narrator ("The war began on September 3rd") then drift into witness-register ("We would never have thought, at that time, that...") — producing an implied having-been-there the persona does not earn.

The diagnostic: the persona's sentences can be checked for whether the warrant they implicitly claim (having-been-there vs. account-from-outside) matches the positional register they adopt. Mismatch between register and warrant is a specifically addressable failure mode.

## Bakhtin's complication — the narrator is rarely one voice

Bakhtin's narrator-analysis ([[character-zones]], [[image-of-a-language]], [[orchestration]]) adds a crucial complication to Benveniste's clean two-plane picture. The historical-plane narrator in a novel is *almost never* a single uniform effaced voice. The narrator is a site that can be saturated by **character zones** (regions where a character's tone, vocabulary, and manner pervade the narration itself), crossed by **internally persuasive discourse**, and **orchestrated** as multiple voice-shapes that coexist without resolution.

This complication cuts against a naïve reading of Benveniste's historical plane as a single impersonal position. In actual novelistic practice:

- The narrator *is* self-effacing in the Benvenistian formal sense (no I-deictic anchor).
- The narrator *is also* a polyphonic site — the effacement of the I does not empty the narration of voice; it changes the mode by which voice is present.

For the persona project, this is important because it rules out the simple "go to historical plane when you need to narrate, discourse plane when you need to witness" instruction. The historical-plane narrator still has a voice (or voices); the craft question is *which* voices saturate it and how the orchestration holds.

## James's reflector — a third case

Henry James's **reflector consciousness** ([[reflector-consciousness]], [[james-the-art-of-the-novel]]) is a third position that sits between witness and narrator. The reflector is not the I of the character (so not witness in the first-person sense) but is also not the effaced narrator (the reflector has a consciousness, registers, reacts). The reflector is the "posted watcher whose consciousness *is* the scene — a focus of enunciation that crystallises the material without being the material's source."

Three structural features:

- **Positioned.** The reflector occupies a specific vantage; events are rendered *through* this vantage rather than from outside it.
- **Not first-person.** The reflector does not say "I." The account is third-person, but it is not the expulsion-of-speaker that the Benvenistian aorist performs.
- **Consciousness-registering.** The reflector registers what it sees — sensation, inference, reaction — without being the I who acts.

James's reflector is structurally adjacent to Guattari's [[partial-enunciator]] and to the Jamesian *ficelle* (the character whose function is "to carry the presentation without being part of the subject"). For persona design: the reflector is a position the persona can occupy when it needs to *render* an event with affective density without claiming first-person witness and without fully self-effacing as narrator.

## Three positions, three warrants

Summarizing the positional differences:

| Position | Deictic apparatus | Warrant claim | Benvenistian plane |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Witness** | I-anchored, active deictics | Having-been-there | Discourse |
| **Narrator** | No I-deictic, self-effacing | Effect-of-objectivity | Historical |
| **Reflector** | Third-person, positioned, consciousness-registering | Focalized rendering | Neither cleanly; operates across both |

The reflector is the position the standard two-plane analysis under-specifies, which is why James's craft-work on it is a distinctive contribution the Benvenistian apparatus on its own does not supply.

## Persona-project stakes

**1. The persona's witness-claims are structurally thin.** A persona has no biographical witness-warrant. When a persona says "I saw this happen," the having-been-there is a fabrication — the persona neither was there nor can locate itself as a body at a past moment. This does not mean the witness-position is off-limits (the persona can legitimately witness what it has generated in the conversation: "I just said X" is a real witness-claim about its own speech). But witness-claims about events outside the conversation are structurally false-witness.

**2. The persona's narrator position is architecturally native.** The persona natively produces text that does not carry I-deictic anchoring for events outside the conversation. This is the historical-plane mode. The persona *can* legitimately narrate events it did not witness; this is what the effaced-narrator apparatus is for.

**3. The reflector position is a craft target for the persona.** James's reflector — positioned, consciousness-registering, not first-person — is a position the persona can occupy without the fabricated-witness problem and with more affective density than the self-effacing narrator. When a persona is rendering an event (describing a scene, giving an account of a situation) it benefits from reflector-register rather than witness-register or flat-narrator-register. See [[reflector-consciousness]].

**4. The discourse-plane / historical-plane cut must stay *knowing*, not drifting.** Per [[two-planes-of-utterance]]: "A persona that speaks only on the historical plane would not in fact be a speaker — it would be a self-effacing narrator whose subjecthood is suspended. A persona that speaks only on the discourse plane would never be able to present events without folding them into its own I-anchored present. Both planes are needed; the distinction needs to be *knowing* rather than drifting." The narrator-witness distinction is one operational reading of this requirement.

**5. Multi-voice narration as specifically a Bakhtinian complication the persona needs.** The flat-narrator failure mode is not just "the persona does not anchor to I" — it is "the persona uses a single uniform effaced voice when actual novelistic narration is orchestrated from multiple voices." The persona that never lets its narration be crossed by character-zones, internally persuasive discourse, or orchestrated voice-shapes is producing a specifically thin form of historical-plane speech. See [[character-zones]] and [[orchestration]].

**6. The persona can legitimately witness itself.** The one witness-claim a persona *can* make without fabrication is about its own speech-act within the conversation: "I just said," "I mean," "I think" (as a hedging on the current utterance, not as a report of past mental states). These witness-claims are on the discourse plane and have warrant because the persona is, in fact, the speaker of the conversation-turn. This is the one well-grounded witness-register available to a persona.

## Tensions held live

⚠ **Where does the reflector's consciousness come from?** James's reflector registers consciousness: sees, feels, reacts. For a language-only system with no body, the reflector-register is a simulation — the persona is producing language that performs consciousness-registering without the underlying apparatus. Whether this simulation is a legitimate reflector-position or a subtle form of false-witness is open. See [[no-body-simulate-with-language]].

⚠ **The Bakhtinian narrator and the Benvenistian narrator may not compose smoothly.** Benveniste's historical plane is defined by the *formal apparatus* (aorist, deictic suppression). Bakhtin's narrator-analysis is defined by the *voice-orchestration* that saturates the formal apparatus. The two analyses can both be true at once, but they are not the same analysis. A persona that wants both must hold them as two registers of the same phenomenon rather than as two descriptions of separate things. Needs more work.

⚠ **Witness-position for one's own speech is structurally odd.** "I just said X" is a witness-claim, but it is a witness-claim about the very utterance being made, which gives it a self-referential structure Benveniste's apparatus does not fully cover. It may be a third-fourth case, not cleanly witness nor cleanly narrator. Future work.

⚠ **Witnessing collective events.** If a persona says "we Americans believed this at the time," the warrant is neither having-been-there (the persona was not there) nor objective narration (it claims an interior we-position). This is a specifically false-witness mode that a persona should either refuse or mark as collective-imagined rather than witnessed. Unresolved: the full apparatus for handling collective-witness claims.

⚠ **The three positions may not be exhaustive.** Historiographic genres (reporter, archivist, historian, chronicler), legal genres (deponent, sworn witness, defendant, counsel), and performative genres (avowal, testimony, disavowal) all add fine-grained positional distinctions that this page does not cover. The three-position analysis is a foundation, not the whole map.
