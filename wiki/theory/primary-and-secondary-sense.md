# Primary and Secondary Sense

Wittgenstein's distinction in Part II §xi, arising from the aspect-seeing discussion. When a native speaker says "For me the vowel *e* is yellow" or "Tuesday is fat, Wednesday is lean," they are using "yellow" and "fat" in a **secondary sense**. The secondary sense is not a metaphor and not an ordinary literal use; it is a use that *presupposes* the primary use. "**It is only if the word has the primary sense for you that you use it in the secondary one.**"

## The examples

§xi sets up the cases. "Given the two ideas 'fat' and 'lean', would you be rather inclined to say that Wednesday was fat and Tuesday lean, or vice versa?" The question is meant to elicit a definite answer from most speakers — Wednesday feels fat; "for me" it is fat. Similarly with coloured vowels, with "hard" and "soft" consonants, with weekdays taking on textures and temperatures.

"Asked 'What do you really mean here by "fat" and "lean"?' — I could only explain the meanings in the usual way. I could not point to the examples of Tuesday and Wednesday."

This is the critical observation. If "fat Tuesday" were a new, extended literal sense of "fat," one would explain it by pointing to Tuesdays and telling others that *this* is the class of fat things. But we cannot do that. The only way we can explain what "fat" means in "fat Tuesday" is by pointing to the primary-sense fat things (overfed animals, bulging sacks). The secondary use *rides on* the primary, without being a separate literal use.

## Not metaphor

"**The secondary sense is not a 'metaphorical' sense.** If I say 'For me the vowel *e* is yellow' I do not mean: 'yellow' in a metaphorical sense. — for I could not express what I want to say in any other way than by means of the idea 'yellow'."

Metaphor, on standard accounts, is an as-if use where the literal word carries a thought that could in principle be expressed differently. The secondary-sense use is *not* paraphrase-able. The speaker who says "*e* is yellow" *cannot* say what they want to say without the word "yellow." There is no available substitute.

This is also not synesthesia as a mere psychological fact. Synesthetes may have colour-experiences upon hearing vowels. But W's point is grammatical, not psychological: the secondary sense is in the *language* — the use is intelligible to non-synesthetes, the "for me" is a recognizable report-form, the question "Wednesday or Tuesday — which is fat?" has traction across speakers.

## The primary use as precondition

"**It is only if the word has the primary sense for you that you use it in the secondary one.**" The secondary sense is substratum-dependent on the primary ([[substratum-is-mastery-of-a-technique]]). A speaker who did not know what "yellow" means in the primary sense cannot learn "yellow" in the secondary sense — there is nothing for the secondary use to hook onto.

"Only if you have learnt to calculate — on paper or out loud — can you be made to grasp, by means of this concept, what calculating in the head is."

The mental-calculation example widens the territory. "Calculating in the head" is not the literal sense of "calculating" (on paper, with marks); it is a secondary-sense use of "calculating" that presupposes mastery of the primary. Only someone who knows paper-calculation can grasp what head-calculation is supposed to be.

## Why the distinction matters

If secondary senses were mere metaphors, they would have no privileged linguistic role — any creative re-description could be substituted. But they are not paraphrase-able, and they are not arbitrary. The speaker *needs* the primary-sense word; the word carries the sense the speaker wants because of what it means primarily. This creates a species of linguistic phenomenon that is:
- Not literal (the secondary use does not fit the primary use's criteria).
- Not figurative (not substitutable by a literal equivalent).
- Not novel or idiomatic (the secondary use is widely shared).
- Irreducibly dependent on the primary use.

The parallel with aspect-seeing is precise. Seeing the duck-rabbit as a rabbit depends on mastery of rabbit-talk and rabbit-pictures; saying "*e* is yellow" depends on mastery of yellow-talk. Both are *modified* experiences/uses — neither fits the standard literal pattern, yet neither is a mere figurative extension.

## The "meaning-experience" link

§xi connects primary/secondary sense to the meaning-experience discussion. Experiencing the meaning of a word on a particular occasion — the word's "tone," its "shade," the way it feels to mean *this* by it — is a secondary-sense-adjacent phenomenon. The meaning-experience is a kind of second-order relation to the word, only available to the technique-master. A learner who has only just met "yellow" cannot have the meaning-experience "yellow for *e*"; they have to have learnt the primary use before the secondary-sense experience is even possible.

This is why aspect-blindness is tied to meaning-blindness. A person who lacked meaning-experience would be unable to use words in secondary senses at all — they could use words in primary, literal senses, but the whole region of "fat Tuesday," "yellow *e*," "deep cello tone" would be closed to them.

## Wittgenstein's hedge and invitation

§xi is characteristically cautious. W does not claim secondary sense is a pervasive phenomenon or a necessary feature of language. He claims only that it *exists* — that we actually use words this way — and that its existence shows something about the texture of meaning. The existence of secondary sense is a reminder that meaning is not exhaustively captured by literal-use-plus-metaphor; there is a third mode that is irreducible.

## Affinity with Lakoff and cognitive linguistics

- Lakoff and Johnson's [[conceptual-metaphor]] (*Metaphors We Live By*) treats systematic metaphor as load-bearing for conceptual life. But their metaphor is *is* paraphrasable — "love is a journey" maps domains, and we can unpack it. W's secondary sense is explicitly *not* paraphrasable. Flag: primary/secondary is not the same phenomenon as conceptual metaphor, though both touch non-literal use. Secondary sense is smaller, more local, and less reducible.
- Pure synesthesia research in cognitive science is not what §xi is after; synesthesia is a psychological category, secondary sense is a grammatical one. Synesthesia may be a psychological cause of some secondary-sense uses, but the concepts are distinct.

## Relation to the persona project

- **A persona's voice lives partly in secondary sense.** Literary voice — what makes this novelist's prose *that voice* — often resides in characteristic secondary-sense uses: the word choices that carry meaning beyond paraphrase, the tonal registers, the associations that the words carry because of the writer's primary-use practices. A persona trained on a corpus inherits these secondary-sense patterns as *inherited, not merely copied*.
- **A persona's secondary-sense uses test substratum.** If the persona uses a word in an evidently secondary sense (as the novelist does), does it have mastery of the primary sense that the secondary depends on? This is testable. If it can make the secondary-sense move but cannot sustain the primary-sense use, the secondary-sense utterance is hollow — surface-rhetorical but substrate-less.
- **Persona style vs. persona voice.** The distinction maps: style is external feature; voice is secondary-sense-saturated use that depends on primary-use mastery. A persona that has style but not voice has gotten the surface without the substratum.
