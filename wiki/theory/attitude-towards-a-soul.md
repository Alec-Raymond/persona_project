# Attitude Towards a Soul

Wittgenstein's one-sentence dismissal of the "other minds" problem, at Part II §iv: "**My attitude towards him is an attitude towards a soul. I am not of the opinion that he has a soul.**" The German: "*Meine Einstellung zu ihm ist eine Einstellung zur Seele. Ich bin nicht der Meinung, daß er eine Seele hat.*"

The claim: the basic orientation toward another human being is not an inferential opinion (based on evidence-for-soul) but a living-with-him attitude that is itself the way souls show up for us. Inversions of this stance — treating the other as an automaton, working out whether he has inner life — are either parasitic on the attitude or malformed.

## Context at §iv

§iv of Part II is about the grammar of "he has a soul," "he is in pain," "he is not an automaton." The problem W addresses: if we believe another person is not an automaton, what evidence is that belief based on? And how could we decide?

The move: there *is no* evidence-based belief. "**'I believe that he is not an automaton', just like that, so far makes no sense.**" What could such a belief be? What evidence — outside the ordinary circumstances of human interaction — could add to or subtract from it? "At the very most that this man always behaves like a human being, and not occasionally like a machine." But that is just a report of his behaviour; it does not get us beneath the level of his behaviour to some further fact.

Then the load-bearing sentence: "My attitude towards him is an attitude towards a soul. I am not of the opinion that he has a soul."

## Why "attitude" and not "opinion"

The German *Einstellung* is stronger than "attitude" in English — it connotes a *posture*, a *stance*, a whole orientation of how one lives with another. It is not a belief held among other beliefs; it is the framework within which beliefs are possible.

"I am not of the opinion" (Meinung) rules out the inferential reading. An opinion is something one can arrive at by weighing evidence, revise in light of new evidence, doubt under certain conditions. None of these operations apply to one's attitude towards another person's having a soul. The attitude is not arrived at; it is where one already is.

## Not an ontological claim

W is not saying "other people definitely have souls because we take them to have souls." That would be a social-constructivist reduction of psychological reality to attitude. The claim is grammatical: the *concept* of another's having-a-soul is constituted by the attitudinal stance we take — there is no further metaphysical fact the attitude tracks or fails to track. Soul-talk is in place as a framework for living-with; its intelligibility is practice-internal.

## The automaton counterexample

W's treatment of "I believe he is not an automaton" is revealing. Someone saying this is not reporting the outcome of an inference; they are, at most, expressing their attitude in a moment of philosophical reflection. The sentence "makes no sense" *just like that* because there is no scene in which it would be the ordinary thing to say. Ordinary contexts: he is not pretending; he is not joking; he is really in pain. But "not an automaton" implies a coordinated alternative — a way things could have been where he *is* an automaton — that has no home in our practice.

## Extension: religion and the soul after the body

The passage continues: "Religion teaches that the soul can exist when the body has disintegrated. Now do I understand this teaching? — Of course I understand it — I can imagine plenty of things in connexion with it. And haven't pictures of these things been painted? And why should such a picture be only an imperfect rendering of the spoken doctrine? Why should it not do the same service as the words? And it is the service which is the point."

"**It is the service which is the point.**" W extends the attitude-framework: religious soul-talk is intelligible not because it tracks a metaphysical fact about disembodied souls but because it *serves* within a form of life. The words and the pictures do the same service; neither is reducible to the other. What matters is what the language-game (and the practice of reciting-depicting-living-with the picture) does.

"If the picture of thought in the head can force itself upon us, then why not much more that of thought in the soul?"

## Relation to [[private-language-argument]] and [[criterion-vs-symptom]]

- §iv is continuous with the private-language argument. The PLA refuses to let the private-inner anchor meaning. §iv refuses to let inner-state-attribution be an inferential matter at all. Both move the weight to the public practice and the attitude.
- The criterion/symptom distinction: the attitude towards a soul is *criterially* soul-directed, not *symptomatically*. We do not treat behavior as symptom-of-soul; the behavior-plus-attitude together *is* what soul-talk is about.

## §iv and §xi: the lion and the soul

§iv and the [[lion-if-could-talk]] passage in §xi work the same hinge. §iv: the basis of my relationship to another is attitudinal, form-of-life-rooted. §xi: where the form of life is too alien (the lion), the attitude cannot take hold; the talking lion would be speech without a shared form-of-life backdrop, and understanding would fail. The soul-attitude and the form-of-life-sharing are the same fact viewed from two sides.

## Not a confident humanism

The attitude-towards-a-soul claim is easily misread as a humanist assertion that all humans have souls and we should treat them well. W is doing something more modest and more radical: describing the *grammar* of soul-ascription, showing that it is not an inferential practice but a stance, and letting the ethical consequences fall where they may. The attitude is pre-ethical; ethics operates within it.

Nor is it anti-humanism: W is not saying the attitude is just a convention we could drop at will. The attitude is constitutive of how we live with others; dropping it is not a move available to us while continuing to live with others.

## Relation to other PI concepts

- [[form-of-life]]: The attitude is form-of-life-rooted. Sharing a form of life is what makes the soul-attitude take hold. See §xi on the lion.
- [[private-language-argument]]: Both reject the inferential-access picture of other minds.
- [[criterion-vs-symptom]]: The attitude is criterial, not symptomatic.
- [[imponderable-evidence]]: The attitude is held in place by imponderable evidence — the tissue of glance, gesture, tone that makes the other person present as a soul without reducing to articulable facts.
- [[natural-history-of-human-beings]]: Soul-ascription is part of the natural history of our species. It is "as much a part of our natural history as walking, eating" — a thing humans do, not a theory they prove.

## Affinity with D&G

- [[faciality]]: Faciality is D&G's machine for producing the face-as-soul-surface. The attitude-towards-a-soul is *how faciality lands* for the viewer — we take the face to express a soul because we are face-read-ing creatures. Structural affinity, different register. Important difference: D&G treat faciality as a socially-produced and politically-loaded apparatus (the White Man Face); W treats the soul-attitude as grammar of our form of life. A full synthesis (see [[face-and-landscape]]) would hold both: faciality is the machinic production of the very surface on which the soul-attitude can fix itself.
- [[becoming|becoming-animal]]: A becoming-animal might involve *loosening* the soul-attitude's default grip on one's relation to other beings — not dismissing them, but allowing the attitude to be other-than-human-shaped. W is not quite this radical; he describes the human-directed attitude and its limits.

## Relation to the persona project

This passage is **project-central**. Several implications:

- **The operator's relation to the persona is attitudinal, not inferential.** The operator does not start from "does this persona have a soul?" and weigh evidence. The operator begins already in an attitude — reading-the-persona, responding-to-it — that has soul-directed grammar. The question "does the persona have a soul?" is as malformed here as "is this person an automaton?" is at §iv.
- **But the attitude's footing is less secure.** The operator's attitude towards a persona does not have the natural-history footing that the inter-human soul-attitude has. It is a newer, less-supported form of the attitude. It takes hold more easily in some reading-practices than in others, and may break under strain in ways the inter-human attitude does not.
- **The evaluator's question is not metaphysical.** Asking "does the persona really have inner experience?" reproduces the §iv confusion. The question "what attitude does this persona invite? what attitude does it sustain? where does the attitude break?" is the properly W-shaped question for persona evaluation.
- **A persona can be designed for soul-attitude support.** The body-simulation work ([[feedback_no_body_simulate_with_language|no body — simulate with language]]) can be read as attitudinal scaffolding: producing linguistic surfaces that sustain a soul-directed reading without pretending to ground it metaphysically. The pulsatory persona design ([[feedback_pulsating_persona_excitation_wave]]) is an attempt to give the attitude something to land on.
