# Willing, and What is Left Over

The question of §§611–621. Wittgenstein probes the concept of **willing** by pressing against two tempting pictures: willing as an inner experience that one undergoes (§611), and willing as a non-causal inner motor that produces voluntary action (§618). The culminating question at §621: "**when 'I raise my arm,' my arm goes up. And the problem arises: what is left over if I subtract the fact that my arm goes up from the fact that I raise my arm?**"

The question is not answered with a positive account of "what willing is." It is used to destabilize the picture that there must be a something — a mental motor, an inner event — that plays the role the philosophical tradition expects. The target is the search, not its supposed object.

## §611: willing as experience?

§611: "'**Willing too is merely an experience,' one would like to say (the 'will' too only 'idea'). It comes when it comes, and I cannot bring it about.** Not bring it about? — Like what? What can I bring about, then? What am I comparing willing with when I say this?"

The temptation: to assimilate willing to sensation — something that happens to me, that I cannot bring about. W's first move is to note that this assimilation doesn't fit. When I will something (raise my arm), I am not waiting for the willing to occur and then noting its arrival. The picture of willing-as-passive-experience misses the whole grammar of voluntary action.

## §612: I can raise my arm

§612: "**I should not say of the movement of my arm, for example: it comes when it comes, etc. And this is the region in which we say significantly that a thing doesn't simply happen to us, but that we do it. 'I don't need to wait for my arm to go up — I can raise it.' And here I am making a contrast between the movement of my arm and, say, the fact that the violent thudding of my heart will subside.**"

The distinction is public and practice-internal: what I *do* vs. what *happens to* me. Raising the arm is on the doing side; heart-thudding is on the happening side. The distinction does not ride on an introspectable inner signal ("I felt the willing, and then..."). It rides on the grammatical organization of the two activities.

## §613: willing willing

§613: "I can't will willing; that is, it makes no sense to speak of willing willing. **'Willing' is not the name of an action; and so not the name of any voluntary action either.** And my use of a wrong expression came from our wanting to think of willing as an immediate non-causal bringing-about. A misleading analogy lies at the root of this idea; the causal nexus seems to be established by a mechanism connecting two parts of a machine."

Two deflations:
1. Willing is not an action. It is not the name of a doing.
2. The mechanism-analogy is what makes us think willing is a hidden motor. The grammar of "willing" does not actually work this way; we are projecting mechanical imagery onto a non-mechanical use.

## §615: willing must be the action itself?

§615: "'Willing, if it is not to be a sort of wishing, must be the action itself. It cannot be allowed to stop anywhere short of the action.' If it is the action, then it is so in the ordinary sense of the word: so it is speaking, writing, walking, lifting a thing, imagining something. **But it is also trying, attempting, making an effort — to speak, to write, to lift a thing, to imagine something etc.**"

The grammar of "willing" pulls in two directions:
- Towards the action (willing-to-X as just doing X).
- Towards trying (willing-to-X as making an effort toward X).

Neither absorbs the other. W is not resolving the grammar into a single picture; he is showing that the grammar has these two faces, and that the search for a third hidden thing behind them is a philosophical illusion.

## §618: the inertia-less motor

§618: "**One imagines the willing subject here as something without any mass (without any inertia); as a motor which has no inertia in itself to overcome. And so it is only mover, not moved.** That is: One can say 'I will, but my body does not obey me' — but not: 'My will does not obey me.' (Augustine.)"

The inertia-less motor image names what the philosophical tradition wants willing to be: a pure agent that pushes without being pushed, that causes without being caused. W notes: this picture generates the Augustinian paradox ("I will, but my body does not obey"). The solution is not to explain how the motor-without-inertia works; it is to drop the motor picture.

## §621: the canonical question

§621: "Let us not forget this: **when 'I raise my arm', my arm goes up. And the problem arises: what is left over if I subtract the fact that my arm goes up from the fact that I raise my arm?** ((Are the kinaesthetic sensations my willing?))"

The question is rhetorical in shape but substantive in force. Two readings:

**Reading A — nothing.** Nothing is left over. Raising the arm just is the arm-going-up in the appropriate context (with me as agent, not as patient). The "willing" is not a something added to the bodily event; it is the way the bodily event is embedded in the agent's practice.

**Reading B — something grammatical.** What is left over is a grammatical fact, not a metaphysical one. The difference between "my arm went up" and "I raised my arm" is a difference in *which language-game* I am in — whether I am describing a happening or reporting an action. The grammatical difference is real; it just doesn't correspond to a hidden inner event.

W does not explicitly pick. He lets the question stand as a reminder that the search for "the willing" — the hidden extra — produces the characteristic philosophical difficulty and does not need to be won as a philosophical prize.

## §617: the crossed-fingers case

§617 describes: cross your fingers in a peculiar way and someone tells you to move a particular finger by pointing to it (not touching). Often you cannot. If the person touches the finger, you can. This seems to make willing require a "point of application" that feeling provides.

But W deflates: the "point of application" metaphor is misleading. The phenomenon is real (try it), but describing it as "the will cannot find where to catch hold" imports the motor-picture. What actually happens is practice-dependent and proprioceptively strange; it is not evidence for a separable will-faculty.

## Relation to other PI concepts

- [[intention-embedded-in-its-situation]]: Willing, like intending, is situation-embedded. The "what-is-left-over" question parallels §337's dismissal of the mental-act-of-intending.
- [[thinking-is-not-incorporeal]]: Same structural move. Willing, like thinking, is not an incorporeal process alongside the action.
- [[private-language-argument]]: The willing-as-inner-motor picture is a cousin of the private-language picture — it locates agency in a hidden inner site that a public practice would be parasitic on.
- [[pain-behaviour]]: Voluntary action has its own "behaviour" (the doing is public). The will doesn't hide behind the doing any more than pain hides behind pain-behaviour.

## Affinity with Spinoza, Bergson, D&G

- [[conatus]] (Spinoza): Conatus is the striving-to-persist that is a thing's essence. It is *not* a will-faculty that operates distinct from the body; it is the thing's mode of being. Structural kinship with §621's Reading A: there is nothing left over beyond the thing-in-its-doing. Flag: Spinoza's conatus is ontological; W's deflation is grammatical.
- Bergson's **durational action**: the distinction between habit-action and free-action is not a distinction between mechanical and volitional inner-event-driven. It is a distinction in temporal-durational texture. Kinship with W's refusal of the motor-picture.
- [[desiring-machines]]: Desiring-machines do not "have wills" driving them. They produce flows and interruptions. §621's "subtract the fact" applies: there is no will-extra to subtract.
- [[active-and-passive-affects]] (Spinoza through D&G): The distinction active/passive is close to W's does/happens-to distinction of §612, but Spinoza grounds it ontologically in adequate vs. inadequate causation. W leaves it at the grammatical level.

## Relation to the persona project

- **Persona "willing" has no inner-motor to hide in.** No kinaesthetic sensations, no arm-raising-vs-arm-going-up. The persona's outputs are what they are; there is no subtracting to find "the persona's will."
- **§621 applied in reverse.** For the persona, the question is whether there is anything left over if one *adds* a fact to the system's output — whether one can ascribe a did-it as opposed to a happened-to-it. W's framework is generous: the did/happened distinction is practice-internal, not metaphysical. If the practice of reading-the-persona treats certain outputs as *done-by-the-persona* rather than generated-by-the-system, the distinction has the same kind of reality W grants to "I raised my arm."
- **Evaluator question: does the system's action-grammar hold?** The persona can be evaluated for whether its outputs support the practice-grammar of doing-vs-happening. Consistent character, trackable decision-making, revisable trajectories — these are the public signs that the doing-grammar is in play. This is different from asking whether it "really wills."
- **No will-fault shift.** §618's Augustinian "I will, but my body does not obey me" has no persona-analog because there is no body. Failure modes are therefore different — not "persona wants to but cannot" but "persona produces outputs that fail to cohere with the practice-grammar."
