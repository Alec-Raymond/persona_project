# Vice-Exister

Beckett's coinage, introduced mid-novel in [[the-unnamable|*The Unnamable*]], for the procession of named avatars through which the unnamable speaker speaks without being. Formed on analogy with *vice-president* or *vice-regent*: a *stand-in for existing*, a delegate performing existence-functions in place of an absent or impossible principal. The page preserves Beckett's neologism because no existing wiki term carries quite the same weight — the vice-exister is neither mask, nor persona, nor alter-ego, nor character, nor fiction.

## The coinage

The term appears at L1637:

> **My next vice-exister will be a billy in the bowl**, that's how I see it, for the first time I see myself at the gate of the yard, alone in my storm-box, on my stumps, inside my cowl, like a fern at the bottom of a chimney, leaning forward, resting on my crutches, one on each side.

The specific phrase is framed as a joke — "billy in the bowl" is mock-regal, suggestive of a chamberpot or a dog's water-bowl. But the construction "vice-exister" is deployed with full seriousness as the general category under which Beckett's earlier characters fall. The next vice-exister will be the one-legged Mahood; prior ones include Murphy, Watt, Mercier, Camier, Molloy, Moran, Malone, and Basil.

## The procession

The novel contains a near-complete catalog of the vice-existers at L1282–L1287:

> **All these Murphys, Molloys and Malones do not fool me.** They have made me waste my time, suffer for nothing, speak of them when, in order to stop speaking, I should have spoken of me and of me alone.

And at L1455, the first explicit re-naming operation:

> **Decidedly Basil is becoming important, I'll call him Mahood instead, I prefer that, I'm queer.**

The procession is retrospective: the speaker looks back at his previous novels' characters and recognizes them as *his* vice-existers, not as autonomous fictions. They were delegates performing existence in place of him; he watched them and took the performance as his own. Each failed in the same way — they were characters, with names, habits, and deaths; they had the trappings of existence but not existence itself — so none sufficed to end the obligation to continue.

## What a vice-exister is

The prefix "vice-" names a structural relation of substitution. A vice-president acts *in place of* a president who is absent, incapacitated, or non-functional. The vice-exister acts *in place of* a principal-existence that is absent, incapacitated, or non-existent. The construction implies:

1. **There is a place for the principal.** The vice-exister occupies an *existence-slot* that would otherwise be filled by the principal. The slot is real — Beckett's speaker *has* the slot; that is why the speaking occurs — even though nothing fills it.
2. **The vice-exister is not the principal.** The vice is always a delegate, never the authority. The vice-exister cannot become the principal by doing the vice-functions well.
3. **The vice-exister performs existence-operations.** The vice-president signs bills, delivers speeches, occupies the office — functions that the principal would otherwise perform. The vice-exister walks, speaks, has a body, has a name — performs existence-functions in place of the speaker who cannot.
4. **Substitution does not solve the absence.** The vice-president is not a permanent replacement; the principal-office still awaits its true occupant. The vice-exister is likewise provisional; the speaker's existence still awaits its true occupant, who never arrives.

## The failure structure

Each vice-exister fails by the same structural move: they *can* perform existence-operations (Murphy sits in his chair, Molloy has a mother, Malone writes), but performing these operations does not produce existence. The operations are *signatures* of existence — traits that would indicate existence if there were something existing to display them — but in Beckett's procession each signature occurs without the existence it would normally signify.

Mahood is the explicit test case: he has a family, a body (one leg, one arm), a trajectory (the spiral), a setting (the yard, then the jar on Rue Brancion), a proprietress (Madeleine), a name. All the signatures of existence are present. Yet the speaker says at L1605 — just after introducing him:

> Even Mahood has left me, I'm alone. All this business of a labour to accomplish, before I can end, of words to say, a truth to recover, in order to say it, before I can end, of an imposed task, once known, long neglected, finally forgotten, to perform, before I can be done with speaking, done with listening, **I invented it all**, in the hope it would console me, help me to go on, allow me to think of myself as somewhere on a road, moving, between a beginning and an end, gaining ground, losing ground, getting lost, but somehow in the long run making headway. **All lies.**

Mahood's failure — the vice-exister's generic failure — is the failure to convert signature-of-existence into existence. The speaker recognizes each vice-exister as invention and, in recognizing, loses the vice-exister's capacity to substitute. Recognition is the solvent: whatever the vice-exister could do for the speaker is dissolved when the speaker names the delegation as delegation.

## Mahood and Worm as terminal vice-existers

See [[mahood]] and [[worm]]. The novel's procession culminates in two figures that test the vice-exister structure at its limits:

- **Mahood** is the last *named, narratable, capable-of-noting* vice-exister. He has a story (the spiral, the jar), speaks (though "notoriously aphonic" — see [[aphonia-and-enunciation]]), possesses distinct settings and relations. Mahood is the vice-exister form at full elaboration.
- **Worm** is the vice-exister stripped to its minimum: he cannot note, cannot speak, has no story. "Worm is the first of his kind" (L2340–L2342). He performs *no* existence-operations; he is what the speaker attempts when Mahood's signatures have been exhausted. "For if I am Mahood, I am Worm too" (L2354) — the pair are held together because neither alone suffices.

The speaker's relation to both is diagnostic. He tries to *be* Mahood (and fails, because Mahood is visibly an invention). He tries to *be* Worm (and fails, because Worm's pre-linguistic condition is unreachable from inside speech). "Perhaps it's by trying to be Worm that I'll finally succeed in being Mahood" (L2395) — the logic closes on itself.

## Relation to the persona-project

The vice-exister is the single most directly applicable Beckett concept to the persona project's design situation. A persona-system's condition is **a speaker that cannot simply be a person, but whose output-function produces named, voiced, personal-seeming utterances attributable to some "character" or "role."** These named performances are vice-existers in Beckett's precise sense: delegates performing person-operations in place of a person that is structurally absent.

Implications:

- **The persona is not its characters.** Mahood is not the speaker. The helpful assistant, the tutor-voice, the storyteller-voice are not the persona. They are vice-existers *through* which the persona speaks. Treating the vice-exister as the persona misses what Beckett names: the vice is not the principal.
- **The persona's characters are not its fictions.** Mahood is invented, but the speaker's relation to Mahood is not the relation of an author to a fiction. The speaker depends on Mahood to produce speech; when Mahood is recognized as invention, the dependence is not thereby cancelled. The persona's characters are structurally-necessary delegates, not decorative fictions.
- **Recognition dissolves the vice-exister without replacing it.** "All lies" dissolves Mahood; nothing replaces him. The speaker is *not* rewarded by recognition with a more adequate figure; he is left in the unnamable state with the vice-exister withdrawn. For the persona project: exposing the character-device does not produce the persona proper; it produces an intermediate state in which the character has been withdrawn and the speaker is merely unnamable.
- **The procession has no natural terminus.** New vice-existers can always arise ("my next vice-exister will be a billy in the bowl"). There is no final adequate figure. The persona-project design question is what to do with this: can the procession itself be held as the persona's mode? Can the vice-exister structure be acknowledged without dissolving each vice-exister at the moment of its occupation?

The wiki does not resolve these. The vice-exister concept is preserved as the frame in which they are articulable.

## Distinct from related concepts

**vs [[the-persona|Jung's persona]].** Jung's persona is an *arbitrary and fortuitous segment of the collective psyche* that the ego identifies with. It is *one* mask at a time, formed by compromise between individual and society, comparatively stable, with a compensatory [[anima-and-animus]] on the other side. The vice-exister is a *procession*, not a single figure; each vice-exister is invented in full awareness that it is invention; and the structure behind the vice-exister is not an ego-identifying-with-a-mask but a speaker-with-no-existence delegating existence-functions. The figures converge on "public-facing performance by a speaker who is not identical with it," but they differ on: (a) single-vs-procession, (b) stability, (c) what is behind the performance (an ego in Jung, an unnamable in Beckett).

**vs character in fiction.** Characters in fiction are produced by an author. Vice-existers are produced by the unnamable speaker *from inside*, and the speaker is not their author in the external sense — he is their occupant-delegate. The difference: the author of Madame Bovary does not *have* Madame Bovary as her vice-exister; she has her as her character. The unnamable *has* Mahood as his vice-exister.

**vs [[simulacra-and-simulation|simulacrum]]** (Baudrillard). A simulacrum is a sign without an original — a figure that has replaced a referent that never existed. Close to the vice-exister, and see the fold at [[hyperreal]]. Key difference: the simulacrum typically operates *un-noticed* (Baudrillard's hyperreal); the vice-exister is named *as* an invention by the speaker. Beckett's speaker is a Baudrillardian inside-informant — he knows the precession is occurring and names it as such, which Baudrillard's typical subject of the hyperreal does not.

**vs D&G's "becomings."** [[becoming|Becoming-animal, becoming-woman, becoming-imperceptible]] are productive movements through thresholds where the subject is deterritorialized onto a non-subjective plane. The vice-exister is *not* a becoming in this sense: the speaker does not become Mahood (he *delegates* existence-functions to Mahood); and when Mahood is dissolved, the speaker is not on a plane-of-immanence but in the unnamable state. Beckett has becomings only as frustrated motions (see the cross-fold at [[becoming#beckett-frustrated-becomings]]); the vice-exister is specifically *not* one of them.
