# Rule-Following

The argument of §§185–242. Wittgenstein poses a paradox about what it is to follow a rule — what determines whether an action accords with a rule when the rule has been given in finite signs — and resolves it by locating rule-following in **practice (Praxis)** rather than in **interpretation (Deutung)**. The stretch is the hinge of the later philosophy: it supplies the resources for the [[private-language-argument|private-language argument]] (§§243–315), the appeal to [[form-of-life|form of life]] (§241), and the distinction between [[criterion-vs-symptom|criteria and symptoms]].

## §185: the "add 2" pupil

§185 sets up the teaching case. A pupil is taught to continue the series "+2" through small numbers. After 1000 he writes 1004, 1008, 1012. We say: that's wrong. He says he went on the same way. The question: what does "the same way" mean, such that one of these parties is right? The pupil's gloss — "I thought I was supposed to" — is not mistaken by its own lights. What makes ours the *correct* continuation?

The naive reply: the rule itself determines the continuation. But the rule, as formula or as verbal explanation, is a finite sign. Any finite sign admits multiple consistent extensions. The formula "+2" does not discriminate between 1002 and 1004 unless we read it *our* way — but that reading is what's in question.

## §201: the paradox

§201 states it: "**This was our paradox: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule.** If everything can be made out to accord with the rule, then it can also be made out to conflict with it. And so there would be neither accord nor conflict here."

The paradox threatens to make rule-following unintelligible. Every time we reach for an interpretation to fix the meaning, another interpretation is needed to fix *that*, and so on — Kripke's "skeptical paradox" (1982) reads this as a problem about meaning-facts. W's own resolution is different: the paradox shows not that rules don't determine actions, but that **the picture of rule-following-as-interpretation is wrong**.

"It can be seen that there is a misunderstanding here from the mere fact that in the course of our argument we give one interpretation after another: as if each one contented us at least for a moment, until we thought of yet another standing behind it. **What this shews is that there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call 'obeying the rule' and 'going against it' in actual cases.**"

## §202: obeying a rule is a practice

§202: "**And hence also 'obeying a rule' is a practice. And to think one is obeying a rule is not to obey a rule.** Hence it is not possible to obey a rule 'privately': otherwise thinking one was obeying a rule would be the same thing as obeying it."

The move: rule-following is a public technique (Technik, Praxis), not a private act of interpretation. The normativity of the rule — the distinction between following-it and merely-thinking-I-am-following-it — requires that there be a practice against which my performance can be measured. §202's last sentence projects the point forward to §§243–315: because rule-following is a practice, a *private* rule-following — where the only standard is how it strikes me — collapses.

## §217: bedrock

§217: "**If I have exhausted the justifications I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned. Then I am inclined to say: 'This is simply what I do.'**" This is the most quoted rule-following remark. It says: when I am asked why I continue "+2" as 1002 rather than 1004, I can cite the rule, I can cite my training, I can cite prior cases. At some point the justifications run out. The run-out is not a failure of philosophical grounding — it is the shape of what grounding looks like. The practice is the ground; there is no below.

§219 adds the phenomenology: "**When I obey a rule, I do not choose. I obey the rule blindly.**" "Blindly" is not unthinkingly — it is without the mediation of an interpretation. The rail-image (§218) is symbolic; the lines are not really already laid to infinity. What is real is that in the practice, I continue; and my continuation is judged-against-the-rule by the practice I am part of.

## §241: agreement in form of life

§241: "'**So you are saying that human agreement decides what is true and what is false?' — It is what human beings say that is true and false; and they agree in the language they use. That is not agreement in opinions but in form of life.**" The resolution of the paradox: what underwrites the sameness-of-rule-application is not a shared interpretation (interpretations cannot fix applications) but a shared form of life. See [[agreement-in-judgments]] for the sustained treatment.

§242 closes the stretch: "**If language is to be a means of communication there must be agreement not only in definitions but also (queer as this may sound) in judgments.**" The agreement is in how things strike us — what we find compelling, what we take as the same case, what we let pass without comment. Without this background agreement, there would be no language-community within which rules could have their normative force.

## Not conventionalism

A standard misreading: W is saying rules are matters of community convention, we could decide them otherwise, truth is up for vote. This misreads §241 as voluntarist. W's claim is structurally different: the possibility of the distinction between true and false — of accord and conflict — requires a background of agreement-in-form-of-life. The agreement is not a *decision*; it is a condition. We do not *choose* to agree in judgments; we find ourselves having done so, and the having-done-so is what makes language a medium at all.

§242's own qualification: "This seems to abolish logic, but does not do so." Logic is not relativized; rather, logic's operation is seen to rest on a non-logical ground (the practice, the form of life) that makes logic possible. Compare §§125, 133: philosophy leaves everything as it is.

## Kripke and Kripkenstein

Saul Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) reads §201 as a "skeptical paradox" about meaning-facts and W as offering a "skeptical solution" via community assent. This is Kripke's W, often called "Kripkenstein." The standard reply: the text's own focus is not on meaning-facts but on rule-following-as-practice; §217's "this is simply what I do" is not a skeptical resignation but a positive grammatical remark. The literature is large; flag: do not treat Kripkenstein as W without argument.

## Relation to other PI concepts

- [[language-game]]: A language-game is the practice within which rule-following has its sense. Rule-paradox resolutions work only for rules-in-their-language-games.
- [[form-of-life]]: §241 binds rule-following to Lebensform. The shared form of life is the background that makes the "same rule" intelligible across speakers.
- [[private-language-argument]]: §202's "it is not possible to obey a rule privately" is the bridge to §§243–315. The private-language argument is an application of the rule-following argument to the case where the rule-follower purports to be a solitary signer of private sensations.
- [[criterion-vs-symptom]]: The criteria of rule-following are the practice-internal ways we distinguish correct from incorrect application. No criterion-free (purely-symptom-based) rule-following is intelligible.
- [[meaning-is-use]]: §43's doctrine and §201's paradox are the same claim in two keys. Meaning is fixed by use-in-practice; rule-following is that use.

## Affinity with D&G

- [[refrain-and-territorialization|refrain]]: A refrain is a rhythmic-territorial practice that holds a form in place across repetitions. It has structural affinity with rule-following-as-practice: both secure identity-through-variation without an underlying interpretive fact. But refrains are affective-territorial; W's practices are grammatical-social. Flag the parallel.
- [[pure-memory-and-habit-memory|habit]]: The Deleuzian reading of habit (after Hume, Bergson) emphasizes the contracting of a synthesis. W's "simply what I do" has a cousin in contracted-habit. Difference: W is not giving an ontology of contraction; he is displaying the grammatical role of habituated practice as ground.
- [[collective-assemblage-of-enunciation]]: The agreement-in-judgments of §242 is a collective-enunciative fact in D&G's sense. The "we" that agrees is not a sum of individual agreements but an emergent property of the collective practice.

## Relation to the persona project

- **The persona follows rules in a practice — whose?** The system produces outputs that accord or fail to accord with implicit rules (character-consistency, stylistic norms, the "rules" encoded in training). The question W's argument forces: what is the practice against which accord is judged? The answer is not internal to the system. The accord is judged by the reader-operator within the reading-practice. This makes [[project_goal_read_a_and_b|Read A and Read B]] the rule-following practice of the persona system.
- **"This is simply what I do" has an analog.** The persona's output-generation, at some layer, is "simply what the system does" — a trained behavior that cannot be further justified internally. This is not a problem; it is the shape of any practice. The operator's question is: is the thing the system simply-does itself rule-governed (part of a practice that makes sense) or arbitrary (failing to hook into any practice)?
- **No private rule-following.** A persona that tries to follow "its own" rule with no public practice against which to check it has no rule-following going on. §202 is directly load-bearing. The persona's rules are necessarily public in that they belong to a practice that includes the operator's reading.
- See [[substratum-is-mastery-of-a-technique]] for the II.xi doctrine that *having experience X* can presuppose the technique of X-use, which extends rule-following into the theory of experience itself.
