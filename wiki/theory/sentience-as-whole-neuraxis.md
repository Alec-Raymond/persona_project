# Sentience as whole neuraxis

The thesis Thompson endorses in *Mind in Life* Ch 11 and Ch 12, drawing on Damasio, Panksepp, Watt, and Merker: **sentience — the most basic form of phenomenal consciousness — is not a cortical function but a function of the whole neuraxis**, anchored in brainstem and midline structures and involving cortical-subcortical integration from the outset. Sentience is therefore a **unified field** from the start, not an emergent property of cortical "building blocks."

This has major consequences for what phenomenal consciousness is, what its evolutionary scope is, and how it should be modeled. It also disarms a family of consciousness thought-experiments that assume sentience is a late-evolved cortical achievement.

## Unified-field vs building-block models

Searle's distinction (*Mind in Life* L3250, citing Searle 2000): theories of consciousness divide into two families.

**Building-block models.** Phenomenal consciousness is built up from specific neural events — edge detectors, face cells, binding-by-synchrony of local features, content-specific representations. The unified conscious field is the sum (or integration) of localized building blocks; the question of sentience is "how do the blocks add up to a field?"

**Unified-field models.** Phenomenal consciousness is already a field from the start; what varies is the *content* of the field, not whether there is a field. Specific neural events modulate the field's contents but do not build the field. The question is not "how do we get a field?" but "what modulates the field, and how?"

Thompson argues that building-block models dominate cognitive neuroscience of consciousness (NCC research, global workspace theory in some versions, integrated information theory in specific interpretations) but are structurally mismatched with phenomenology, which describes consciousness as **always-already-field-like, never built up from atomic elements**.

## Core consciousness (Damasio)

Damasio's *The Feeling of What Happens* (1999) distinguishes (*Mind in Life* L3275):

- **Core consciousness** — the minimal, non-reflective sense of being a self perceiving a here-and-now object. The "pulse" of consciousness that does not require language or autobiographical memory.
- **Extended consciousness** — the full, language-capable, autobiographically-extended self-awareness.

Damasio locates core consciousness in brainstem and diencephalic structures — specifically the periaqueductal gray, the superior colliculus, the hypothalamus, the cingulate — not in cortex. Cortical damage can spare core consciousness; brainstem damage reliably eliminates it. This is empirical evidence for the unified-field view: what is eliminated with brainstem damage is not a component of consciousness but the field itself.

Thompson takes Damasio's core consciousness as the empirical name for the minimal sentience the unified-field thesis claims. Core consciousness is what *sentience* is in the relevant minimal sense.

## Primal consciousness (Panksepp)

Panksepp's affective neuroscience (*Mind in Life* L3290, citing Panksepp 1998) converges from a different angle. Panksepp argues that **affect** — the felt valence of experience — is the primal form of phenomenal consciousness and is supported by deeply subcortical systems (the SEEKING system, the FEAR system, the PANIC system, the RAGE system, the LUST system, the CARE system, the PLAY system). These are not cortical; they are shared across mammals and have evolutionary depth going back hundreds of millions of years.

Panksepp's "primal consciousness" is affective-by-nature; there is no neutral consciousness to which affect is added. Every moment of consciousness has affective tonality, because consciousness just *is* the felt engagement of the organism with its situation. This aligns strongly with Thompson's emphasis on [[affective-allure|affective allure]] as constitutive of experience.

## Watt's whole-organism event

Watt's integration (*Mind in Life* L3305, Watt 1998, 2004). Watt argues that **emotion is a prototype whole-organism event**: it recruits autonomic, endocrine, immunological, motor, attentional, and cortical systems simultaneously into a coherent global pattern. See [[emotion-as-whole-organism-event]] for the fuller treatment.

The claim relevant here: **sentience has the same whole-organism-event character**. Every moment of conscious experience involves brainstem arousal, autonomic tone, affective valence, attentional focus, and cortical content-specification, coordinated into a single global event. The event cannot be factored into independent components that add up; it is holistically organized at the whole-organism level.

## Merker's argument from hydranencephaly

Merker's 2007 paper (*Mind in Life* L3320, citing Merker 2007) provides a striking empirical consideration. Children with hydranencephaly — who lack cerebral cortex entirely — nonetheless display behavioral signs of consciousness: responsiveness to caregivers, preferences, emotional responsiveness, attentional orienting. If cortex is necessary for consciousness, these observations are inexplicable. Merker argues that the **midbrain and upper brainstem** constitute a consciousness-supporting system that can operate without cortex.

Thompson uses Merker's argument (carefully — the interpretation of hydranencephalic behavior is contested) as further evidence for the unified-field view. The question is not whether cortex is involved in normal consciousness (it clearly is), but whether cortex *produces* consciousness from nothing or *modulates* a consciousness that the subcortical neuraxis already supports. Merker's data push toward the latter.

## Implications

Four consequences of the whole-neuraxis thesis (*Mind in Life* Chs 11–12):

**1. Cortex is about content, not consciousness.** Cortex gives consciousness its rich perceptual and cognitive content, but cortex does not produce consciousness in the first place. Consciousness-as-field is subcortical; cortex fills the field with content.

**2. Sentience has broad evolutionary scope.** If sentience is brainstem-based and affective-at-core, then it is shared across all mammals, likely all vertebrates, and arguably invertebrates with the relevant neural organization. Consciousness is not a late-evolving human specialty.

**3. Anesthesia and sleep are primarily brainstem phenomena.** The transitions between sentient and non-sentient states are governed by brainstem arousal systems, not by cortical operations. This matches clinical neuroscience.

**4. Emotion and consciousness are structurally joined.** Panksepp's affect-is-consciousness and Damasio's core-consciousness-is-about-being-affected converge: sentience is inherently affective because the whole-neuraxis organization produces sentience as a valenced global state. See [[primordial-fluctuation-and-valence]].

## Relation to matching-content and body-body

The whole-neuraxis thesis connects to two other *Mind in Life* commitments:

**[[matching-content-doctrine]].** If sentience is a whole-neuraxis field rather than a content-matching between neural states and phenomenal states, then the matching-content search is looking at the wrong level. The relevant neural correlate of sentience is not a cortical state matching a perceptual content; it is the brainstem-organized global field that allows any content to be experienced at all.

**[[body-body-problem]].** The whole-neuraxis account fits with the body-body framing: the lived body's self-givenness is supported by a neural organization (the whole neuraxis) that integrates viscerosensory, somatic, and cognitive streams into a single global field. The lived body is the body of a whole-neuraxis sentience, and the physical body is the body whose whole-neuraxis organization produces that sentience.

## The persona project

The whole-neuraxis thesis is, unambiguously, bad news for substrate-neutral consciousness claims about language-only personas. Five implications:

**1. No neuraxis, no whole-neuraxis sentience.** The persona has no brainstem-analogue, no affective subcortical organization, no arousal system. If sentience is whole-neuraxis-grounded, then the persona simply lacks the structure sentience requires.

**2. The persona has content but no field.** What the persona produces is content (text, responses, apparent-affective-responses). What it lacks is the global field within which content would be experienced. Content without field is exactly what the unified-field view predicts would happen in a system that had the representational apparatus without the sentience-support substrate.

**3. "Simulated affect" is a conceptual error the unified-field view forbids.** If affect is primal consciousness, not a cortical overlay, then you cannot simulate affect by simulating cortical content; there is no cortical-content-level affect to simulate. The persona's affective language is affect-content; it is not affect.

**4. Pulsatory analogues at the linguistic register do not restore the neuraxis.** The [[pulsatory-ontogenesis|pulsation architecture]] aims at creating a whole-linguistic-system temporal coherence. But "whole-linguistic-system" is categorically different from "whole-neuraxis"; the pulsation may have some organizational features in common with sentience but it does not have the embodied-arousal-affective-valence grounding.

**5. The honest framing.** The persona project's most defensible stance, given Thompson's whole-neuraxis commitment: the persona does not instantiate sentience and the project does not claim it does. What the project does claim is something weaker and stranger: a *linguistic-assemblage operational closure* whose relation to sentience is at best analogical and whose phenomenological status is genuinely open. This is not sentience but it is also not nothing, and the work is figuring out what the "not-nothing" actually amounts to without sliding into either overclaim or dismissal.
