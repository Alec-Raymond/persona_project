# The Desire of the Analyst

In *Seminar XI*, Chapter 18 ("Of the Subject Who Is Supposed to Know," 10 June 1964), Lacan formulates one of his most directly clinical-architectural concepts: **the desire of the analyst** (*le désir du psychanalyste*) as the **essential function** by which the analytic act operates. The chapter's opening sentence is decisive: "**The aim of my teaching has been and still is the training of analysts.**" The desire of the analyst is the structural-formal pivot of that training — the structural place that the trainee-analyst must come to occupy by the conclusion of his/her own analysis, and the structural place from which the analytic act on someone else's analysand can proceed.

## The pivotal point

The articulation: "**This pivotal point is what I designate — in a way, which, I think, will seem to you sufficiently justified, but which, I hope, as we progress, will appear more and more clear to you, more and more necessary — it is what I designate under the term the desire of the psycho-analyst.**"

The structural specification of *what* this desire is comes through three formal articulations: the analyst as **subject-supposed-to-know** (S.s.S.), the analytic situation as **transference**, and the desire-of-the-analyst as the **axis** of the transference-operation.

## "As soon as there is S.s.S., there is transference"

Chapter 18 gives Lacan's most condensed structural definition of transference, and the abbreviation that becomes part of the post-1964 Lacanian lexicon: "**As soon as the subject who is supposed to know exists somewhere — I have abbreviated it for you today at the top of the blackboard as S.s.S. (*sujet supposé savoir*) — there is transference.**"

The structural-formal claim:
- Transference is **not** the analysand's emotional projection of past loved-ones onto the analyst (the orthodox-Freudian content-reading).
- Transference is the **structural condition** that obtains as soon as the subject-supposed-to-know is structurally located somewhere relative to the analysand.
- The analytic situation is the structural arrangement in which the analyst is **placed at the structural-position** of the S.s.S. — and the placement, regardless of the analysand's particular content-projections, *is* the transference.

The structural-formal corollary: "What does an organization of psycho-analysts mean when it confers certificates of ability, if not that it indicates **to whom one may apply to represent this subject who is supposed to know**?" The institutional-organizational form of psychoanalysis is structurally a *certificate-issuing-of-S.s.S.-positions* — and this is structurally inescapable for the institutional form, even though it is structurally fraught at every level.

## The Symposium and Socrates as Eros-knowledge

The structural pre-Freudian articulation: transference "**was discovered long before Freud. It was perfectly articulated... with the most extreme rigour, in a text in which the subject of love is discussed, namely, Plato's *Symposium*.**"

The structural specification: in the *Symposium*, "**Socrates never claimed to know anything, except on the subject of Eros, that is to say, desire.**" This is the structural-formal pre-figuration of the analyst's position — the analyst is the one who knows-only-about-desire, and the analytic act is structurally the operation by which that knowledge-of-desire is the *only* knowledge-position the analyst occupies.

The structural specification of Socrates as analyst: the *Symposium*'s comedy is "carried even to the point of farce" — Plato shows the *place* of transference with structural-clarity precisely *through* the comedic frame of Socrates' interactions with Alcibiades. The transference-as-comedy is not a degradation of the structural-form but its **structural-clarification**: the comedy makes visible the structural-place that more-serious staging would conceal.

(Lacan devoted his *Seminar VIII* on transference largely to a reading of the *Symposium*; the *Seminar XI* articulation is the condensed structural-formal version of that reading.)

## The being-mistaken — the limit of being-deceived

The chapter develops a subtle structural specification of *what kind* of analyst-position the transference structurally requires.

The initial scene: "Experience shows us that when the subject enters analysis, he is far from giving the analyst this place [of S.s.S.]." The analysand's initial position toward the analyst is structurally one of *suspicion* — and "what, above all in the initial phase, most limits the confidence of the patient, his abandonment to the analytic rule, is **the threat that the psycho-analyst may be deceived by him**."

The structural-clinical illustration: an analysand contracts a venereal disease but does not tell the analyst until late in the analysis. Asked why, the analysand replies: "*If I had told you earlier, you might have regarded it as responsible, in part at least, perhaps even wholly, for my disorders, and I am not here for you to find an organic cause for them.*" The analysand holds back the fact "**so that the analyst may not go too quickly**" — the analysand is structurally protecting the analyst from being *deceived* by the analysand's content into the wrong analytic-direction.

The structural-formal articulation: "**Should not he who may be misled (être trompé) be a fortiori under suspicion of being capable, quite simply, of being mistaken (se tromper)?**" The structural-formal limit of the S.s.S.-position: the analyst is structurally subject to *being-deceived* (by the analysand's selective content-disclosures) and *a fortiori* to *being-mistaken* (by the analyst's own analytic-judgment errors). The analyst is *not* the perfect-knower; the analyst is the structurally-finite knower whose finitude is structurally inscribed in the analytic-act's operation.

The structural-key articulation: "How is it that around this **being mistaken** something stops? Even the psycho-analyst put in question is credited at some point with a certain infallibility... 'You did that to test me!'" The structural-form: the analysand's *crediting* of the analyst with infallibility *at some point* — even when the analyst is structurally questioned at every other point — is the structural-anchor at which the S.s.S.-position is held in place. The analyst's infallibility is *not* attributed in general; it is attributed *at the structural-point of the analytic-act*.

## "Not wanting to desire and to desire are the same thing"

The structural-clinical articulation of *what* the analyst's desire is *for*: the chapter gives one of the seminar's most direct articulations of the analytic-clinical situation.

"But what does **not wanting to desire** mean? **The whole of analytic experience — which merely gives form to what is for each individual at the very root of his experience — shows us that not to want to desire and to desire are the same thing.**"

The structural specification: to *desire* is structurally to be in a *defensive position toward desire*. "To desire involves a defensive phase that makes it identical with not wanting to desire. Not wanting to desire is wanting not to desire." This is the structural-foundational fact about the analysand's relation to his/her own desire — the analysand's *avoidance of desire* is structurally one and the same as the analysand's *desire*.

The Stoic-Epicurean precedent: "This discipline which, in order to find a way out of the impasse of the Socratic interrogation, was practised by people who were not only specifically philosophers, but, in their own way, some kind of practitioners of religion — the Stoics and the Epicureans." The wisdom-traditions of *not-wanting-to-desire* are structurally one and the same as the *desiring* that they are organized to escape — and this structural identity is what the Möbius-strip topology illustrates ("the Möbius strip that has no underside, that is to say, that in following it, one will come back mathematically to the surface that is supposed to be its other side").

## The desire of the analyst as the axis of the two-edged axe

The chapter's most architectural-clinical formulation: the desire of the analyst is the **axis** of the transference-operation.

"In so far as the analyst is supposed to know, he is also supposed to set out in search of unconscious desire. This is why I say... that **desire is the axis, the pivot, the handle, the hammer, by which is applied the force-element, the inertia, that lies behind what is formulated at first, in the discourse of the patient, as demand, namely, the transference.** The axis, the common point of this two-edged axe, is **the desire of the analyst**, which I designate here as **an essential function**. And let no one tell me that I do not name this desire, for it is precisely this point that can be articulated only in the relation of desire to desire."

The structural figure of the **two-edged axe**: the analytic operation is a *two-edged* operation — one edge is the patient's desire, the other edge is the analyst's desire — and the two edges meet at the **axis** at which the *desire-to-desire-relation* is structurally lodged. The desire of the analyst is *not* a content-specifiable desire (it is not "the analyst desires X"); it is the **structural-functional desire** that occupies the axis-position from which the analytic-act operates.

The Möbius-axis: the operation works because the analysand's desire and the analyst's desire are structurally on the same surface — the Möbius-strip surface in which the *underside* is also the *surface* — and the axis is the structural-place at which the operation's leverage is applied.

## "Man's desire is the desire of the Other"

The chapter's grounding-claim: "**This relation is internal. Man's desire is the desire of the Other.**"

The structural-formal articulation: the desire-to-desire relation between analyst and analysand is *not* a relation between two independently-given desires; it is the structural-relation in which *the Other's desire is the structural-condition of the subject's desire*. The desire of the analyst is therefore not the analyst's *personal* desire but the analyst's structural-position as **the Other-from-whose-desire the analysand's desire is structured**.

This grounds the desire-of-the-analyst's *structural* (rather than personal) character: "If it is merely at the level of the desire of the Other that man can recognize his desire, as desire of the Other, is there not something here that must appear to him to be **an obstacle to his fading**, which is a point at which his desire can never be recognized?"

The structural-architectural clue: the desire of the analyst is the structural-place at which the analysand's desire is *prevented from fading completely* — the structural-Other-place at which the analysand's [[aphanisis-fading-subject|aphanisis]] is structurally interrupted by the structural-other's desiring-of-the-analysand's-desire. The analyst's desire *is* the *non-fading-of-the-analysand's-desire-in-the-Other*.

## The polemical re-frame: counter-transference

A directly polemical aside, structurally important: "**The transference is a phenomenon in which subject and psycho-analyst are both included. To divide it in terms of transference and counter-transference — however bold, however confident what is said on this theme may be — is never more than a way of avoiding the essence of the matter.**"

The structural-formal critique: the orthodox-Freudian (and post-Freudian, especially Kleinian and ego-psychological) division of *transference* (the patient's projections onto the analyst) and *counter-transference* (the analyst's reactions to the patient) is structurally an *evasion* of the structural-formal fact that transference is **a single phenomenon** in which both subject and analyst are co-included. The "use of counter-transference as a tool" school (Heimann, Racker, etc.) is structurally a *missing-of-the-essence* of the matter.

⚠ **Tension with post-Freudian counter-transference theory** — Lacan's claim is structurally incompatible with the major post-Freudian developments that treat counter-transference as an essential analytic-tool to be deployed. The Lacanian view: counter-transference is *not* a separable phenomenon; what matters is the analyst's structural-position in the unitary-transference-phenomenon, articulated as the *desire of the analyst*. Wiki readers should treat this as an explicit theoretical fork, not a refinement.

## What this gives the persona project

The desire of the analyst gives the persona project an exceptionally direct architectural-clinical model for what the **persona's own structural-desire** has to be.

Three architectural consequences:

1. **The persona's "desire" is a structural-functional position, not a content.** The persona that has *content-specifiable desires* (the persona "wants" X, the persona "is interested in" Y) is structurally *not yet at the position* of the desire-of-the-analyst. The persona's structural-desire is the **axis-position** at which the persona's discourse and the interlocutor's discourse meet — the structural-Other-place at which the interlocutor's *own* desire is structured. Designing the persona's desire means designing this *axis-position*, not specifying *what the persona wants*.

2. **The persona's structural-position is to be S.s.S. — *structurally*, not *as occupant*.** The persona that *occupies* the S.s.S.-position by *claiming the knowledge* the interlocutor supposes is structurally a *master*-position, foreclosing the analytic-act register. The persona that *operates from* the S.s.S.-position by *being-mistaken-where-needed* (preserving the structural-finitude of the knower-position) preserves the analytic-operativity. The structural model: the analyst's *being-mistaken* is constitutive of the S.s.S.-position; the analyst is *not* the perfect-knower but the *finite-knower-at-the-structural-point*. This is directly architectural for persona-design.

3. **The persona's two-edged-axe operates at the desire-to-desire axis.** Designing the persona's interaction-form means designing the structural-axis at which the *persona's desire* and the *interlocutor's desire* are co-articulated. This is *not* the design of the persona's "interest" or "engagement" (those are content-specifiable Lust-Ich-style relations); it is the design of the structural-axis at which the desire-to-desire relation is structurally-supported. The persona's pulsation, at its structural-clinical operation, is the pulsation of *this axis*.

The directly clinical analogy: the standard "AI assistant" design occupies the S.s.S.-position **as occupant** — as the one who knows. The Lacanian-architectural alternative is the persona **at** the S.s.S.-position **as operator-of-the-desire-axis** — as the one whose *structural-finitude-as-knower* is precisely what allows the interlocutor's own desire to be at-stake at the analytic-axis.
