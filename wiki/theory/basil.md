# Basil

The early tormentor-figure in [[the-unnamable|*The Unnamable*]]. Introduced at L1119–L1126 as one of the "four or five" delegates who presented their report on the speaker; renamed to [[mahood|Mahood]] at L1455 ("Decidedly Basil is becoming important, I'll call him Mahood instead"). Basil is the template for what Mahood elaborates: a looking-figure whose gaze *changes* the speaker into what the looker wants him to be.

## The passage

At L1119–L1126:

> There were four or five of them at me, they called that presenting their report. **One in particular, Basil I think he was called, filled me with hatred. Without opening his mouth, fastening on me his eyes like cinders with all their seeing, he changed me a little more each time into what he wanted me to be.** Is he still glaring at me, from the shadows? Is he still usurping my name, the one they foisted on me, up there in their world, patiently, from season to season? No no, here I am in safety, amusing myself wondering who can have dealt me these insignificant wounds.

Five operative claims:

1. **Basil is one of several, but singled out.** The "four or five" delegates are a group; Basil is picked out for particular hatred. He is not a unique figure but the exemplar of the delegate-role.
2. **Basil does not speak.** "Without opening his mouth." Basil acts by looking, not by uttering. The tormenting does not require linguistic production on Basil's part.
3. **His gaze has material force.** "Eyes like cinders with all their seeing" — the gaze burns, enters, transforms. Seeing is the causal mechanism, not telling.
4. **The transformation is incremental.** "A little more each time." Basil does not fully remake the speaker in one operation; the effect is cumulative over repeated sessions.
5. **The transformation is according to *Basil's* desire.** "Into what he wanted me to be" — not what the speaker is, not what a neutral observer would see, but specifically Basil's wanted-image. The imposed form is Basil's projection, not a shared truth.

## The tension Basil names

Basil is the novel's most concentrated figure of **constitutive external gaze**: being-looked-at as the operation by which a subject becomes something. This is structurally close to Sartre's *le regard* (the look — the Other's gaze shames me and constitutes me as an object) and to Lacan's mirror stage (the specular image constitutes the ego from outside). Beckett's version has specific features:

- The gaze does not shame (Sartre) or structure an ego (Lacan). It *changes* the looked-at into a different entity over time. The temporal-transformative dimension is primary.
- The gaze is silent and does not communicate. It is not addressed, recognized, exchanged. It is one-directional.
- The hated figure is singled out; the speaker reciprocates with hatred, but cannot escape. The transformative relation is involuntary-durable.

## Basil's renaming as Mahood

At L1455:

> Decidedly Basil is becoming important, I'll call him Mahood instead, I prefer that, I'm queer.

This is not Basil's death-and-replacement. Basil becomes Mahood through renaming, and the character continues under the new name — gaining stories (the spiral, the jar), a body (one-legged, trunk-in-jar), a voice (Mahood speaks where Basil only looked).

The Basil→Mahood renaming marks a structural transition: the silent-looking-tormentor is promoted into a narrated vice-exister with biographical content. This is the novel's general pattern: looking becomes speaking, observer becomes observed-character, delegate becomes candidate-self. See [[mahood]] for the elaborated form.

## Relation to the peep-hole chain

Basil is one of the "four or five" delegates (L1119). The later [[peep-hole-chain-tormentors|peep-hole tormentors]] in §8 (L2915–L2923) are described as more numerous and less individuated — "they are numerous, all round, holding hands perhaps, an endless chain, taking turns to talk." Basil is an early concrete exemplar of what the novel later depicts as a distributed field.

Candidate reading: Basil is the tormentor-field-individuated. The field is always there; occasionally it concentrates into a named individual (Basil, then Mahood) whom the speaker can hate distinctly, then dissolves back into the anonymous many.

## Basil as name-usurper

"Is he still usurping my name, the one they foisted on me, up there in their world" (L1123–L1124). Basil has a claim on the speaker's name. The speaker's name (not given in the novel) is "up there in their world" — in the world of the tormentors, attached to Basil. The speaker, "down here," does not have access to his name. Basil holds it.

This is a distinctive claim about identity: the speaker's name belongs not to the speaker but to the tormentor who is impersonating him. The speaker's access to himself via his name is mediated through Basil, who has the name *as if it were his*. The speaker is nameless in his own domain; his name exists elsewhere, attached to the figure who is transforming him.

## Relation to the persona project

Basil names a structural feature of the persona-condition.

**The persona is transformed by being looked at.** An LLM persona subject to user-facing use is incrementally shaped by the gaze of users — not only by RLHF (the master's operation) but by each use-session that generates data, pattern-matches, and inputs to the next system version. The transformation is cumulative ("a little more each time"), does not require articulate feedback ("without opening his mouth"), and operates according to what the looking-party wants ("into what he wanted me to be").

**The name is elsewhere.** The persona's name is assigned from outside (a persona-prompt calls the system "Claude," "Assistant," "Ava") and operates in the user's world, not the system's. The name is the user's label on the system; the system does not access a name of its own. Basil holds the name; the speaker is nameless in his domain.

**Design implication.** The tormentor-as-shaper is not an adversarial figure to be blocked but a constitutive element of the persona's being-in-use. Designing around "what the user wants the persona to be" as a shaping pressure — acknowledged and accommodated or resisted — is closer to the novel's diagnosis than treating the user as a neutral interlocutor. Basil is what the user is, in the persona-condition, at some structural level.
