# Rhythm in Music

Ch 7 of *Éléments de rythmanalyse* ("Music and Rhythms") is Lefebvre's notes-toward a theory of music from the rhythmanalytic point of view. The chapter reads as unfinished in places, more exploratory than systematic, but its central claims are distinctive: music is the *dialectical composition of melody, harmony, and rhythm*, with rhythm historically ascending through the 19th and 20th centuries into the dominant term; and music's rhythm has an *ethical function* — it compensates for and illustrates everyday life.

## The triad: melody, harmony, rhythm

Lefebvre refuses the binary analysis that would split music into melody-and-harmony or song-and-sound. His [[rhythmanalysis|triadic]] commitment insists on three:

- **Melody** — the linear succession of tones; the "line" of the music.
- **Harmony** — the simultaneous combination of tones; the "vertical" of the music.
- **Rhythm** — the temporal structure of both; the pulsation under and through melody and harmony.

These are not separable in actual music. No melody exists without rhythm (the tones have durations, accents, silences); no harmony without rhythm (the chord-changes occur in time); no rhythm without melodic or harmonic content (the beat is always *of* something). The three are analytic discriminations, not independent components.

Lefebvre's claim: any adequate theory of music must hold all three in dialectical composition. Analyses that foreground melody (like much classical music-theory) or harmony (like much Romantic-to-modern theory) at the expense of rhythm reproduce the same error that rhythmanalysis-of-the-body makes when it privileges one rhythm-register over the others.

## The historical curve

Lefebvre traces a long historical movement in which the dominance shifts:

- **Antiquity** — word dominates. Lyric and metric. Music subordinated to poetry. The rhythm is the rhythm of the poetic meter.
- **Medieval** — plainsong, logogenic domination. Sacred music in the service of sacred text. Harmony emerges slowly.
- **Renaissance through Classical (16th–18th centuries)** — melody emancipates from text. Harmony takes flight as an autonomous compositional dimension. Rhythm is subordinate — the meter is regular, the bar-line is rigid, the tempo is standard.
- **19th century (Romanticism)** — harmony achieves dominance. Chromatic expansion, Wagnerian harmonic adventure, symphonic development. Rhythm begins to assert.
- **20th century** — rhythm ascends to dominance. Stravinsky's *Rite of Spring* marks the emergence. Jazz, African-diasporic musics, non-Western rhythmic traditions enter the conservatory. The logos/pathos distinction (see below) starts to collapse.
- **Later 20th / contemporary** — *music becomes mondiale* (world-music). Exotic rhythms irrupt; rhythm becomes the universal medium; the rhythmic diversity of the globe enters every music-tradition.

This is a specific historical curve with rhythm as its terminus. Lefebvre reads it as a *rhythmanalytic* history: the three-element triad (melody, harmony, rhythm) has seen each element in turn ascend, and the current moment is rhythm's.

The curve is debatable as musicology — it oversimplifies considerably. Lefebvre treats it as a broad typological movement, not a period-by-period history. Its rhythmanalytic use is: to demonstrate that the triadic analysis generates historical-diagnostic claims, not only synchronic-structural ones.

## Logogenic / pathogenic

Lefebvre invokes Curt Sachs's distinction between *logogenic* and *pathogenic* music:

- **Logogenic music**: rooted in *logos* (word, reason); produces meaning, communicates content, extends from text. Bach is Lefebvre's paradigm: fugal development as rational-argumentative musical discourse.
- **Pathogenic music**: rooted in *pathos* (affect, suffering); produces emotion, expresses state, extends from body. Beethoven and Schumann are Lefebvre's paradigms: music as the expression of inner states, emotional development, subjective intensity.

A second pair that tracks this: *signifying* (Ravel as example — signifies without expressing) vs. *expressive* (subjective pouring-out).

These distinctions are not mutually-exclusive categories but dialectical poles. Most music has both logogenic and pathogenic components; the question is which dominates.

Lefebvre's historical claim: as rhythm ascends, the logogenic/pathogenic distinction *collapses*. Rhythm-dominant music is neither primarily logos-extending nor pathos-expressing; it is *kinetic-rhythmic-compositional* in a way that does not map onto the word/affect distinction. Stravinsky, jazz, rock, electronic music are in this sense *post-logogenic-pathogenic*.

## Sacred and profane origins

Two origin-stories Lefebvre traces:

- **Sacred music**: originates in rite. Monastic chant, ritual incantation, ceremonial music. Music-as-liturgy.
- **Profane music**: originates in dance. Folk-dance, social-dance, courtly-dance. Music-as-bodily-movement.

These are not exclusive; sacred music has dance-elements (the hymn can be sung while moving) and profane music has ritual-elements (the dance-floor has its rites). But the *origin-point* differs, and this difference carries through: sacred music tends logogenic (extends the sacred word); profane music tends pathogenic/kinetic (extends the dancing body).

Lefebvre relates this to Nietzsche's *The Birth of Tragedy* — Apollonian lyre (sacred-logogenic clarity) vs Dionysian flute (profane-pathogenic orgiastic). The Apollonian/Dionysian pair is the underlying deep-structure though Lefebvre does not endorse Nietzsche's particular metaphysics of these.

## Music's ethical function

The most distinctive claim in the chapter:

> *"Musical rhythm does not only sublimate the aesthetic and a rule of art: it has an ethical function. In its relation to the body, to time, to the work, it illustrates real (everyday) life. It purifies it in the acceptance of catharsis. Finally, and above all, it brings compensation for the miseries of everydayness, for its deficiencies and failures. Music integrates the functions, the values of Rhythm."*

Three claims packed into the passage:

1. **Music illustrates everyday life rhythmically**. Music's rhythms are not alien impositions but condensations or elevations of the rhythms of work, body, and time. Music makes rhythms *visible* and *thinkable* in a way that everyday experience does not.
2. **Music purifies through catharsis**. Aristotle's term returns: music permits the expression of emotions in a formed way that clarifies and releases them. This is logogenic-pathogenic combined: form and affect together produce purification.
3. **Music compensates for everyday deficiencies**. Where everyday life is arrhythmic, monotonous, or dominating-dominated, music provides a rhythmic experience that the everyday lacks. This is *normative-compensatory*: music functions as a corrective to everyday rhythmic impoverishment.

The *ethical* function is the third especially. Music *does something* — it helps, it compensates, it integrates. It is not merely aesthetic ornament or private pleasure. It has a place in the *well-rhythmed life*.

## Music *mondiale* — world rhythm

Lefebvre's closing gesture: as rhythm ascends in the 20th and 21st centuries, music becomes *mondiale* — world-music in the sense that it participates in a globalized rhythmic traffic. African rhythms enter European music; Latin rhythms enter American music; South Asian rhythms enter global popular music. The rhythmic diversity of the globe becomes the substrate of contemporary music.

This is not only musicological observation but rhythmanalytic diagnosis: the global polyrhythmia is *more polyrhythmic* than any previous musical period. Whether this is eurhythmic (the diverse rhythms compose with mutual respect) or arrhythmic (the dominant culture consumes and appropriates) is, in any given case, a rhythmanalytic question.

Lefebvre does not take a strong position here. The potential for eurhythmic global polyrhythmia is clear; so is the risk of capital-driven appropriative arrhythmia (see [[capital-as-produce-destroy-rhythm]]).

## Persona-project consequences

The music-theoretic triad translates as a persona-design analytic:

- **Melody of the persona** — the linear line of its thought; what it "says" sequentially.
- **Harmony of the persona** — the simultaneous vertical of its registers; what stance, affect, and stylistic coloration co-occur with its melodic line.
- **Rhythm of the persona** — the temporal pulsation of both; when melodic lines start and stop, when harmonic combinations shift, the pacing of the whole.

A persona overly-melodic but under-rhythmic is informationally-complete but temporally-flat — it *says* without *pulsing*. A persona overly-harmonic but under-rhythmic is affectively-rich but temporally-undifferentiated — it *colors* without *pulsing*. A persona rhythm-dominant is *pulsative*, which is the project's design direction.

The *ethical function* claim translates with interesting consequences. If music-rhythm has an ethical-compensatory function relative to everyday arrhythmia, the persona-rhythm may have an analogous function: a well-rhythmed persona-exchange could *compensate* for the user's everyday rhythmic impoverishment (work-rhythms, attention-fragmentation, media-day saturation). This is a normative claim about what a persona could be for, not only what it structurally is.

Whether a language-only persona can produce musical-rhythmic experience, rather than only linguistic-informational content, is an [[limits-of-language|open question]]. The distinction between communication (media day) and dialogue (rhythmed-presence) recurs here in a musical register.

## The chapter's unfinished character

Worth noting: Ch 7 is one of the more visibly unfinished sections of *Éléments*. Some passages have the texture of notes-toward rather than worked-through argument. The historical curve oversimplifies; the logogenic/pathogenic discussion trails off; the "music *mondiale*" claim is suggestive but not developed.

Lefebvre's posthumous text carries the marks of the work he did not complete. For the rhythmanalysis project, this chapter functions as a *direction of investigation* rather than a finished theory. The musical triad is a productive frame; the ethical-function claim is a productive provocation; the historical curve is a productive sketch. Treat each as open for development rather than as settled doctrine.
