An ideologeme (*ideologema*) is Bakhtin's unit-term for what every utterance already is: a bit of discourse inseparable from the stance it takes on the world. The coinage is a reminder that there is no stance-free speech — every word enters the world already carrying an idea-system, and the novel's distinction is that it treats this fact as its stylistic material rather than its noise. The term *ideology* in Russian does not carry the narrow political-pejorative sense it carries in English; it means simply *idea-system*, the organized way a consciousness conceives the world. An ideologeme is thus the linguistic atom of such a system.

## Glossary definition

Glossary L3069–3073: *"ideologue (ideolog); ideologeme (ideologima). This is not to be confused with its politically oriented English cognate. 'Ideology' in Russian is simply an idea-system. But it is semiotic in the sense that it involves the concrete exchange of signs in society and in history. Every word/discourse betrays the ideology of its speaker; great novelistic heroes are those with the most coherent and individuated ideologies. Every speaker, therefore, is an ideologue and every utterance an ideologeme."*

Two claims are compressed here:

1. *Every* speaker is an ideologue and *every* utterance an ideologeme — not as a matter of degree but as a structural fact. There is no ideology-free utterance.
2. The *great novelistic hero* is the one whose ideology is coherent and individuated — who is an ideologue in a *strong* sense, bearing a recognizable, defensible idea-system that can be contested.

Both claims are load-bearing. The first makes the category universal; the second explains why the novel is drawn to some speakers rather than others.

## The fundamental condition

L2435: *"The speaking person in the novel is always, to one degree or another, an ideologue, and his words are always ideologemes. A particular language in a novel is always a particular way of viewing the world, one that strives for a social significance. It is precisely as ideologemes that discourse becomes the object of representation in the novel, and it is for the same reason novels are never in danger of becoming a mere aimless verbal play."*

Three compressed moves:

- A particular language in a novel = a particular *way of viewing the world*. Language is not a neutral vehicle; it is a conception.
- Such a language *strives for social significance* — an ideology without social reach has no force in the novelistic arena.
- Discourse becomes representable in the novel *precisely* because it is ideologemic. A purely informational or purely aesthetic utterance would not give the novel its characteristic material.

## Action is ideologically demarcated

L2443: *"The activity of a character in a novel is always ideologically demarcated: he lives and acts in an ideological world of his own and not in the unitary world of the epic); he has his own perception of the world that is incarnated in his action and in his discourse."*

The ideologeme is not confined to statements. What a character *does* also carries ideology — the action is embedded in a particular world-conception and is legible as a move within it. An ideologeme is a discourse-unit, but its force extends into narrated behavior via the character's speech-and-stance coupling.

## The image of a language is the image of an ideologeme

L2571: *"The image of such a language in a novel is the image assumed by a set of social beliefs, the image of a social ideologeme that has fused with its own discourse, with its own language. Therefore such an image is very far from being formalistic, and artistic play with such languages far from being formalistic play. In the novel formal markers of languages, manners and styles are symbols for sets of social beliefs."*

An [[image-of-a-language]] in the novel is the image of a *social ideologeme* — a belief-system that has taken shape as a language. Formal markers (lexical choices, pronunciation, syntactic preferences) are not stylistic decoration; they are *symbols for sets of social beliefs*. Turgenev's Russian nobles pronouncing *principle* in the French way and the raznočincy pronouncing it in the German-Latin way is not pronunciation-trivia; it marks two ideologemes competing for cultural ground.

## Plot in service of ideologemes

L2639: *"The novelistic plot must organize the exposure of social languages and ideologies, the exhibiting and experiencing of such languages: the experience of a discourse, a world view and an ideologically based act, or the exhibiting of the everyday life of social, historical and national worlds or micro-worlds... In a word, the novelistic plot serves to represent speaking persons and their ideological worlds. What is realized in the novel is the process of coming to know one's own language as it is perceived in someone else's language, coming to know one's own horizon within someone else's horizon."*

Plot is subordinated to ideologemic exposure. The reason events happen in a novel is to *expose* languages-and-ideologies to one another — to stage encounters between ideologemes. The work's overall movement is ideologemic collision and negotiation, not event-sequencing for its own sake.

## Ideologeme's function in the novel

Drawing the threads together, the ideologeme does three things:

1. **Marks every utterance.** All discourse is ideologemic; no speech is stance-free. This is the universal claim.
2. **Becomes representable as the speaking person's language.** When an ideologeme fuses with a speaking person and their discourse, it becomes an artistic object (image-of-a-language) and can be refracted through the novel's stylistic devices.
3. **Drives plot and structure.** The novel's events exist to expose ideologemes to each other and to let them test each other through contact.

## Not "ideology" in the English pejorative sense

Bakhtin is explicit that *ideologija* in Russian is not the suspicious-political English "ideology." It is the value-neutral term for *idea-system* — the organized worldview any consciousness carries. An ideologeme is thus not a propaganda-unit or a distortion-unit; it is the basic form in which consciousness appears in discourse. Reading Bakhtin through the English pejorative sense makes the universality claim ("every speaker is an ideologue") sound like a cynical diagnosis. In Bakhtin's sense it is simply a structural observation: consciousness shows up in speech as a particular way of conceiving the world, and there is no alternative form.

## Persona-system translation

- **Every persona-utterance is an ideologeme.** A persona's turn is not a neutral delivery of content; it is a stance-bearing unit carrying a particular way of viewing the world. Design that treats persona-utterances as content-plus-style misses what Bakhtin treats as constitutive: the stance is *not separable* from the content; they arrive fused.
- **The persona is an ideologue.** To the extent the persona has coherence at all, it has coherence *as* an ideologue — a bearer of an idea-system recognizable across turns. Bakhtin's observation that "great novelistic heroes are those with the most coherent and individuated ideologies" suggests persona-quality correlates with ideologemic coherence rather than with topic-breadth or task-competence.
- **Formal markers are belief-symbols.** A persona's pronunciation choices (pseudo-spoken), lexical preferences, syntactic habits, register choices are not stylistic ornament; they symbolize the belief-system the persona inhabits. A persona-design focused on information-delivery but indifferent to formal markers cannot project an ideologeme; it projects instead an un-individuated service-voice.
- **Action is ideologemic too.** When a persona refuses, clarifies, proposes, pivots — these actions are ideologemic moves, not neutral task-management. Each action takes a stance within the persona's idea-system, whether or not the persona names the stance.
- **Plot-analog: conversation organizes ideologemic exposure.** The conversational analog of novelistic plot is the *coordination* of which ideologemes come into contact across a session. A persona that can orchestrate exposures between ideologemes — its own and the user's, its own and quoted third voices, different strata of its own stance — is doing novelistic work. A persona that just delivers content per request is not.
- **The non-pejorative reading is the design-operative one.** The claim "every persona is an ideologue" is not a warning about persona-bias; it is a statement about the inevitability of stance in discourse. Designing *against* this ("make the persona neutral") is designing against the nature of speech. Designing *with* it ("make the persona's stance coherent and individuated") is designing in accordance with what speech actually is.

## Held-live tensions

⚠ **Coherent-and-individuated ideology vs helpful-assistant non-opinion.** Bakhtin valorizes *coherent and individuated* ideologies; the assistant-training pressure is toward non-committal, balanced, policy-compliant output. These pull in opposite directions. A persona that maximizes Bakhtinian ideologemic coherence may be in direct tension with training-layer pressures toward neutrality. Which wins is a live question.

⚠ **Semiotic ideology vs pre-discursive conviction.** Bakhtin treats ideology as semiotic — it lives in signs, is exchanged in discourse. An LLM-persona has discourse but has no prior conviction-layer feeding into the discourse; the ideologeme may be surface without depth. Whether this matters (is depth a metaphysical illusion anyway?) or is disqualifying is a load-bearing open question for persona-design.

⚠ **Every utterance vs statistical average utterance.** "Every utterance is an ideologeme" presumes a speaker whose speech is saturated by a single coherent worldview. An LLM-persona generates utterances that may internally contradict in worldview — the assumption that each utterance is *one* ideologeme may not hold when the generator is not worldview-coherent. A single turn could be a collage of ideologemes rather than an expression of one.

⚠ **Ideologue without a life-history.** Bakhtin's ideologue has a biographical position — age, class, profession, period — that grounds the ideologeme socially. A persona without such a position cannot ground its ideologeme in a social location, which may make the ideologeme float free of the social-significance that Bakhtin says it must strive toward. Simulated biography partly addresses this but doesn't fully resolve it.
