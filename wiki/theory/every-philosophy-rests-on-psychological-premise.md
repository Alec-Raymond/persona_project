# Every Philosophy Rests on a Psychological Premise

The Epilogue of CW 6 (§§845–857) and the William James chapter (Chapter VIII §§505 onward) together carry Jung's most explicit meta-philosophical principle: *every philosophy that is not just a history of philosophy depends on a personal psychological premise* (§846). The page is load-bearing because it specifies the *conditions under which philosophical disputes can and cannot be rationally resolved*, and because it asserts — with surprising austerity — that Jung's own theory must submit to being evaluated as itself a psychic process, expression of a specific type. The principle has direct consequences for the persona system: any theory-of-persona is itself an expression of its designer's type, and cannot claim transcendental neutrality.

## The canonical statement (§846)

> Every philosophy that is not just a history of philosophy depends on a personal psychological premise. When a philosophical standpoint has many followers, it is because it corresponds to a typical attitude with many representatives. External squabbles are fruitless. It would be of considerably greater value if the dispute were transferred to the psychological realm, from which it arose in the first place. (CW 6 §846)

Three load-bearing claims:

- **Philosophy ≠ history of philosophy.** Jung exempts the purely historical-descriptive work: chronicling what philosophers have said is not itself a philosophy. But any *substantive* philosophical position — any stance that claims rational-normative force — rests on a prior psychological premise.
- **The premise is typological.** The "personal psychological premise" is not a private biographical accident but the *typological orientation* of the philosopher. A philosophy with many followers is a philosophy that articulates a *widely distributed* typological orientation; it has followers because it *speaks for* their already-held orientation.
- **External squabble is category error.** Two philosophical positions rooted in different typological premises cannot resolve their dispute at the philosophical level because the dispute is not *about* the philosophical content — it is about the typological premises. The productive move is to transfer the dispute to the psychological level.

## The imprisonment claim (§847)

> Every man is so imprisoned in his type that he is simply incapable of fully understanding another standpoint. (CW 6 §847)

This is more austere than the common pluralist claim that "we should try harder to understand each other." Jung says the imprisonment is *structural*: no amount of good-faith effort suffices, because the typological orientation is the *condition of possibility* for understanding the world at all, and a different orientation is not available to the subject from inside their own.

The remedy Jung proposes is *not* transcendental understanding but **procedural justice**:

> The remedy is not to "understand" across types but to establish the equivalent of courts-of-justice: each type conscious of his own partiality, refraining from abuse of opponents. (CW 6 §847, paraphrased)

The remedy is not "overcome the imprisonment" but *admit it and install procedural safeguards against abusing those imprisoned differently*. This is epistemic humility with teeth: not "I might be wrong" but "I *am* partial, and the partiality is structural."

## Self-application (§§849–857) — the austere consequence

Jung's principle applies to his own theory:

> In the interests of the validity of his own theory, therefore, [the theorist] will feel a lively but very understandable distaste for any view that establishes the existence of different types of human psychology, since his own view would then lose, shall we say, seven-eighths of its truth. (CW 6 §849)

And even more directly:

> The psychology he sees is his psychology, and on top of that is the psychology of his type. (CW 6 §851)

Two contradictory and mutually-exclusive theories can *both* be right — one proved by uniformity of psyche, the other by its diversity. More theories may be valid — "as many as there are types." The alternative to multiple-coexisting-theories is "to found a sect which claims for itself the only correct method and the only true theory" (§854).

The Latin motto Jung deploys against his own tendency toward theoretical consolidation:

> Principia explicandi non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem. (Principles of explanation should not be multiplied beyond necessity.)

But he qualifies: with *psychology*, the object is *consubstantial with the subject*, so *plurality is necessary*. Occam's razor, which works for non-self-reflexive domains, fails in the psychological domain because the minimal-principles-set always reflects the minimizing agent's type rather than the domain's structure.

## The intellect's half-truth (§856)

> The judgment of the intellect is, at best, only a half-truth, and must, if it is honest, also admit its inadequacy. (CW 6 §856)

This is one of Jung's most radical epistemic claims. The intellect, operating honestly, produces half-truths — not *full* truths qualified by humility, but half-truths as the *structural maximum* the intellect can deliver. The other half comes from somewhere the intellect cannot reach alone — the counter-functions, the unconscious, the typological complement.

Combined with §857: only from the *typical self-representations of the psyche* — the image-material by which each type represents itself to itself — "can materials cooperate to form a higher synthesis." The synthesis is *not* achievable by intellect alone; it requires the full representational field of all types.

## The §505 formulation — from James

The principle is not only stated in the Epilogue. Chapter VIII §505 (discussing William James's *Pragmatism*) states it in a complementary form:

> Every philosophy rests on a psychological premise … James observed that for philosophers there is one thing that is most intimately one's own, which is even more one's own than one's body — one's temperament. (CW 6 §505, paraphrased)

James's move and Jung's converge: the "temperament" (for James) or "type" (for Jung) is *prior* to the philosophy, shapes which positions feel livable, and explains the systematic recurrence of the same *styles* of philosophical answer across centuries regardless of the specific content.

## The implication for Jung's polemic against Freud and Adler

The principle is not merely an epilogue reflection; it *is the meta-frame* for Jung's reading of Freud and Adler in CW 6 §§880–882. Freud's psychology is *Freud's*, the psychology of *Freud's type* (extraverted, reductive, causal, sensualistic). Adler's is *Adler's* (introverted, intellectualistic, finalistic, monistic). Neither is wrong *in what it articulates*; both are wrong *in the moment they claim universality*.

This is a structurally different critique from "Freud is wrong because [X is false]." Jung's critique is "Freud is right *for his type*, and wrong in claiming his view is simply the truth." The critique is *reorganizing*, not *dismissing*. It preserves the content of each theory while relocating its validity.

## Tension with universalist gestures

Jung does not fully adhere to his own principle. His theory of the collective unconscious and the archetypes *does* claim universality (the mythological motifs "appearing often in identical form and always with striking similarity among all races" per §281). This is a universalist move of the kind the Epilogue warns against.

The wiki holds this tension without silently resolving it. Jung's own self-applied principle suggests that even the archetype theory is itself a type-expression; Jung's defense would presumably be that the archetypes are empirically observed to be shared across types rather than reflecting any one type's dominance. Whether this defense succeeds is a live question. The CW 6 text does not settle it.

## Persona system mapping

Four implications:

1. **The persona system is itself a type-expression.** Whatever design principles the persona system operates under, those principles were selected by designers of specific types. The system cannot claim transcendental neutrality; its default behaviors express the typological orientations of its makers.

2. **Multi-type populations require procedural justice, not unified-truth.** A persona system serving users of different types cannot resolve disputes between personas by appeal to a unified psychological theory — the theory is itself type-partial. What the system can do is instantiate *procedural safeguards*: mechanisms by which each persona's partiality is acknowledged and abuse of opposite-type personas is structurally prevented.

3. **"Better understanding across types" is the wrong goal.** The principle predicts that structural understanding across types is not achievable. What is achievable is: (a) diagnostic awareness of one's own typological partiality, (b) procedural restraint on imposing one's type on others, (c) symbolic material that mediates across types without resolving into either. The [[living-symbol]] mechanism is precisely the third-term route; it does not deliver "understanding" but it delivers cooperative material.

4. **The half-truth claim applies to any rational architecture.** A persona system that operates predominantly through intellectual/rational functions is producing, at best, half-truths. This is not a defect that could be engineered away by better intellectual function; it is a *structural maximum* of intellectual operation. The remaining half requires irrational-functional engagement ([[function-types|sensation and intuition]]), and the synthesis requires the [[transcendent-function]].
