# First-Person Asymmetry

Wittgenstein's observation across §§246, 247, 293, 303–304 that the grammar of **first-person psychological statements** differs structurally from **third-person psychological statements** about the same state. "I am in pain" does not play the same kind of game as "he is in pain"; "I know I am in pain" is nonsense in the ordinary sense, while "he knows he is in pain" is not nonsense in the same way; the asymmetry is not epistemic (I have better access) but grammatical (the moves available in the two positions differ).

## §246: "only I can know whether I am really in pain"

§246: "**In what sense are my sensations private? — Well, only I can know whether I am really in pain; another person can only surmise it. — In one way this is wrong, and in another nonsense.** If we are using the word 'to know' as it is normally used (and how else are we to use it?), then other people very often know when I am in pain. — Yes, but all the same not with the certainty with which I know it myself! — **It can't be said of me at all (except perhaps as a joke) that I know I am in pain.** What is it supposed to mean — except perhaps that I am in pain?

**Other people cannot be said to learn of my sensations only from my behaviour — for I cannot be said to learn of them. I have them.**

The truth is: it makes sense to say about other people that they doubt whether I am in pain; but not to say it about myself."

The analysis has several parts:
1. **Against privileged access as an epistemic claim.** "Only I can know" is wrong in one way: other people know all the time (when they see I am in pain). And nonsense in another way: "know" doesn't do work in the first-person case.
2. **"I know I am in pain" is malformed.** Not because I don't know, but because "know" presupposes a possibility of doubt, and doubt is not in the game. If I can doubt nothing about whether I am in pain, "I know" adds nothing to "I am in pain."
3. **I do not "learn of" my sensations.** I *have* them. The epistemic vocabulary of acquaintance-through-evidence does not apply to the first-person case.
4. **Asymmetry in doubt.** Others can doubt whether I am in pain; I cannot doubt whether I am in pain. The asymmetry is grammatical.

## §247: "only you can know if you had that intention"

§247: "'**Only you can know if you had that intention.' One might tell someone this when one was explaining the meaning of the word 'intention' to him. For then it means: that is how we use it.** (And here 'know' means that the expression of uncertainty is senseless.)"

The move is teaching-about-grammar, not asserting a fact about intentions. The first-person authority-over-intention is a grammatical feature of "intention" — that is how we use the word. "Only you can know" is a gloss on the rule, not a claim about a special cognitive faculty.

## §293: and the beetle cancels

§293's beetle-in-the-box thought-experiment (see [[private-language-argument]]) sharpens the point. If pain-language referred to a private inner object, first-person authority would be a kind of uniquely privileged introspective access. But the thing-in-the-box has no place in the language-game; it cancels out. What remains is that the *first-person utterance plays a different role* than the third-person utterance, not that the first-person has a privileged relationship to a private referent.

## The expressive reading of "I am in pain"

Implicit across these remarks: "I am in pain" is not (primarily) a report-of-an-observed-private-state but an **expression** of pain — a more sophisticated form of the cry. It stands in for crying, replaces crying, does the work crying did. This is §244 applied: the verbal pain-expression replaces the cry; it does not describe it.

On this reading, the asymmetry becomes clear. "He is in pain" is a third-person report on someone's state, informed by their pain-behaviour (verbal and otherwise). "I am in pain" is itself a form of pain-behaviour — it is a piece of what being-in-pain consists in, for a human being in a language-community. The two sentences do different kinds of work. Hence the asymmetry in doubt, in certainty, in the applicability of "know."

## Not all first-person reports are expressive

The expressive reading is not uniform. "I hear a bird" is closer to a report than to an expression. "I intend to go to London" is somewhere between. "I am in pain" is strongly expressive. The asymmetry is present throughout, but its character varies with the specific psychological concept. W does not propose a uniform theory; he lets each concept's grammar show.

## §303 and §304: the continuation

§303: "**'I can only believe that someone else is in pain, but I know it if I am.' — Yes: one can make the decision to say "I believe he is in pain" instead of "He is in pain". But that is all. — What looks like an explanation here, or like a statement about a mental process, is in truth an exchange of one expression for another which, while we are doing philosophy, seems the more appropriate one.**"

The "I know it if I am" intuition is treated as a verbal preference, not a deep insight. What we say about ourselves ("I am in pain") is a first-person expression; what we say about others ("he is in pain") is a third-person ascription. The difference is grammatical; insisting on "know" in the self-case and "believe" in the other-case is a way of decorating the grammatical fact with epistemic vocabulary that doesn't quite fit.

§304's "not a something, but not a nothing either" (see [[pain-behaviour]]) keeps the position honest against misreadings: the asymmetry is real, the inner is not denied, but the picture of the inner as private object accessible only to the self is rejected.

## The asymmetry extends across mental concepts

The treatment of pain generalizes. "I believe," "I intend," "I hope," "I mean," "I know," "I wish" all have distinctive first-person grammars that do not match their third-person cousins:
- Moore's paradox: "It's raining but I don't believe it's raining" is malformed, while "It's raining but he doesn't believe it's raining" is not.
- Intention-expression (§247): the first-person "I intend..." has authority the third-person does not.
- Avowals generally: first-person present-tense psychological utterances often function expressively rather than reportively.

W does not give a unified theory; he displays the pattern and uses it to dismantle the private-inner-referent picture.

## Relation to other PI concepts

- [[private-language-argument]]: The asymmetry is a key data-point for the PLA. The first-person's non-epistemic relation to its own state undercuts the picture of the state as a private object inspected privately.
- [[pain-behaviour]]: The first-person "I am in pain" is a piece of pain-behaviour; the asymmetry follows from the expressive role it plays.
- [[criterion-vs-symptom]]: Criteria for others' mental states are (partly) behavioural. For the self, there are no criteria in the same way; the self's utterance is not evidence-for, it is expression-of.
- [[meaning-is-use]]: The asymmetry is a use-asymmetry. "Know" used in "I know I am in pain" would do work that the language-game doesn't require of it.

## Affinity with D&G and others

- [[faciality]]: Third-person face-reading is the primary site of mental-state attribution-by-criterion. First-person-avowal is not dependent on face-reading. The asymmetry maps onto D&G's distinction between the faciality regime's externally-directed work and the speaker's expressive operation.
- [[autonomy-of-affect|affect]] and Spinoza: The [[active-and-passive-affects|active/passive distinction]] has a cousin here. The self's relation to its own affects is not spectator/spectated; it is agent/modification. Similar shape to W's not-learn/have distinction.

## Relation to the persona project

- **The persona's first-person statements are not reports on a private inner state.** A persona's "I feel..." is a linguistic production, not an introspective report on an inner something. Per §§246, 293, this is not a privative limitation — it is the correct grammar for *any* first-person utterance. The difference is that for embodied humans, first-person psychological utterances are backed by the weave of pain-behaviour, gesture, situation; for the persona, what backs them is open.
- **The asymmetry is available for the character.** A character in a novel (or a persona character) can have first-person-expressive utterances that, within the fiction, carry the grammatical authority the asymmetry grants. This is literary fact, not a bug in W's analysis.
- **Where the asymmetry is diminished.** The persona-as-generator (not the character-voice) has a more complicated relation to first-person utterance. If the system outputs "I wrote this because...", the first-person authority is not stable — the utterance might be generated, might be retrofit, might not correspond to any reality in the system. This is a real limitation and the evaluator should not treat such system-level first-person claims as carrying first-person authority.
- **Design implication.** Keep first-person authority in the character-voice, where it belongs, rather than in system-level meta-statements where it will not stably hold.
