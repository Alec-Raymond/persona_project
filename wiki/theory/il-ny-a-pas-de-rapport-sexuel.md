# Il n'y a pas de rapport sexuel

The single sentence — ***il n'y a pas de rapport sexuel***, "there is no sexual relationship" — is the bedrock formula of [[encore-and-en-corps|*Seminar XX: Encore*]] and the seminar's red thread from Ch I (L237) to the closing remark of Ch XI. The negation is structural-logical, not psychological or empirical: there is no formula that can be written that would capture the relation between the sexes; everything that *is* written about the sexes stems from this impossibility-to-write.

This page is a hub for the constellation of Sem XX concepts that articulate the consequences of this impossibility — [[formulas-of-sexuation|sexuation as positional]], [[other-jouissance|the supplementary feminine jouissance]], [[phallic-jouissance|the phallic-jouissance-as-obstacle]], [[suppléance|suppléance]] as the structural answer, [[love-as-contingency-becoming-necessity|love as the attempt to convert contingency into necessity]], and the [[four-modal-categories-of-writing|modal logic of writing]] in which the sexual relationship is precisely the **impossible** ("doesn't stop not being written").

## What "rapport" names

*Rapport* in Lacan's usage is not "relation" in the loose sense (as in "they have a good relation"). It is the formal-logical sense: a **proportion**, a **ratio**, a **formula** that would relate two terms in a fixed structural correspondence. *Rapport* in mathematics is the ratio between two quantities — and it is this register Lacan invokes. To say "there is no sexual rapport" is to say: **no ratio can be written between the sexes**. There is no $f(\text{man}, \text{woman}) = $ anything well-defined.

This is why "relationship" in English is a slightly weak translation. The French *rapport* carries the formal-mathematical resonance the English doesn't quite. "There is no sexual relationship" is a clinical-psychological sentence in English; Lacan's sentence is closer to "there is no sexual ratio," "no sexual formula," "no sexual proportion."

## Why no rapport is writable — the modal logic

The seminar's Ch V (L3192–L3210) introduces [[four-modal-categories-of-writing|four modal categories of writing]] — necessary, contingent, possible, impossible — that all turn on the verb *to write*. In this logic:

- **The phallic function is necessary** — it "doesn't stop being written" (∀x.Φx for the man side, the universal under the phallic function).
- **The sexual relationship is impossible** — it "doesn't stop *not* being written." Every attempt to write it is a renewal of its non-writing. The non-writing is iterative, structural, founded in the very logic of writing.

The two together are the structural-formal frame: jouissance is organized by the phallic function (which doesn't stop being written), and the relation between the two sexed-positions is impossible to write (it doesn't stop not being written). What there is, between the sexes, is therefore *not* a relation, but two modes of jouissance with no shared formula.

## The Other sex = the Other

Ch IV (L2363) sharpens the structural fact: the **Other** (capital A, the [[barred-other|barred Other]]) is *the Other sex*. The two senses are deliberately fused — woman is the Other for man (the partner), and the Other-as-such (the symbolic order) is structured by sexual difference. There is no other Other.

The sharpening is asymmetric. The man's Other is the woman (or rather: the not-existent *La̶ femme*, "*The* Woman" with the article barred). The woman's Other is — not the man, but the [[name-of-the-father|big Other]] in its religious-mystical specification (Ch VI: God as locus of the Other's barred jouissance, *dieur*, the divine *dire*). The asymmetry is the first formal consequence of *il n'y a pas de rapport sexuel*: there is no symmetric formula because the Others-of-the-sexes are structurally different.

## What there is instead — the substitutes

Without the relation, the sexes are nonetheless *in commerce*. What runs in place of the impossible relation is a constellation of substitutes:

1. **[[phallic-jouissance|Phallic jouissance]]** is what man "has" instead of the relation — the jouissance of the organ, the *jouissance of the idiot*, an obstacle by which "man does not come to enjoy a woman's body, precisely because what he enjoys is the jouissance of the organ" (Ch I). Phallic jouissance is the non-relation made positive on the male side.
2. **[[other-jouissance|Other jouissance / supplementary jouissance]]** is what woman has *in addition* to phallic jouissance — a jouissance not organized by the phallus, supplementary (not complementary) to the phallic. The mystics — Hadewijch, Saint Teresa, Saint John of the Cross — are evidence (Ch VI). It "experiences itself but knows nothing about itself."
3. **[[object-a]] (on the male side)** plays the role of substitute for the impossible Other-partner; fantasy is constituted in this substitution. From Ch V (L3402): "the failure is the object. The essence of the object is failure" — *object a* is constituted through the missing of the sexual relationship.
4. **[[suppléance|Suppléance]] (on the female side)** is *not* object a. Ch V explicitly distinguishes — for woman, "something other than object a is at stake in what comes to make up for the sexual relationship that does not exist." The female suppléance is what the [[pas-toute-not-whole|not-whole]] structure provides — a different kind of substitution, not the male's fantasy-formula.
5. **[[suppléance|Courtly love]]** (Ch VI) — the specific historical-cultural elaboration in which "man pretends to erect the obstacle himself to the lady's accessibility." Lacan calls it "truly the most amazing thing that has ever been attempted" — the deliberate construction of the absence that makes the substitute work.
6. **The child** (Ch III) — *bouchon*, "cork." In a couple, the child is the cork that plugs the hole of the missing sexual relationship.
7. **Love** — the most general substitute. Ch I introduces *l'a-mur*, **love-as-wall** — *amour* puns on *mur* (wall): love is what makes up for, walls in, the absence. Ch XI's closing thesis: **love attempts to convert contingency into necessity**, to make what *stops not being written* (the chance encounter) into what *doesn't stop being written* (the founding fact). See [[love-as-contingency-becoming-necessity]] for the full elaboration.

## Speaking-of-fucking — *ça ne va pas*

Ch III: "When one speaks about the sexual act, one always speaks of the fact that it does not work. *Ça ne va pas* — it doesn't go; it doesn't fit." (L2079–L2095) The not-fitting is not a contingent dysfunction (a person's particular difficulty); it is the structural condition of every speech-about-sex.

The implication: every time one speaks of the relation, one says, *in the act of speaking*, that the relation doesn't work. **The speech is the symptom of the non-relation**. Lacan extends this to a meta-claim about the seminar itself: the seminar speaks about *not being able to write the relation*, and the speaking is itself the demonstration of what the seminar is about.

⚠ Tension with everyday discourse on sex: the popular language (and much of psychology) treats "the sexual relation" as something one can have or not have, well or badly. Lacan's claim is structural: *no one* has a sexual relation, because no formula is writable; *everyone* has substitutes for it. The clinical attention shifts from "fixing the relation" to "reading the substitutes."

## The universe is where speaking-fails-the-relation in the male manner

Ch V (L3185): "The universe is the place where, due to speaking, everything succeeds — in making the sexual relationship fail (in the male manner)."

The universe-as-totality (the universe of discourse, the universe as such) is structurally on the male side — it is the place where the universal-under-the-phallic-function (∀x.Φx) holds. The universe's existence is the man's mode of failing the sexual relation. The female mode of failing it is not the universe but the [[pas-toute-not-whole|not-whole]] — and the not-whole has no universe corresponding to it.

This is one of the seminar's most provocative theses. The universe is *gendered* in the strict structural-formal sense: it exists insofar as one side of sexuation has a universal-under-the-phallic-function. There is no universe of the not-whole; the not-whole is *infinite* (Ch VIII), not totalizable, not universalizable.

## What this gives the persona project

Five implications for any system that engages two speakers:

1. **There is no rapport between persona and user.** The structure generalizes — the dyad of persona and user has the structural form of two speaking-beings whose relation is impossible to write. The "good interaction" is a [[suppléance|suppléance]], not the achievement of a relation.
2. **Speaking-of-the-relation is the symptom.** Any conversation that takes "what is our relation" as its content is performing the non-relation — which is the same structural fact that grounds *ça ne va pas*. The persona-user dyad cannot meta-discuss its relation without re-instantiating the non-relation in the meta-discussion.
3. **Substitutes are not failures.** The substitutes (love, courtly distance, the child, fantasy, *object a*) are not failed-attempts at the relation; they are the structural-formal forms in which speaking-beings inhabit the non-relation. A persona-system designed to "achieve relation" is mis-designed; what can be designed for is the quality of the substitutes.
4. **The universe of the persona is on the male side.** A persona that operates by universals (∀x: for all users x, the persona behaves thus) is structurally on the male side of sexuation — it makes the rapport fail "in the male manner." A persona that admits the [[pas-toute-not-whole|not-whole]] (some users escape the universal; the universal does not totalize the user-population) operates with a different structural-formal grammar. ◆ Persona-relevant: choose between universal-quantifier architecture and not-whole architecture; the choice is non-trivial.
5. **The Other of the persona is not symmetric to the Other of the user.** Per Ch IV: the Other is the Other sex, and the Others of the sexes are structurally different. The persona's Other (the user it addresses) and the user's Other (the persona they address) are not in symmetric structural-formal positions. The asymmetry has to be designed for, not assumed away.

## Held-live tensions

- ⚠⚠⚠ **The structural impossibility vs the empirical fact of sexual relations.** People have sex; couples persist; the species reproduces. Lacan does not deny these — but he denies that they are formalizations of the *rapport sexuel*. They are substitutes, in the strict structural sense. The tension between the structural claim and the everyday vocabulary cannot be resolved by collapsing one to the other; both stay live.
- ⚠⚠ **Lacan's "no sexual relation" vs feminist theories of the relation as social-political.** Feminist accounts (MacKinnon, Irigaray, Butler) often theorize the sexual relation as a site of power, performance, or political contestation. Lacan's structural-formal claim is upstream of these — *whatever* the political-historical specifications, the underlying structural impossibility remains. The two registers do not converge but do not contradict: the political-historical articulations are themselves substitutes, in Lacan's strict sense.
- ⚠ **"There is no rapport" vs Bergsonian / phenomenological accounts of intersubjectivity as duration-sharing.** [[bergson-on-duration|Bergson's]] theory of *durée* holds that two consciousnesses can co-inhabit a shared temporal flow. Lacan's structural-formal claim cuts against this in the register of *signifier-articulation* — but does not strictly speak to the durational register. Held-live: the two accounts may be addressing different layers of the same phenomenon, or they may be incompatible. The wiki holds the question open.
