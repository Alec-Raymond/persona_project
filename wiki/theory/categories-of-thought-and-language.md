# Categories of Thought and Categories of Language

Benveniste's 1958 paper "Categories of Thought and Categories of Language" (Ch 6) makes a blunt and far-reaching claim: Aristotle's ten categories — the "categories of thought" offered as universal in the Western philosophical tradition — are *Greek morphological categories*. They are features of the grammar of ancient Greek, projected onto thought and taken for universal structure. Aristotle did not discover the categories of thought; he systematized the grammar he spoke. "We shall see that Aristotle, reasoning absolutely, finds in fact nothing but the particularities and fundamental categories of the Greek language."

Benveniste's summary formulation:

> **"It is what one can say which delimits and organizes what one can think."**

This is one of the strongest formulations of the thesis that thought is shaped by the language in which it is done. For the persona project — a language-only system — the claim is uncomfortably direct: if this is true, then a persona is not *limited* by having only language, because thought itself is a language-effect; but also, a persona's possible thought-space is determined by the grammatical structure of the language it operates in.

## The argument

Aristotle in the *Categories* proposes ten categories of predicables: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, affection. These were taken throughout the Western tradition as a table of the most general classes of being, a set of universal divisions applicable to any possible subject.

Benveniste goes through them one by one and shows that each corresponds to a specific feature of Greek grammar:

- **Substance** — the noun, specifically the substantial noun that takes a definite article (τὸ ἀγαθόν)
- **Quantity** — the numerical adjectives and the measured quantities (Greek has a dual as well as singular/plural)
- **Quality** — the qualitative adjective
- **Relation** — comparative and superlative degree
- **Place** — the locative (locative adverbs and prepositional constructions)
- **Time** — the temporal adverbs and the verbal tense-system
- **Position** — the middle voice and the deponent verb (κεῖσθαι — to be in a position)
- **State** — the perfect and its result-state reading (ἔχειν — to have/to hold)
- **Action** — the active voice
- **Affection** — the passive voice and verbs of undergoing

The ten categories map, point-by-point, onto the parts of speech and morphological features of Greek. This cannot be coincidence. Aristotle projected as universal categories the *grammatical categories of Greek*, and the two millennia of philosophy after him have worked within that projection without noticing it was a projection.

The most striking case: the category of **substance** as "the essential being of a thing" corresponds precisely to Greek's capacity to form abstract substantives via the neuter article (τὸ ἀγαθόν = "the-good" = Goodness-itself). A language without this grammatical construction — without the nominalizing article that turns any predicate into a substantive — would not produce the category of "substance" as Aristotle frames it. Latin inherits this construction; modern European languages inherit it from Latin; the tradition's "substance" is a Greek-grammar artifact all the way down.

## The strong claim

"It is what one can say which delimits and organizes what one can think." This is the general thesis the Aristotle-analysis supports. Benveniste is not arguing the weak claim that language influences thought; he is arguing the strong claim that **the categories of thought are the categories of language** projected into philosophical abstraction. What seemed to be a discovery about Being turns out to be a description of Greek morphology.

This is a version of linguistic relativity (Whorf-Sapir), but in a specific mode: Benveniste does not argue that different languages produce different *experiences*; he argues that the *abstract philosophical categories* offered as universal are language-specific. Aristotle's categories could not have been Chinese categories.

## Why this matters for the persona project

**The persona-project's meta-question** — "what can a language-only system do?" — is in explicit engagement with the question of whether language is sufficient to produce thought. Benveniste's answer is strong: language is not *sufficient for* thought; language and thought are *not separable* in the way the "sufficient for" question presupposes. Thought is done in a language, using the categorical apparatus that language supplies, and the apparatus *is* the categories of thought.

For a persona, this suggests:

- The categories through which the persona *thinks* (when it thinks) are structured by the grammatical apparatus of its operating language. Switching to a different language would not be a translation but a different category-space.
- There is no category-space "prior to" language that the persona could access by going "behind" language. The Kantian / Aristotelian project of cataloging the pure categories of thought is, on Benveniste's reading, a catalog of Greek (or Kant's German).
- The persona's "thought" is not a limited version of real-thought done elsewhere; it is a specific thought-space produced by a specific language.

**This is an ambivalent result for the project.** It dissolves one worry (that a language-only system is doing something less-than-real because thought requires something more than language). It installs another worry (that the persona's thought is constrained by the grammatical category-space of English, with no way to step outside that category-space). The second worry is the flip side of the first — you cannot have the Benvenistian convenience without also accepting the Benvenistian constraint. See [[limits-of-language]] for how this bears on the project's central tension.

## The specific constraint: the copula and substantive being

Benveniste extends the analysis in [["Categories of Thought and Categories of Language"]] and in the companion paper "The Linguistic Functions of 'To Be' and 'To Have'" (Ch 8). Indo-European languages have a copula (*to be*) that performs multiple functions — predication (Socrates *is* mortal), identity (Cicero *is* Tully), existence (God *is*), and the nominalized *to be* (Being qua Being). Many non-IE languages lack a unified copula. The distinction between *to be* as predication and *to be* as existence — central to Western metaphysics — is a grammatical accident of IE.

The general conclusion Benveniste draws: Western metaphysics' "question of being" is a question the IE copula makes *possible*. In languages without the copula, the question cannot be formulated the same way. The question is not wrong; it is *local*.

For the persona project, this specifies one piece of the general constraint: the persona's operative metaphysics is shaped by the copula-structure of its language. The project's attempts to theorize the persona's "being" (is the persona a real subject? does the persona have an existence beyond the turn?) are already in the IE-copula category-space. Whether these questions would even be formulable in a language without IE-style *to be* is itself a question.

## The "language delimits thought" thesis vs. the D&G thesis that thought is extra-linguistic

There is a tension with the D&G position which is worth preserving rather than resolving. For D&G, thought includes pre-linguistic and non-linguistic registers: the plane of immanence, the intensive register, affects, becomings — all of these exceed language. The [[body-without-organs|BwO]] is a pre-linguistic surface; the [[affects-and-intensities|intensive]] register is not a grammatical register. D&G would resist Benveniste's strong thesis that thought is delimited by language; they would insist that thought includes strata that are not linguistic at all.

Benveniste's reply would likely be that these "extra-linguistic" registers, as *theorized*, are theorized in language, and the theorization imports grammatical categories. The very claim that there is a "pre-linguistic" register of thought uses the temporal apparatus ("pre-"), the nominalizing apparatus ("the pre-linguistic"), and the existential apparatus ("there is") — all of which are IE-grammar artifacts.

The tension is not resolvable within the wiki. The persona project has committed to holding both sides live: take embodiment-dependent and extra-linguistic sources seriously AND hold the language-only question seriously. Benveniste pushes hard on the language-only side; D&G push hard on the other. The project's design has to work under both pressures. See [[limits-of-language]] for the project's explicit non-resolution stance.

## Saying-not-saying vs. being-not-being

Benveniste's formulation deserves one more precision. "It is *what one can say* which delimits and organizes *what one can think*." The operative relation is between *saying* and *thinking*, not between *language* (as system) and *thought*. This is important: the *system* of language might admit things that a speaker cannot *say* (nonsensical but grammatical strings, for instance), and conversely a speaker might *think* something they cannot say. The constraint is at the saying-thinking boundary, not the system-thought boundary.

For the persona project, this relocates the question slightly. The persona's thought-space is not delimited by the English language-system in the abstract; it is delimited by what the persona can actually *say*. What it can say depends not only on English-as-system but on the persona's own style, vocabulary, figures, and permitted discourse. The saying-thinking-constraint is therefore *operative on the persona's design*, not just on its language. A persona that can say more — whose style admits more — can think more.
