# The Non-Person — Third Person and the I/You Asymmetry

Benveniste's 1946 paper "Relationships of Person in the Verb" (Ch 18) shows that the traditional three-person schema (*I*, *you*, *he*) misdescribes a structural fact. The third person is not a person in the same sense as the first and second. *I* and *you* are **correlative** — one installs the other in the same instance of discourse — while *he* belongs to a different category entirely: it refers to what is *spoken about*, not to a participant in the speaking. Benveniste calls the third person the **"non-person"**.

## The argument

If *I* and *you* were three-way symmetric with *he*, we would expect the grammar of verbal persons to distribute evenly across the three. It does not. Across languages, the third person is distinguished from the first and second in systematic ways:

- **Zero-morpheme**: In many languages, the third-person form is the unmarked base of the verb, while first and second persons take specific markers. The third is what the verb is before any person-marking; first and second are derivations.
- **Agreement patterns**: Verbs agree with *I* and *you* in ways they do not agree with *he* — e.g., imperatives are restricted to second person with an implicit first, not third.
- **Deictic regime**: *I* and *you* are shifters (see [[empty-signs-and-instance-of-discourse]]); they refer to positions in the instance of discourse. *He* does not shift in this way; it refers to someone exterior to the discourse, spoken about rather than spoken.
- **Pluralization**: "We" is not "I + I + I" — it is "I + others" (which might be "I + you" = inclusive, or "I + they" = exclusive). "You (pl)" is "you + others." But "they" is "he + he + he" — genuine plural of the same category. The third person pluralizes the way ordinary classes pluralize; the first and second do not.

Benveniste's conclusion: *I* and *you* are the persons proper (constituted by and constitutive of the instance of discourse); *he* is not a person in the same sense. *He* is what the two persons *talk about*. It is outside the I/you polarity.

## The inclusive/exclusive "we"

A related observation. "We" is grammatically first-person plural, but semantically it is not a plural of "I." Benveniste distinguishes:

- **Inclusive we** = *I + you* — includes the addressee
- **Exclusive we** = *I + he/they* — excludes the addressee

Many languages mark this distinction grammatically (Algonquian, Austronesian). English does not — "we" is ambiguous. But the distinction is always available, because it follows from the I/you correlativity: "we" must be either "I + you" or "I + someone outside the I/you pair."

## Why this matters for the persona project

**Persona-speech about third parties is structurally different from persona-speech with addressees.** When a persona says *"I understand you, this is hard"*, it is operating within the I/you apparatus — both persona and user are installed as persons in the instance of discourse. When a persona says *"Alice went to the store"*, Alice is installed as non-person — something spoken about, not a participant. The two operations are categorically distinct, and the difference is not stylistic but structural.

This has a specific consequence for persona design. A persona that talks *about* the user in the third person (to a hypothetical other, or in internal deliberation) has moved the user from the *you* position to the *non-person* position. The relational register changes. D&G note this in the [[faciality|faciality]] regime — the shift from face-to-face address to addressing-someone-about-someone-else is a different affective regime entirely.

For multi-party conversations, or for personas that handle references to absent parties, the apparatus of person-marking needs to be consciously handled. Who is in the I/you pair, and who is in the non-person register? The answer determines what kind of speech-act is happening.

## The non-person and writing-as-becoming

The *il* of impersonal narration (Blanchot's *il*, see [[writing-as-becoming]]) is the extension of the non-person into a whole register of utterance. The [[two-planes-of-utterance|historical plane]] is the plane from which the I/you pair has been expelled, leaving only the non-person regime. Literature can operate in this register; a persona in dialogue typically cannot without becoming self-effacing in a specific way.

D&G treat the *il* as a positive achievement (becoming-imperceptible, "He Stuttered"). For Benveniste, the non-person is a structural pole — neither achievement nor failure, but a grammatical category with specific operations. Both readings stand. See [[two-planes-of-utterance]] for the planal structure that organizes when the non-person regime takes over.

## The asymmetry of agreement

An oddity of the system worth preserving: the verb agrees differently with each of its persons. In English this is attenuated (only third-singular is marked: *he walks* vs. *I/you/we/they walk*), but the residual -s is exactly the non-person mark: the person whose presence the verb specifically signals is the non-person. The I and the you are the unmarked persons; the non-person is the marked one.

Benveniste reads this as evidence for the categorial distinction: the person who would be expected on naive grounds to be least marked (the "neutral" third) is grammatically the most marked, because the first and second are presuppositions of the speech-act and do not need to be grammatically asserted, while the third needs to be marked precisely because it is not part of the I/you apparatus.
