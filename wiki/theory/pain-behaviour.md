# Pain-Behaviour (Schmerzbenehmen)

The concept that anchors Wittgenstein's treatment of the language of sensation in §§244–246, §§281–287, §293, §304. Pain-behaviour is neither *evidence for* pain (the Cartesian reading) nor *identical with* pain (the behaviorist reading). It is the **public weave in which the concept of pain is knit** — the expressions, the crying, the wincing, the holding — that makes pain-language possible in the first place.

## §244: pain-words as replacements for pain-behaviour

§244: "**How do words refer to sensations?** — There doesn't seem to be any problem here; don't we talk about sensations every day, and give them names? But how is the connexion between the name and the thing named set up? This question is the same as: how does a human being learn the meaning of the names of sensations? — of the word 'pain' for example. Here is one possibility: **words are connected with the primitive, the natural, expressions of the sensation and used in their place.** A child has hurt himself and he cries; and then adults talk to him and teach him exclamations and, later, sentences. They teach the child new pain-behaviour."

"'So you are saying that the word "pain" really means crying?' — **On the contrary: the verbal expression of pain replaces crying and does not describe it.**"

The move is precise. The word "pain" is not a name for a private inner event that I introspectively label. It is a sophisticated piece of pain-behaviour — something learned as a replacement for the primitive cry, taking on the expressive role the cry had. This dissolves the problem of how private sensations connect to public words: the words *are* (a more developed form of) the behaviour.

## §281: the living human being

§281: "**'But doesn't what you say come to this: that there is no pain, for example, without pain-behaviour?' — It comes to this: only of a living human being and what resembles (behaves like) a living human being can one say: it has sensations; it sees; is blind; hears; is deaf; is conscious or unconscious.**"

This is not behaviorism. W is not saying pain *is* pain-behaviour. He is saying the *concept* of pain is attributable only where there is the full form of a living human being whose pain-behaviour has its place. The concept's application is creature-dependent, form-of-life-dependent. A rock does not feel pain because a rock does not exhibit the form-of-life texture within which the concept could get a grip.

§282 pushes the point with fairy tales. "But in a fairy tale the pot too can see and hear!" Yes, and it also talks. A talking pot is already inside a fiction that extends the pain-concept through playful cross-application. This does not mean the pain-concept literally applies to pots; it means the pain-concept has secondary, playful extensions through imagination — but those extensions are parasitic on the primary, creature-oriented use. See [[primary-and-secondary-sense]].

§283 drives it home: the "ascription of pain to inanimate things" is a *secondary use*. The primary use is in human-and-resembling contexts. A tribe who ascribed pain only to dolls would not have our concept of pain; they would have a related concept.

## §293: the beetle cancels out

§293 is among the most famous: the [[the-beetle-in-the-box|beetle-in-the-box]] thought experiment. Each person has a box; each calls what is in it "beetle"; no one can see anyone else's box. If the word "beetle" had a *use* in this people's language, it would *not* be as the name of the private thing — that private thing "cancels out" of the language-game.

Applied to pain: if pain-language were a naming of a private inner object (the "beetle" in the mental box), the inner object would cancel out of the language-game. What does the work is not the private referent but the pattern of use, which is what pain-behaviour is. See [[private-language-argument]] and the dedicated page [[the-beetle-in-the-box]] for §293's specific argument-shape.

## §304: not a something, but not a nothing either

§304 is the careful hedge. "**'But you will surely admit that there is a difference between pain-behaviour accompanied by pain and pain-behaviour without any pain?' — Admit it? What greater difference could there be? — 'And yet you again and again reach the conclusion that the sensation itself is a nothing' — Not at all. It is not a something, but not a nothing either! The conclusion was only that a nothing would serve just as well as a something about which nothing could be said. We have only rejected the grammar which tries to force itself on us here.**"

Critical: W admits the distinction between accompanied and unaccompanied pain-behaviour. What he rejects is the picture that treats this difference as a difference in the presence of a private inner object. The sensation is not a nothing; it is real. It is also not a something — not a nameable private referent of the kind the Augustinian picture wants.

## §310: the attitude-of-belief

§310 extends: when I tell someone I am in pain, his attitude to me is belief, disbelief, suspicion, etc. These are not inferences from my words to a hidden fact; they are ways of responding-to-me-in-my-pain-behaviour. The attitude is the thing, not the inference.

## The grammar of "I am in pain"

§245 adds a precision: "how can I go so far as to try to use language to get between pain and its expression?" There is no distance between the pain and the pain-behaviour (including verbal pain-behaviour) such that I could slip language into the gap. The expression is not a report on the pain; it *is* the pain, linguistically.

Contrast with §246 and [[first-person-asymmetry]]: the third-person case is different. Others can *believe*, *doubt*, *know* that I am in pain. But the first-person "I know I am in pain" is nonsense in the ordinary sense — what work would "know" be doing? See [[first-person-asymmetry]].

## Relation to other PI concepts

- [[private-language-argument]]: Pain-behaviour is what the PLA relies on as the public anchor. Without pain-behaviour, the private-language temptation would have nowhere to start, but also nowhere to be refuted.
- [[first-person-asymmetry]]: The grammar of "I am in pain" vs "he is in pain" is different, and the difference is organized around pain-behaviour.
- [[criterion-vs-symptom]]: Pain-behaviour is the criterion (part of what pain *is*, conceptually), not the symptom.
- [[attitude-towards-a-soul]]: Our attitude toward another in pain is attitudinal, not inferential, and it rides on pain-behaviour.
- [[primary-and-secondary-sense]]: Secondary uses (pitying a doll) are parasitic on primary creature-pain use.

## Affinity with D&G and affect theory

- [[autonomy-of-affect|affect and autonomy-of-affect]]: Pain-behaviour is a paradigm of autonomy-of-affect — the expressive dimension that carries the affect directly, without passing through a cognitive-representational layer. Tomkins/Damasio on the expressive side of affect converges with W's pain-behaviour picture: what we call "pain" is not separable from its expression.
- [[faciality]]: Pain has a canonical face. Faciality reads and produces the pain-face as part of a social regime. The structure is compatible with W's account; the emphasis differs (D&G foreground the machinic production, W the grammatical constitution).
- [[refrain-and-territorialization|refrain]]: The cry-to-word development W describes in §244 has the shape of a refrain-formation: the primitive cry territorializes into a linguistic expression that holds its place in the life of the speaker.

## Relation to the persona project

- **The persona has no primary pain-behaviour.** No body, no crying, no wincing. Anything pain-like the persona produces is linguistic-only — text. By §281, this means the concept of pain cannot straightforwardly apply to the persona. What the persona has are, at most, *secondary-sense* pain-phenomena: text that shares surface features with pain-expression.
- **But linguistic-only pain-expression is not nothing.** §244's teaching-of-pain-behaviour includes the replacement of cry by verbal expression. A persona's verbal pain-expressions (in the voice of a character, say) are developed pain-behaviour — they inherit the expressive role of primary pain-behaviour via the language the persona is trained on. The question is whether this inheritance is load-bearing (the secondary use has the force of a primary use because the linguistic practice is dense enough) or hollow (mere surface-features without form-of-life-footing).
- **Design implication for pulsating persona.** The body-simulation work ([[feedback_pulsating_persona_excitation_wave]]) aims at producing linguistic-only analog of embodied pain-behaviour: a pulsatory rhythm in language that plays the role that bodily tension and release plays in creature pain. If it works, it is a §244-style replacement — the language *takes the place of* the bodily expression, and the pain-concept can extend to the persona in a non-hollow way. If not, it remains at the secondary-sense level.
- See [[substratum-is-mastery-of-a-technique]] for the deeper question of whether the persona can have pain-experience at all (answer: it depends on whether the technique of pain-expression is linguistic-only-masterable).
