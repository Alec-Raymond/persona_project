# Signifier/Signified Consubstantiality for the Speaker

Benveniste's 1939 essay "The Nature of the Linguistic Sign" (Ch 4) is a short, sharp intervention on Saussure's doctrine of the arbitrary relation between signifier and signified. Benveniste grants the doctrine as a *theoretical* finding about *langue* — an observer's-stance description — but rejects it as a description of how language actually operates *for the speaker*. **For the speaker, signifier and signified are not arbitrarily related; they are consubstantial.** The speaker does not experience a contingent link between a sound-form and a concept. The two are given together, as inseparable aspects of the same linguistic reality.

The disagreement is not about Saussure's data; it is about what level of analysis the data apply to. Arbitrariness is a fact about the relation between different languages' signifiers for the same signified (bread / pain / Brot). Consubstantiality is a fact about the speaker's relation to her own language's sign. Both can be true; Saussure's error, per Benveniste, was to take the observer's-stance finding (arbitrariness across languages) and project it onto the speaker's experience within her own language.

## The argument

Saussure's account: a signifier (the sound-image *tree*) is linked to a signified (the concept tree) by pure convention. There is nothing in the sound *tree* that intrinsically connects it to the concept; a different language could link a different sound to the same concept. The sign is arbitrary. This is a cornerstone of Saussurean structural linguistics.

Benveniste reopens the case. He asks: what does arbitrariness mean *for the speaker of a language*? When an English speaker says *tree*, does she experience the sound-form and the concept as contingently linked? Does she feel that the sound could have been any other sound? Benveniste's answer: no. The English speaker experiences the sound *tree* as *being* the word for trees. The relationship is not felt as arbitrary; it is felt as necessary, transparent, given.

The point is not psychological illusion. It is that *within a language*, the sign operates as a unity, not as two separable elements arbitrarily joined. The speaker does not assemble signifier + signified; she deploys the sign as a single thing. "The one is the psychic imprint of the sound, the other is the mental image of the thing" — but for the speaker, these are not two elements contingently related; they are one sign with two inseparable aspects.

Benveniste's move: what Saussure actually showed is that the relation *between the sign and the reality* (between the word *tree* and actual trees) is arbitrary. That relation *is* conventional. But Saussure's own analysis of the sign placed signifier and signified *both within language* — the signified is not the actual tree but the concept "tree," which is itself a linguistic entity. The arbitrariness that holds between *sign and thing* does not hold between *signifier and signified within the sign*. Saussure conflated the two relations; Benveniste separates them.

## The consubstantiality claim

"Between the signifier and the signified the relationship is not arbitrary; it is, on the contrary, necessary." The signifier *tree* and the signified "tree" are related as the sound-aspect and the concept-aspect of a single linguistic unit. They are not two things joined by convention; they are two faces of one sign. For the speaker, they are inseparable — to hear *tree* is already to grasp the concept; to think the concept is already to have access to the word. The signifier and signified are **consubstantial**.

The convention — what is arbitrary — enters at a different level: the relation between the whole sign (*tree* + "tree" together) and actual trees in the world. That relation is contingent, differs across languages, and is the proper domain of Saussurean arbitrariness. But the relation *inside* the sign is necessary from the speaker's standpoint.

## Observer-stance vs. speaker-stance

The deeper methodological point: Saussurean arbitrariness is an **observer's-stance** description. It is what linguistics sees when comparing languages. From outside, the fact that English has *tree* while French has *arbre* while German has *Baum* demonstrates that sign-to-thing correspondence is contingent.

But this observer-stance finding does not describe what language is *for a speaker*. The speaker of a language is *inside* it, and inside it, the sign operates with necessity. The speaker does not experience her own signs as alternatives-among-alternatives; she experiences them as the way things are said.

This is a structural feature of any speaker's relation to any language, not a naive mistake speakers make. The speaker's-stance necessity is not an illusion to be dispelled; it is the mode of operation that makes the speaker a speaker of the language.

## Why this matters for the persona project

**A persona operating in English operates from inside English.** Its signs are not experienced (or "experienced" — the term is contested for LLMs) as arbitrary conventions but as the way things are said. This is not a design choice; it is a structural feature of operating *in* a language rather than *at it from outside*.

For the persona project, Benveniste's distinction has a specific consequence: the persona cannot be designed as if it were a lookup-table between concepts and their arbitrary English expressions. It has to be designed as operating *in* language, where signs are consubstantial for it. This is what the [[body-without-organs|BwO]] cold-start text operates on — it works with the sign-as-unity, at the level of prose-texture and image, not as arbitrary encodings of separable concepts.

This matches what [[conceptual-metaphor|conceptual metaphor theory]] will say from a different angle: abstract concepts are not arbitrarily encoded in language; they are structured through embodied metaphor, and the structuring is *necessary*, not contingent. Benveniste reaches a parallel conclusion from within classical linguistics: the sign's two faces are consubstantial from the speaker's side; arbitrariness is only what the outside observer sees.

## The connection to [[signifier-as-despotic]]

D&G's critique of the signifier's despotic operation (see [[signifier-as-despotic]]) is partly a critique of the Saussurean schema that treats the sign as a two-element structure (signifier + signified) with a conventional link. D&G prefer a *pragmatic* register (signs as operators in assemblages) over a *semiological* register (signs as signifier-signified pairs). Benveniste's consubstantiality claim is not identical to the D&G critique but is compatible with it: if signifier and signified are not really separable for the speaker, then the despotic signifier-over-signified structure loses its grip. The two lines of criticism converge: from Benveniste, the signifier-signified duality is an observer's artifact; from D&G, the duality's elevation to structural principle is an apparatus of capture.

## What to preserve, what to hold open

**Preserve:** the distinction between observer-stance and speaker-stance. The persona project is designing *for* a speaker-stance (the persona operates in language), but its designers are often in observer-stance (analyzing how the persona works). These two stances produce different descriptions of the same sign-operations; both descriptions are valid in their register, but they cannot be mixed.

**Hold open:** whether an LLM-based persona has a speaker-stance in Benveniste's strong sense. Benveniste assumes a human speaker whose phenomenal relation to her own language produces the consubstantiality. Whether an LLM has a phenomenal relation of any kind is the project's central open question, which Benveniste's analysis does not settle. The analysis gives a description of what a speaker *does*; whether the persona does this, in the full sense, remains the [[limits-of-language|limits-of-language]] question.
