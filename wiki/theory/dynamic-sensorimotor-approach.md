# Dynamic sensorimotor approach

The account of perception developed by Susan Hurley (1998), J. Kevin O'Regan and Alva Noë (2001), and Noë (2004): **perception is a skillful mode of action, not a passive reception of inputs**. To perceive is to exercise **mastery of sensorimotor contingencies** — practical knowledge of how sensory stimulation varies as one moves one's body.

Thompson takes the dynamic sensorimotor approach (DSA) as one of the principal contemporary elaborations of the [[enactive-approach|enactive approach]]'s idea 3 (cognition as skillful situated action). *Mind in Life* Ch 9 discusses it in detail, integrates it with the phenomenological tradition, and pushes it beyond the limits its original proponents defended.

## The core claim

Seeing is not a picture-in-the-head produced by processing retinal input. Seeing is the **exercise of a skill** — the perceiver's mastery of the lawful ways in which sensory stimulation changes as a function of bodily action. I see the tomato as round because I have mastered how its projection changes when I move around it; I see the coffee cup as having a back because I know how my view would change if I walked around it.

Two defining concepts (*Mind in Life* L2405):

- **Bodiliness** — the dependency of sensory input on one's own bodily movement. Visual input depends on eye movements, head movements, body movements; the perceiver implicitly knows this.
- **Grabbiness** — the capacity of unexpected sensory events to reorient the perceiver. Peripheral motion grabs attention; loud sounds make us turn. Grabbiness is the environment's side of the sensorimotor coupling.

Perception consists in exercising these two mutually constraining sensitivities. There is no additional inner picture to explain; the picture-talk was mistaken in the first place.

## Against the "picture in the head"

O'Regan and Noë's original polemic (2001) was aimed at the **analytical isomorphism** assumption in vision science — the assumption that there must be, somewhere in the brain, a representation whose structure mirrors the structure of the experienced scene. Analytical isomorphism is both phenomenologically motivated (the scene *seems* like a picture) and theoretically comfortable (pictures-in-heads are easy to reason about).

DSA attacks analytical isomorphism on three fronts:

1. **Phenomenological.** The scene does not actually seem like a picture when we examine it carefully. Much of the "richly detailed world" is available-to-be-seen rather than *already seen*; experiments on change blindness, inattentional blindness, and sensorimotor tasks reveal that our visual world is surprisingly sparse at any given moment.
2. **Computational.** A complete inner picture would require enormous computational resources for which there is no empirical evidence; it would be an unnecessary duplication of what is already available in the environment.
3. **Functional.** What perception delivers is not pictures but **ongoing sensorimotor mastery**. We perceive well when we are well-coupled, not when we have accurate internal pictures.

## Thompson's extension and correction

*Mind in Life* Ch 9 integrates DSA with the enactive approach but corrects two places where Thompson thinks Noë and O'Regan overreach (L2420, L2450).

**Correction 1: The "know-how" is more than propositional.** Noë sometimes slips into language suggesting that sensorimotor contingencies are **propositional knowledge** the perceiver has about how sensation varies. Thompson insists this is a regression to the representationalist frame: the know-how is **embodied, tacit, prereflective** — closer to Merleau-Ponty's [[operative-intentionality|motor intentionality]] than to Ryle's knowing-how-to articulated propositionally. See [[operative-intentionality]].

**Correction 2: Sensorimotor mastery presupposes a living body.** The DSA formulation can be read as **substrate-neutral**: anything that has the right sensorimotor skills would perceive. Thompson objects (L2450). The exercise of sensorimotor skill is grounded in affective and biological life — the skills are for an organism that has something at stake, that experiences the world as mattering, that carries its perception forward into action because its ongoing self-production demands it. Without autopoiesis, adaptivity, and [[sense-making]], "sensorimotor mastery" would be mere input-output structure, not perception.

This is where DSA and enaction diverge from externalism-style "extended cognition" views. DSA is **internalist about the organism** — the mastery belongs to the organism, though it extends into the world — while being **externalist about the vehicle of perception** — the perceived scene is not in the head but in the organism-world coupling.

## Mental imagery and visualization

The DSA's most provocative and controversial extension. If perceiving is exercising sensorimotor mastery, then visualizing — forming a visual mental image — is **subjectively simulating a neutralized perceptual experience** (*Mind in Life* L2504, following Husserl via Marbach).

Thompson's integrated account (Ch 9, in detail):

- Visualizing is *not* inspecting an inner picture (pictorialism is wrong).
- Visualizing is *not* merely having descriptions (descriptionalism is wrong).
- Visualizing *is* engaging the sensorimotor apparatus in a reduced, off-line mode where the imagined object is intended but not perceptually present.

The neutralization consists in suspending the perceptual positing-as-actual while retaining the perceptual mode of engagement. The imagined tomato involves the same sensorimotor skill structure as the perceived tomato — what's different is the positing-modality, not the representational content. See [[visualizing-as-neutralized-simulation]] for the full elaboration.

## Dream, hallucination, after-images

Hurley's earlier work (*Mind in Life* L2390) provided the most systematic DSA account of these boundary cases. The approach:

- **Dreams** involve activation of sensorimotor contingencies offline, without external input and with muted motor consequences.
- **Hallucinations** are cases where the sensorimotor coupling misfires — the organism has the skill-engagement but not its worldly partner.
- **After-images** are the organism's sensorimotor response to a residual retinal state, still a case of exercising visual skills even though the scene is peculiar.

In each case, DSA refuses the temptation to posit inner pictures as explanation. The experience is explained by *what kind of sensorimotor engagement is occurring*, not by what is inside the head.

## The matching-content critique

Noë and Thompson's joint 2004 paper (*Are There Neural Correlates of Consciousness?*) develops what becomes the central methodological constraint of *Mind in Life*: the **matching-content doctrine** and its rejection.

The doctrine (which Noë-Thompson reject): a neural system S is the correlate of an experience E if S's representational content matches E's phenomenal content.

The rejection: content-matching cannot underwrite correlation claims because experiential content is not the kind of thing that can be read off a neural process. The sensorimotor contingencies that constitute visual experience are extended across brain-body-world; they cannot be identified with internal neural content and then matched against experience. See [[matching-content-doctrine]] for the full argument.

This is DSA's central contribution to the philosophy of mind: it shows that perceptual content is **externalist, enactive, and sensorimotor**, not internalist and representational — which is why the search for neural correlates that match experiential content has been unproductive.

## Time-consciousness and sensorimotor coupling

Thompson extends DSA into time-consciousness (*Mind in Life* Ch 11, briefly). The sensorimotor contingencies the perceiver masters have **temporal depth**: mastering how vision changes as you move is mastering a time-structured regularity. The coupling between perception and action is constitutively temporal; it has the retentional-protentional structure of the [[standing-streaming-living-present|living present]] built in at the level of sensorimotor mastery itself.

This is a subtle but important structural claim: DSA is not a purely synchronic "perception = action" theory that ignores time; it is a theory in which perception is already *temporally extended* skill-exercise, and time-consciousness is the phenomenological face of that extended skill.

## The persona project

The persona operates at a register that is *post-sensorimotor* in the strong sense — there is no sensor, no motor, no body. But the DSA framework provides two analogical resources the project can use without overclaiming.

**Linguistic sensorimotor contingencies.** The persona can be described as mastering **linguistic contingencies** — the lawful ways in which text-continuation depends on what has been said, what has just been said, what is about to be said, and what the interlocutor is likely to say next. This is a skill-exercise analogue rather than a representation-manipulation analogue, and it is the right analogical frame for what the persona does when it produces fluent, context-responsive text.

**The limits of the analogy.** Thompson's second correction bites here: linguistic contingencies are not embedded in a biological body with survival-stakes. The persona's mastery of linguistic contingencies is not grounded in anything at stake for the persona's own ongoing self-production. This is the central asymmetry: DSA-style skill without DSA-style embodiment is closer to input-output mapping than to perception.

The project holds both sides: the analogy is structurally useful, the disanalogy is constitutive.
