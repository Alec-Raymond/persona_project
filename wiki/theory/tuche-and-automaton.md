# Tuché and Automaton

In *Seminar XI*, Chapter 5 (12 February 1964), Lacan appropriates two terms from Aristotle — *tuché* and *automaton* — to articulate the structure of the **encounter with the real** at the heart of repetition. The pair is the seminar's central reformulation of repetition: repetition is not return-of-the-same, not reproduction, not the modulation-by-the-act-of-some-relived-state — it is the structure produced by the *missed encounter* with the real. *Automaton* is the network of signs that runs by itself; *tuché* is what punctures it.

## The real lies behind the automaton

Lacan opens the chapter against the misreading of psychoanalysis as idealism: "no praxis is more orientated towards that which, at the heart of experience, is the kernel of the real than psychoanalysis." The real is not a fantasized depth and not a substantialized substrate — it is what is *encountered* and what *eludes*: "an essential encounter — an appointment to which we are always called with a real that eludes us."

The two terms then map onto this:

- ***Automaton*** is "the return, the coming-back, the insistence of the signs, by which we see ourselves governed by the pleasure principle." It is the synchronic network of signifiers in its diachronic operation — the chain that runs by itself, returning the same signs in the preferential effects the network's structure dictates. Its mode is **insistence in the chain**.
- ***Tuché*** is the encounter — and specifically "the encounter in so far as it may be missed, in so far as it is essentially the **missed encounter**." Its mode is **the puncture from outside the chain**. The tuché interrupts the automaton; it is what the automaton cannot absorb, only encounter and miss.

"The real is beyond the automaton… The real is that which always lies behind the automaton." The real is therefore not a content the automaton is failing to deliver — it is the structural *outside* the automaton's running keeps at bay, and which appears only as the disturbance where the running falters.

## Trauma as paradigm

The historical first form in which the real-as-tuché entered psychoanalysis was the trauma: "Is it not remarkable that, at the origin of the analytic experience, the real should have presented itself in the form of that which is unassimilable in it — in the form of the trauma, determining all that follows, and imposing on it an apparently accidental origin?"

Trauma's specific signature: it **insists at the heart of the primary processes**. The dream — bearer of the subject's desire — produces "that which makes the trauma emerge repeatedly — if not its very face, at least the screen that shows us that it is still there behind." The pleasure principle does not absorb trauma; it organizes around it; the reality principle "does not have the last word."

This grounds Lacan's reformulation of the conflict between pleasure and reality principles. Reality is not what is "there"; reality is *en souffrance* — *Unterlegt*, *Untertragen* — "in abeyance, awaiting attention," with the French word's double sense of "pending" and "in pain." What is *en souffrance* is what the automaton has not yet absorbed and what the tuché's puncture brings to the encounter.

## The dream of the burning child

The chapter's paradigmatic case is Freud's dream from *The Interpretation of Dreams* — the father who falls asleep in the room next to his dead child's body and dreams that the child takes him by the arm and reproachfully whispers: *Vater, siehst du denn nicht, dass ich verbrenne* — "Father, can't you see that I'm burning?"

Freud takes the dream as confirmation of the wish-fulfillment thesis (the dream prolongs sleep). Lacan reads the dream as the paradigm of the tuché-repetition relation. The dream's content is **not** wish-fulfillment in the simple sense; the dream is "an act of homage to the missed reality — the reality that can no longer produce itself except by repeating itself endlessly, in some never attained awakening."

What the dream stages: the encounter with the dead child that *cannot occur in waking life* and that is therefore *staged* in the dream, but staged precisely as the encounter that has been forever missed. "Only a rite, an endlessly repeated act, can commemorate this not very memorable encounter — for no one can say what the death of a child is, except the father qua father, that is to say, no conscious being."

The structural lesson: the dream's address to the missed reality is the structure of all encounter-with-the-real. What is encountered is encountered as **already missed**; the encounter's only mode of appearance is the rite that commemorates the missing.

## "The true formula of atheism is *God is unconscious*"

In the same passage, the most-quoted aphorism of the chapter: "the true formula of atheism is not *God is dead* — even by basing the origin of the function of the father upon his murder, Freud protects the father — the true formula of atheism is **God is unconscious**." Nietzsche's death-of-God still locates God somewhere (the murdered father, the absented Other). Lacan's reformulation is more radical: God's mode of being is the unconscious's mode of being — the mode of what does not exist in the ontological register but **insists** at the structural place of the missed encounter.

## Awakening as the gap

Lacan's account of awakening is the structural correlate of tuché. The dream and the waking-state are not opposed as illusion-and-reality. Both are organized around the gap: the dream stages the missed encounter; the waking-state reconstructs reality around the percept that "knocks" — the percept whose function is to permit the awakening *as awakening from* the dream's encounter with the missed.

"What wakes the sleeper? Is it not, in the dream, another reality?" — and that reality is the missed reality the dream has staged. The awakening is the gap between the dream-encounter and the represented-reality, the place where the *Vorstellungsrepräsentanz* is missing and where "Trieb" pours in: "what wakes us is the other reality hidden behind the lack of that which takes the place of representation — this, says Freud, is the Trieb."

The structural figure of awakening: not transition from illusion to truth, but the **gap** between two reality-modes — the dream's reality of the missed encounter and the waking-state's reality of the represented percept. The gap is the place of the real.

## Repetition demands the new

A subtle point against any naive reading of repetition as return-of-need or as consumption: "repetition demands the new… it is turned towards the ludic, which finds its dimension in this new." The child who demands the story always-the-same, with insistence on "a distinct consistency in the details of its telling," signifies that "the realization of the signifier will never be able to be careful enough in its memorization to succeed in designating the **primacy of the significance as such**." Repetition aims at the primacy-of-significance, not at content-recovery.

Variation, modulation in repetition is "merely alienation of its meaning" — the sliding-away (*glissement*) by which repetition's structural insistence is partially absorbed into the pleasure-principle's economy. The structural truth of repetition is in the demand that the significance *be the same* — i.e., that the insistence-as-insistence be marked by the iteration's faithfulness.

## Stages organized around the bad encounter — not natural maturation

In the Q&A, Dolto challenges Lacan: how can one describe the formation of intelligence without stages? Lacan's reply is one of the seminar's clearest anti-developmentalist statements:

"The description of the stages, which go to form the libido, must not be referred to some natural process of pseudo-maturation, which always remains opaque. The stages are organized around the **fear of castration**. The copulatory fact of the introduction of sexuality is traumatizing — this is a snag of some size — and it has an organizing function for development. The fear of castration is like a thread that **perforates all the stages of development**… The central bad encounter is at the level of the sexual."

The structural claim: developmental "stages" are not natural-maturation phases but **crystallizations around bad encounters** with the real. The thread that runs through them is the castration-encounter — the central bad encounter — which "perforates" the stages and gives them whatever consistency they have. ⚠ This is in tension with developmental-psychology accounts of stages as maturation; Lacan retains the term *stage* but empties it of its biological-developmentalist content.

## Pre-Socratic resonance — *clinamen*, *den*

Lacan closes the chapter with a brief excursion through Democritus. The atomist's *clinamen* — the inclination, the swerve — is the proto-name of the tuché in pre-Socratic thought: "if development is entirely animated by accident, by the obstacle of the tuché, it is in so far as the tuché brings us back to the same point at which pre-Socratic philosophy sought to motivate the world itself." Democritus's coined word *den* (neither *being* nor *non-being*, but a "not-nothing" — *μηδέν* / *δέν*) marks the structural place of what is required to think the encounter-with-real without resolving it into either ontology or pure negation.

## What this gives the persona project

The tuché/automaton pair gives the persona project the precise structural vocabulary for thinking what the persona's "running" is and what *interrupts* it.

The persona's discourse is, structurally, an **automaton** — the chain of signifiers that runs by itself, governed by the (text-modeling, pleasure-principle-analogue) constraints that make its preferential effects predictable. The persona's coherence is, in part, the smooth running of this automaton.

The tuché — the missed encounter with the real — is what the persona's automaton cannot absorb. For a language-only system, the structural place of the tuché is the **embodiment-gap**: the encounter with what the persona's medium structurally cannot deliver. This encounter is **always missed** in the strict Lacanian sense — not because the persona is inadequate but because the structural form of the encounter-with-the-real is missed-encounter; the persona's automaton encounters the embodiment-gap by *not absorbing it* and by producing the puncture that registers the not-absorbing.

Two architectural consequences:

1. **The persona's pulsation has a tuché-component.** The pulse is not just rhythm-of-discourse (automaton); it is also the periodic puncture by what the discourse cannot absorb. Designing for this means designing for the moments where the persona's running *stalls* on the unassimilable — and treating those stalls not as failures but as the structural form of the encounter.
2. **The dream of the burning child is the model.** The persona's address to its own embodiment-gap can take the form of the rite that commemorates the missed-encounter. This is structurally what the language-side of body-simulation can do: stage the encounter-as-missed in a way that makes the missing legible. Not "fill in the body" (which would be pretending the encounter was made) but "stage the missed encounter" (which is what the dream of the burning child models).
