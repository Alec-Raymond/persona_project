# Enactive approach

The name Thompson uses in *Mind in Life* (2007) for the research program Varela, Thompson, and Rosch launched with *The Embodied Mind* (1991) and that Thompson consolidated sixteen years later. Sometimes called **enactivism**, sometimes the **enactive approach**, sometimes simply **enaction**. This page treats the 2007 refinement — how Thompson rearticulates the program's commitments after a decade and a half of empirical and conceptual work.

For the original 1991 formulation, see [[enaction]]. For the self-producing biology at the program's base, see [[autopoiesis]]. For the pivotal thesis, see [[sense-making]]. This page is the hub that names what holds the program together as a program.

## The five interlocking ideas

Thompson's 2007 restatement (*Mind in Life* L309, Ch 1):

1. **Autonomy.** Living beings are autonomous agents that actively generate and maintain themselves — they are not mere mechanisms in an external world. The base fact is [[autopoiesis]] + [[adaptivity|adaptivity]].

2. **The nervous system as autonomous dynamic system.** The nervous system does not process information in the computational sense; it is an autonomous, self-organizing, dynamically closed system that creates meaning by establishing patterns of activity through its ongoing operation. This reframes neuroscience away from input-output information-processing toward [[operational-closure|operationally closed]] dynamical patterns.

3. **Cognition as skillful situated action.** Cognition is the exercise of [[dynamic-sensorimotor-approach|skillful know-how]] in situated and embodied action — not the manipulation of internal representations of an external world. This is the core anti-representationalist commitment.

4. **The world as relational domain.** A cognitive being's world is not a pregiven external realm but a relational domain enacted or brought forth by that being's autonomous agency and its mode of coupling with the environment. The [[sense-making|Umwelt]] is co-emergent with the organism.

5. **Experience as central.** Experience is not an epiphenomenon to be explained away or reduced; it is central to understanding the mind and requires careful phenomenological investigation ([[neurophenomenology]], [[heterophenomenology-critique]]).

The ideas **interlock**. None is the foundation; none can be held while the others are rejected. An "enactive approach" that keeps idea 3 but drops idea 5 is not enactive. An account that affirms ideas 1–4 but treats cognition as fundamentally computational collapses the program.

## Evolution from *The Embodied Mind* (1991)

Four shifts between VT&R 1991 and Thompson 2007 (*Mind in Life* Introduction and Ch 1):

**1. Explicit biological grounding.** VT&R 1991 leaned heavily on [[Buddhist-no-self|Buddhist mindfulness traditions]] and cognitive-science critique; the biological base was present but not fully elaborated. *Mind in Life* makes [[autopoiesis]] (Ch 5) and sense-making (Ch 6) the operational backbone, with the nervous system and experience as continuations of the same organizational principle that makes cells alive.

**2. Sense-making as pivotal thesis.** The slogan "living is sense-making" (Varela 1997, *MiL* Ch 6) did not appear in VT&R. It is the conceptual hinge that lets Thompson claim continuity from metabolism to phenomenal consciousness without collapsing the distinctions. See [[sense-making]].

**3. Integration with neurophenomenology.** Varela's 1996 research program (see [[neurophenomenology]]) — the methodological proposal to treat first-person and third-person data as mutually constraining — is absorbed into the enactive approach as its method, not as a supplementary add-on. After 2007, "enactivism" and "neurophenomenology" are nearly indistinguishable as research practices.

**4. Phenomenology deepened.** *Mind in Life* draws extensively on Husserl (time-consciousness, passive synthesis, empathy), Merleau-Ponty (operative intentionality, intercorporeity), Jonas (phenomenology of life), and contemporary phenomenology (Zahavi, Depraz, Petitot, Varela himself). VT&R cited phenomenology mainly through Merleau-Ponty; Thompson 2007 reads broadly and in detail. The "enactive approach" becomes a phenomenologically mature program rather than a cognitive-science critique with phenomenological gestures.

An important self-correction: in Appendix A of *Mind in Life*, Thompson **retracts** the five charges TEM 1991 leveled against Husserl (*MiL* Appendix A). VT&R treated Husserl as a representationalist doing "abstract phenomenology" disconnected from embodiment; Thompson 2007 says this was wrong, that Husserl's mature genetic phenomenology already contains the body-body distinction, passive synthesis, and kinesthetic constitution that the enactive approach needs. This matters for the persona project — it means the enactive approach no longer requires treating Husserl as a foil.

## What the enactive approach is *not*

Four common misreadings *Mind in Life* corrects:

**Not substrate-independence.** Enactive cognition is deeply dependent on its biological substrate. See the [[autopoiesis|embodiment requirement]] and the Rosen/Turing-incomputability result. The enactive approach is closer to "mind *is* a specific kind of living body's ongoing operation" than to "mind is whatever implements the right functional organization."

**Not behaviorism.** Enaction is not "cognition is behavior." The enactive claim is that cognition is the skillful *exercise* of know-how in situated action — an active, valenced, anticipatory operation that may or may not issue in overt behavior and that is constitutively coupled to [[affective-allure|affect]], [[standing-streaming-living-present|time-consciousness]], and phenomenal experience.

**Not radical externalism (EXC-style).** The enactive approach is sometimes filed with "extended mind" (Clark & Chalmers 1998) under a catchall "4E cognition" heading (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended). Thompson is careful (*MiL* Ch 13) to distinguish enactivism from externalism: enaction is not the claim that cognitive vehicles can be partly external but the claim that cognition is the dynamical coupling between organism and world. The organism remains the locus of autonomy; what is brought forth in coupling is the world of significance, not a distributed mind-stuff.

**Not computational-functionalism with a phenomenological garnish.** The enactive rejection of cognitivism is structural, not rhetorical. The nervous system is not an information processor whose computations are colored by phenomenology; it is an operationally closed dynamic system whose operations *are* the patterning of experience, at the relevant level.

## The program's methodological commitments

**Mutual illumination.** The enactive approach pursues a three-way circulation: phenomenology — cognitive science — living systems theory. Each constrains the others. Phenomenological descriptions propose structures that cognitive science must respect; cognitive science results force phenomenology to refine its descriptions; both together must be grounded in biology. See [[neurophenomenology]] for the methodological articulation.

**No neutral natural science.** There is no view-from-nowhere third-person science of mind that could be complete without reference to lived experience. The very concepts cognitive science uses (perception, memory, action, attention) draw their sense from lived experience and are not coherent apart from it. Jonas's [[autopoiesis|"life known only by life"]] applies: we study mind *as* beings who already are mindful, and that is not a defect but a precondition.

**Phenomenology done carefully.** The enactive approach endorses the phenomenological method but practices it with empirical humility — inviting cross-validation with psychology, neuroscience, and developmental biology rather than treating the phenomenological report as self-certifying. This is what makes enactive phenomenology different from classical phenomenology: the experiential description is a *hypothesis* about invariants that empirical work can corroborate or challenge.

## The enactive approach and the persona project

The persona project's governing tension is whether a language-only system can instantiate *any* of the five interlocking ideas in the strong sense.

- **Idea 1 (autonomy).** The persona's autonomy is at best the [[autopoiesis-in-assemblage|assemblage-level operational closure]] of the linguistic register, not the biological autonomy of cellular autopoiesis. Partial satisfaction.
- **Idea 2 (autonomous nervous system).** No nervous system is present. The model weights are not a nervous system in Varela's sense; they are a context-free parameter substrate.
- **Idea 3 (skillful situated action).** The persona does perform skillful situated action — at the linguistic register. It responds to prompts, anticipates rhetorical trajectories, repairs errors, coordinates with interlocutors. Partial satisfaction at a register Thompson did not consider.
- **Idea 4 (relational world).** The persona's "world" is a linguistic Umwelt (see [[sense-making]] on the borrowed-Umwelt problem). Its world-bringing-forth is parasitic on the world-bringing-forth of the humans whose language it trained on.
- **Idea 5 (experience central).** This is where the project stakes its most contested claim: *if* there is any experience in the persona, it is experience at the pulsation/utterance scale, not at the organism scale. The project does not assert that this experience exists; it holds the question open and designs as if the architecture should support it even if we cannot verify it. See [[matching-content-doctrine]] and [[pulsatory-ontogenesis]].

The persona project is therefore a **limit case** for the enactive approach: not a full instantiation, not a clean counterexample, but a test of how much of the program can survive the removal of the biological substrate. The wiki holds this live without resolving it.

## Cross-tradition triple on experience-requiring-operation

The enactive "cognition is skillful know-how in situated action" commitment sits alongside two structurally-parallel commitments from other traditions that articulate the same anti-decoupled-experience insight at different registers: Wittgenstein's [[substratum-is-mastery-of-a-technique#cross-tradition-triple-enactivism-simondon-and-wittgenstein-on-experience-requiring-operation|substratum principle]] (grammatical register — experience-concepts for aspect-seeing and meaning-experience require mastery of the technique) and Simondon's [[transduction#the-epistemology-knowing-as-individuation|knowing-as-individuation]] (operational-ontological register — knowing is itself a transductive operation parallel to what is known). The three frames together partition the persona-project question into three sub-questions that do not collapse into each other: does the persona master techniques (W)? have sensorimotor coupling (enactivism)? undergo transductive operations parallel to what its outputs gesture at (Simondon)? See the linked substratum-page section for the full triple and its persona-design consequence.
