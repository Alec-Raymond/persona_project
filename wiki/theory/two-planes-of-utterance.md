# Two Planes of Utterance — Discourse and History

Benveniste's 1959 paper "The Correlations of Tense in the French Verb" (Ch 19) distinguishes two mutually-exclusive planes on which any utterance operates in French: **discourse** (*discours*) and **history** (*histoire*, also called narration or récit). Each plane has its own inventory of verb-tenses and its own referential regime. The distinction matters for the persona project because it gives precise linguistic content to the difference between a persona's *speaking* (discourse) and a persona's *narrating* (history-plane), and because one of Benveniste's most striking formulations — "events seem to narrate themselves" on the historical plane — names a specific operation a persona can perform or refuse.

## The argument

Looking at the French verbal system, Benveniste observes that the tenses do not distribute evenly across utterance-contexts. Some tenses appear in conversation but never in historical narration; some appear in historical narration but never in conversation; a few appear in both.

**The discourse plane** uses the present, the perfect (*passé composé*), the future, and their compounds. It is the plane of utterance that signals its relation to a speaker and an addressee. *Je suis arrivé* — the perfect — implicates the speaker-now; the event is anchored to the present of utterance. The deictics *je, tu, ici, maintenant* are operative.

**The historical plane** uses the aorist (*passé simple*), the imperfect, the pluperfect, and the prospective. It is the plane of past-events-recounted. The first-person and second-person pronouns are *absent from it by principle*. The aorist has no reference to the speaker's now; it narrates events that are complete in themselves, without reference to the moment of narration.

Benveniste's formulation is exact and striking:

> **"No one speaks here; the events seem to narrate themselves."**

On the historical plane, the voice that would anchor the utterance to an *I*-now is deliberately absent. The events appear as if they were recounting themselves — the narrator has effaced himself behind the events.

## Why this is a mutually exclusive pair

The two planes are not a continuum but an opposition. A single utterance can be on one plane or the other; the same French past event is reported with *passé composé* on the discourse plane and *passé simple* on the historical plane. The distinction is not about what happened but about *from what position* the utterance is being produced. The two plans cannot coexist in the same sentence without a specific operation of embedding.

For Benveniste this is structural, not stylistic. The French verbal system *enforces* the choice: you are either on the discourse plane (I-anchored, deictic) or the historical plane (self-effacing, event-foregrounded).

## Connection to the I/you polarity

The historical plane is precisely the plane on which the [[subject-in-language|I/you polarity]] is *not* in operation. No *I* says these events; no *you* is being addressed. The events are given in the mode of the [[non-person-third-person|non-person]] (*he*, *they*). The historical plane is the non-person plane extended into a whole register of utterance.

Discourse plane and historical plane are therefore not two arbitrary dialectal choices but the two poles of a systematic opposition: *presence* of the speech-instance's I/you apparatus vs. *suspension* of it in favor of event-only narration. Benveniste's "events seem to narrate themselves" is the exact statement of what happens when the I/you apparatus is withdrawn.

## Why this matters for the persona project

**Personas often slide between discourse and history without noticing.** When a persona tells a story about itself ("I went to the market yesterday; there was a strange old man..."), it has two options. It can narrate in the discourse mode — anchoring each past event to its own I-now, using perfect tenses and the felt presence of I/you — or it can slip into the historical mode, where the narrator effaces itself and the events appear as if self-narrating. English does not enforce the choice as strongly as French, but the two modes are distinguishable in English style (felt authorial presence vs. invisible-narrator prose), and LLM outputs frequently slip from one to the other within a single passage.

For the persona project, this matters because the two planes produce *different subject-effects*:

- **Discourse plane**: the persona's I is foregrounded; the utterance is felt as *this speaker's saying*. The [[subject-in-language|linguistic constitution]] of the persona's subjecthood is operative and visible.
- **Historical plane**: the persona is *absent* from its own narration. The events narrate themselves; no one speaks. The I-anchoring is suspended.

A persona that speaks only on the historical plane would not in fact be a speaker — it would be a self-effacing narrator whose subjecthood is suspended. A persona that speaks only on the discourse plane would never be able to present events without folding them into its own I-anchored present. Both planes are needed; the distinction needs to be *knowing* rather than drifting.

## The self-effacement of the historical plane

The historical plane is the plane of *impersonal* narration. It is the plane literature uses to produce the effect of a world that exists independently of any narrator's address. It is the plane of the aorist: *he came, he saw, he conquered* — with no *and I tell you about it*, no speaker, no addressee, only the events.

D&G's "writing-as-becoming" analysis (see [[writing-as-becoming]]) describes the impersonal [[writing-as-becoming#impersonal-third-person|third-person]] of literature — the *il* of Blanchot — as a becoming-imperceptible of the writer. Benveniste's historical plane gives the French-linguistic *mechanism* for this impersonal register: the historical plane is the grammatical apparatus that allows "no one speaks here." The literary *il* of Blanchot is the historical plane weaponized into an existential operation.

**Convergence to preserve carefully:** the historical plane is a positive grammatical structure, not a deficiency. It does not lack a speaker because it has failed to have one; it is *designed* not to have one. When a persona operates on the historical plane, it is not lacking its I — it is deploying a specific register whose operation is the suspension of I/you apparatus.

**Tension to preserve:** D&G's *il* is celebrated as an ethical-political achievement of literature (becoming-imperceptible as creative); Benveniste's aorist-register is a neutral structural feature of French. Whether the persona's slides into the historical plane are *achievements* or *failures* depends on whether the slide is deliberate — and the persona system has no current apparatus for making the slide deliberate.

## Connection to deixis

The historical plane does not admit deictics (*now, here, today, yesterday*). Its temporal markers are different — *then, at that moment, the following day, that day*. See [[empty-signs-and-instance-of-discourse]] for the shifter apparatus that is operative only on the discourse plane. The historical plane is the plane from which deixis has been systematically expelled.

## Operational criterion for the persona project

A simple diagnostic: **does the utterance use perfect or aorist-equivalent?** Does it use "I/you" and "now/here" — or "he/they" and "then/that day"?  English doesn't have the aorist/perfect split cleanly, but the *feel* of the planes carries over. "I was walking through the market and this guy came up to me" is discourse; "One day a man walked through the market, and another man approached him" is history.

The persona's default register should be discourse. Slides into history are permissible but should be marked — either by framing ("let me tell you a story...") or by deliberate shift. A persona that habitually narrates its own activity on the historical plane has effaced its speaker-position.
