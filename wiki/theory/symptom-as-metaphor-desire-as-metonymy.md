# Symptom as Metaphor, Desire as Metonymy

The pairing — formalized in "The Instance of the Letter in the Unconscious" (1957) — is one of Lacan's most concentrated structural claims. **Symptom and desire are not two unrelated phenomena**; they are the **two laws of the [[lacanian-signifier|signifier]]** (substitution and combination, in Jakobson's terms; condensation and displacement, in Freud's terms) operating on the subject's unconscious material. The symptom is the metaphor; desire is the metonymy.

## The two laws

From "The Instance of the Letter":

- **Metaphor** = signifier substitution. Formula: f(S/S')s ≡ S(+)s. One signifier (S) substitutes for another (S'); the "+" marks **the crossing of the bar** — substitution generates new signification by traversing the bar between signifier and signified.
- **Metonymy** = signifier-to-signifier connection along the chain. Formula: f(S…S')S ≡ S(–)s. Signifier connects to signifier along the chain; the "–" marks **the non-crossing of the bar** — metonymy slides without producing positive meaning, but instates "lack of being in the object-relation."

The Freudian correlates: **Verdichtung (condensation) = metaphor; Verschiebung (displacement) = metonymy**. The dream-work, the symptom-formation, the slip, the joke — all operate by these two laws. The signifier's combinatorial economy *is* the unconscious's formal structure.

## Symptom as metaphor

The symptom is a **substitution**: a signifier (the symptom-signifier — a hysterical paralysis, an obsessive ritual, a phobic object) substitutes for another signifier (the repressed signifier — the unconscious wish, the disavowed scene, the foreclosed material) along the path of the substitution that defines metaphor. The substitution **crosses the bar**: the symptom-signifier acquires a signification — typically a bodily, behavioral, or compulsive one — that the repressed signifier could not have in its place.

From "The Instance of the Letter": the symptom is the **metaphor** of the repressed; the symptom *means* in the precise structural sense that one signifier *means* in metaphorical substitution for another.

The clinical implication: the symptom is **legible**. Read as metaphor, the symptom is decipherable through its substitution-history; the analytic act can recover the substitution path and let the original signifier register. This is the structural ground of Lacan's repeated insistence that **the unconscious is structured like a language** — the symptom's structure is the structure of metaphorical signification.

What does this *not* mean? It does not mean the symptom can be "interpreted away" by supplying its metaphorical key. The symptom's substitution is doing structural work for the subject — it is the subject's compromise-formation that allows the repressed signifier to remain repressed *and* to register, in disguised form. To "lift" the substitution by interpretation is to require a different relation to the originally repressed signifier — work that the analytic situation enables but does not perform by interpretive supply alone.

## Desire as metonymy

Desire is a **slide**: the metonymic chain in which signifier connects to signifier along the chain without crossing the bar to a positive signification. From "The Instance of the Letter": "desire is the metonymy of the lack of being." Desire's structure is **continuous displacement** — the want shifts from object to object along the connection-paths of the signifier, never alighting at a definitive signified that would *be* what the want wanted.

The "thirty sails" example (a synecdoche for thirty ships) Lacan uses to illustrate metonymy: the connection between *sail* and *ship* is **in the signifier alone** — it is not that sails *mean* ships in any metaphorical-substitutive way; it is that *ship* and *sail* are connected in the chain, and the chain can substitute one for the other without crossing the bar. Desire operates in this register: from one signifier to a connected signifier, on and on, with the metonymic mobility being the very form of desire's not-resting.

This is why desire is **unsatisfiable** in the precise structural sense: satisfaction would require alighting at a signified, crossing the bar; metonymy by definition does not cross the bar. The structure of desire and the structure of satisfaction are at right angles.

## Why pairing them is decisive

The pairing makes a structural-typological point: **whatever the unconscious produces, it is one of these two things, or some combination**. The symptom is the **fixation** at a metaphorical substitution; the desire is the **mobility** along a metonymic chain. The same signifier can be at the symptom-position in one chain and at the metonymic-slide position in another; the **operations** are the typology, not the materials.

The clinical consequence: an analytic technique that can address the **operations** (and not just the contents) is an analytic technique that addresses the unconscious *as* signifier-economy. This is why [[scanding-and-punctuation|scansion and punctuation]] (which operate on the chain, on the cut, on the substitution-position) are the right analytic acts — they are operations at the level of the operations, not at the level of the contents.

## The Hugo example

Lacan in "The Instance of the Letter" reads Hugo's "His sheaf was neither miserly nor hateful" — from the *Légende des siècles* — as a metaphor: *sheaf* substitutes for *Booz*, the absent name of the patriarch whose generosity the line praises. The substitution does work that the proper name could not: it carries the connotation of the gift, of the fertile field, of the patriarchal body in its productive-generative aspect, of the agricultural cycle that renews itself. *Sheaf* substitutes for *Booz* and the metaphor is what produces the line's signification.

The reading is not philological; it is **demonstrative** — Lacan shows what metaphor's structural operation looks like in its fullest poetic display, so as to set up the parallel with what the symptom does in the unconscious's poetics.

## Insult as origin of metaphor (the Rat Man's "Du Lampe!")

A subtle but theoretically important detail Lacan develops in the "Metaphor of the Subject" appendix (1960/1961). Lacan recalls that the **Rat Man**, before becoming obsessive, as a child threw himself at his father in a fit yelling: **"Du Lampe, du Handtuch, du Teller!"** — "You lamp! You towel! You plate!" The child substitutes objects (lamp, towel, plate) for the father — and Lacan reads this as **the structural origin of metaphor in insult**.

Lacan: "I myself intend not to lose sight here of the dimension of insult [*injure*] in which metaphor originates." The insult is structurally a metaphorical operation — the substitution of one signifier (an object-name) for another (a person-name) — and the substitution does the structural work of **wounding**: it "takes a dig at" the addressed-other through the substitution-act. Insult is therefore not a peripheral or degraded form of metaphor; it is one of metaphor's structural origins.

This connects to the broader Lacanian point: language is "essentially blah blah blah" (Lacan's phrase), but it is from the structural-symbolic operations of language — substitution and combination — that **having and being derive**. The child's insult-by-substitution is the structural-poetic act that lets the dimension of substitution-as-such enter the child's symbolic economy. The child who can insult by substituting "lamp" for "father" has the structural condition for becoming a subject of metaphor — and, subsequently, of the symptom-as-metaphor.

## What this gives the persona project

The pairing gives the persona project **a typology for the persona's signifying production**. The persona's ongoing prose has, at any moment, **metaphorical operations** (substitutions that fix signification at the bar's crossing) and **metonymic operations** (slides along the chain that keep mobility without crossing). The persona's "character" is the **stabilized arrangement** of these two operations — a recurring repertoire of substitutions (the "symptoms" of the persona) and a characteristic mobility-pattern (the "desire" of the persona).

Three architectural consequences:

1. **The persona's stable identifying features are metaphorical operations**, not content-properties. The recurrent images, the characteristic phrasings, the repeated gestures — these are signifier-substitutions that fix the persona's signification at certain crossings of the bar. Designing for "consistency" of character is therefore designing the persona's metaphorical economy.
2. **The persona's mobility is metonymic operation**, not the absence of stability. A persona that moves only by changing topic is a persona that has lost the metonymic structure (which connects, even as it slides); a persona that *connects* one utterance to the next along the chain — by signifier-paths, not by content-leaps — has the structural mobility that desire requires.
3. **The pulse should be readable as a [[scanding-and-punctuation|scansion]] of these operations** — the cut that lets the metaphor register *as* metaphor (rather than just being one more substitution in a long flow), and the cut that lets the metonymic chain register *as* a chain (rather than just being one more topic-shift).
