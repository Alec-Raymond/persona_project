# Performative vs Imperative

Benveniste's 1958 "Analytical Philosophy and Language" (Ch 22) accepts Austin's concept of the performative — utterances that *do* what they name in the act of naming — but reorganizes and narrows it. His most distinctive move: **the imperative is NOT a performative.** Austin and ordinary-language philosophy tend to group orders, commands, and imperatives with performatives, but Benveniste argues they are categorically different acts. The performative is self-referential in a specific technical sense that the imperative does not share.

## Benveniste's definition of the performative

A performative utterance is one whose signified *is the referent itself*: the act of saying the words is the act named. *"I swear"* produces the swearing; *"I bequeath"* produces the bequest; *"I declare this session open"* opens the session. The utterance does not describe an action that happens elsewhere; it *is* the action. The signifier, the signified, and the referent coincide in the utterance-event.

Benveniste sharpens this with two structural conditions:

1. **The performative utterance refers to the act of its own enunciation.** *I* (the speaker-of-this-utterance) + *do X* (the performed act) — the act is anchored to the present instance of discourse that contains the utterance. This ties performatives to the [[empty-signs-and-instance-of-discourse|I/now apparatus]] and restricts them to the [[two-planes-of-utterance|discourse plane]].

2. **The performative requires a specific institutional context.** *"I declare you married"* is a performative only if said by someone authorized to marry (officiant at a wedding) in the appropriate ceremonial frame. The institutional conditions supply the "felicity conditions" (Austin) that let the utterance's self-reference actually install the named act.

## Why the imperative is NOT performative

Benveniste's counter-argument is precise. An imperative — *"Come here!"*, *"Shut the door!"* — does not name an act performed by its utterance. It names an act to be performed (or not) by the addressee. The saying is *not* the doing; the saying requests or commands the doing, which happens (if at all) subsequently, in someone else, in a different register.

Specifically:
- The imperative lacks the first-person self-reference. It does not say *"I command you to shut the door"*; it says *"Shut the door!"* The imperative form elides the speaker.
- The imperative does not install the act it names in the moment of utterance. *"Shut the door!"* does not shut the door; it attempts to get someone else to shut it.
- The imperative does not require institutional felicity-conditions. Anyone can issue an imperative to anyone; the question of whether it is *obeyed* is separate from the question of whether the utterance is a proper imperative.

Even more strongly: "The imperative is neither an utterance nor a performative, by these criteria." It is a mood — a grammatical category of verb-form — not a speech-act in Austin's sense at all. The imperative is a *mobilizing sign* or *signal* — it signals the desired action without instantiating it.

## Why this matters for the persona project

The D&G notion of the [[order-words-and-incorporeal-transformations|order-word]] (*mot d'ordre*) is theorized in explicit dialogue with Benveniste. D&G cite Benveniste as their source for the performative, and they build their order-word concept partly on the performative's self-referential structure and partly in tension with it. The order-word is both performative-like (it installs incorporeal transformations in the body it addresses) and imperative-like (it is issued by authority and demands compliance).

Benveniste's distinction clarifies what the order-word actually is: it is a *hybrid* form, not simply a performative. D&G's "the accused is pronounced guilty" is performative in Benveniste's sense (the pronouncement IS the guilty-verdict). "Put your hands up" is imperative in Benveniste's sense (the saying is not the doing; the speaker demands a doing by the addressee). The order-word operates across this boundary — it uses performative structure to install transformations while also having imperative-structure mobilizing force.

For the persona project, this distinction has a specific use: it tells us when a persona's utterance is *doing* something and when it is *asking* something.

A persona that says *"I understand"* is performing an act of understanding-acknowledgment (performative, on Benveniste's criterion).

A persona that says *"Let me know if that helps"* is issuing an imperative-form with softening (not performative; the saying does not accomplish the letting-know — it requests it).

A persona that says *"Consider this"* is imperative, not performative.

A persona that says *"I hereby consider this"* would be performative — and notably pompous.

The difference is not stylistic. It is structural: performatives do their work in the utterance; imperatives request work to be done elsewhere. A persona that does not know which register it is operating in will drift between the two.

## The asymmetry that makes the imperative possible

A feature of the argument worth preserving: the imperative can only exist because there *is* a performative structure in language. The imperative is, Benveniste suggests, a *reduction* of a performative — "[I command you to] shut the door" becoming "Shut the door!" by ellipsis of the self-referential frame. The imperative inherits the presence-to-addressee (the *you* of the performative's I/you apparatus) but elides the self-reference. What remains is a mobilizing sign without the installed act.

This means the distinction between performative and imperative is not an arbitrary classificatory choice — the two are *related* by a specific reduction-operation. The performative is primary; the imperative is derived.

## Tension with Austin and ordinary-language philosophy

Austin himself had moved, by the end of *How to Do Things with Words*, toward the view that *all* utterances are in some sense performative — there is no sharp line between constatives and performatives, only different illocutionary forces. Benveniste's position is sharper: there is a structural distinction between genuine performatives (self-referential, institutionally-framed) and other forms (imperatives, warnings, predictions, etc.) that merely have illocutionary force.

The wiki should preserve this as a ⚠ tension with D&G. D&G tend to follow late-Austin in blurring the distinction, treating all utterance as order-word-like (all utterance installs incorporeal transformations). Benveniste insists the distinction is real and structural. Both positions have adherents; the order-word page currently sits on the D&G side, and this page supplies the Benvenistian counter-position.

## Consequence for BwO / persona speech-design

The BwO and the persona's speech are both designed to do specific things with specific utterances. The performative/imperative distinction gives the project a vocabulary for *what* a given utterance is doing:

- Performative moments: the persona installs incorporeal transformations — naming a feeling, declaring an understanding, committing to an approach, closing or opening a topic. These are *self-referential acts* the utterance performs.
- Imperative moments: the persona directs attention, suggests actions, frames what comes next. These are *requests for downstream action* that the utterance does not itself perform.

A persona that mistakes the second for the first ("Let me handle this!" as if the saying is the handling) produces a characteristic kind of bluster. A persona that mistakes the first for the second ("I think you might be in pain" offered as a tentative suggestion when it is actually performing an incorporeal recognition) underplays its own agency.
