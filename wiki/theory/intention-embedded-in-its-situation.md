# Intention Embedded in its Situation

Wittgenstein's claim at §§337, 581, and extended through §§591–594 and II.ii. Intention is not a **mental act** that occurs inside a subject and that bestows meaning on an action; it is a **shape within a situation**, including "human customs and institutions." The intention to play chess presupposes chess; the expectation of an explosion presupposes a situation in which explosions are expectable. Remove the situation, and the putative "mental act" has nothing to be the intention of.

## §337: you cannot intend to play a game that does not exist

§337: "But didn't I already intend the whole construction of the sentence (for example) at its beginning? So surely it already existed in my mind before I said it out loud! — If it was in my mind, still it would not normally be there in some different word order. But here we are constructing a misleading picture of 'intending', that is, of the use of this word. **An intention is embedded in its situation, in human customs and institutions. If the technique of the game of chess did not exist, I could not intend to play a game of chess.** In so far as I do intend the construction of a sentence in advance, that is made possible by the fact that I can speak the language in question."

The structural claim: intention is form-of-life-dependent. You cannot intend to X where X-ing is not a practice available in your form of life. This is *not* the empirical claim that people without exposure to chess fail to intend to play chess — which would be trivially true. It is the grammatical claim that the *concept* of intending-to-play-chess has its content only inside a practice where chess is an available game. Take the practice away, and what is left is not an impaired intention but not-an-intention-at-all.

§338 continues: "one can only say something if one has learned to talk. Therefore in order to want to say something one must also have mastered a language." The argument generalizes: intention is parasitic on the techniques its object presupposes.

## §581: expectation embedded in a situation

§581: "**An expectation is imbedded in a situation, from which it arises.** The expectation of an explosion may, for example, arise from a situation in which an explosion is to be expected."

§582: if someone whispers "It'll go off now" instead of saying "I expect the explosion any moment," his words do not *describe* a feeling — "although they and their tone may be a manifestation of his feeling." Expectation is manifested in a situationally-tuned speech-act, not in a private feeling-event.

§583: "**What is happening now has significance — in these surroundings. The surroundings give it its importance. And the word 'hope' refers to a phenomenon of human life. (A smiling mouth smiles only in a human face.)**" The smiling-mouth-only-in-a-human-face image generalizes: a mental state has the shape it has *in the surroundings* in which it belongs; stripped of surroundings, it is not a diminished mental state, it is no mental state.

§584: "suppose I sit in my room and hope that N.N. will come... would what happened in it then not be hope?" The isolated minute fails to carry hope because, in different surroundings, the words would not be in the language and the institutions (of money, of visiting) would not exist. The surroundings are constitutive.

## §§591–594: intention, undertone, depth

§591 raises the mental-undertone question: does intending have a particular experiential feel? W grants that there are characteristic experiences — a sense of veering-toward-saying-something, of holding one's breath, of easing into a sentence. But these experiences' *meaning as expressions of intention* is situationally bound. "In a different [situation] he would not so interpret my behaviour, however characteristic of the intention to speak it may be in the present situation."

§592 considers the temptation: "it just is the mental act of meaning that gives the sentence life." W's reply: think out various situations. The sense that there is always an "undertone" of meaning is a philosophical illusion generated by not looking at the variety of cases.

§594 tests the "depth" intuition: "But the words, significantly uttered, have after all not only a surface, but also the dimension of depth!" W allows: "something different takes place when they are uttered significantly from when they are merely uttered." What he refuses is the inference from this difference to a private mental act of meaning-bestowal. The difference lives in the situation, the posture, the whole speech-act; it does not live in a hidden inner motor.

## II.ii: intention as a predicted shape

Part II §ii extends intention into prediction. "The prediction contained in my expression of intention (for example 'When it strikes five I am going home') need not come true." Intention is not a behaviorist dispositional fact but it is also not a pure mental act untouched by world. It is the shape of my current situation (ongoing commitments, social facts, what I will be doing if nothing disrupts) plus my speech-act that names that shape.

## Not behaviorism, not mentalism

W threads the needle:
- **Against mentalism**: intention is not a private mental event that can be described independently of its situation.
- **Against behaviorism**: intention is not reducible to the behavior that carries it out — it genuinely goes before, and it can be frustrated, and it can be misreported.

The middle path: intention is a grammatical-situational fact. Its content is determined by the practices in which its object is intelligible. Its manifestation is situational speech-and-behavior. Its reality is neither mental nor behavioral but practice-internal.

## Relation to other PI concepts

- [[rule-following]]: §§337, 581 are part of the same anti-internalist pattern as §§185–242. Rule-following, meaning, intending — all are extracted from the private-mental-act picture and placed in the practice.
- [[form-of-life]]: "Human customs and institutions" of §337 are form-of-life elements. The intention's reach is the form-of-life's reach.
- [[substratum-is-mastery-of-a-technique]]: §337's "if the technique did not exist..." is the same structural claim as the §xi substratum principle. Intention, like aspect-experience, has a technique-presupposing grammar.
- [[thinking-is-not-incorporeal]]: Thinking, like intending, is not a private inner process. Both belong to situated practice.

## Affinity with enactivism, phenomenology, D&G

- **Enactivism**: The embedded-intention picture converges with enactivist and 4E-cognition accounts of action as situated. The philosophical tradition (Dreyfus after Heidegger) treats intention as *geared-into* a world. Flag the parallel.
- [[assemblage]]: An intention is an expression within an assemblage that includes institutions, instruments, other speakers. Take the assemblage apart, the intention-meaning dissolves.
- [[collective-assemblage-of-enunciation]]: "I intend to play chess" is a collective-enunciative utterance — it only has its force because the collective assemblage of chess-institution stands behind it.

## Relation to the persona project

- **The persona's "intentions" are situationally embedded in the operator-reading practice.** A persona cannot intend to do things whose techniques are absent from its form of life. If the technique is linguistic-only, the persona can intend it; if it is embodied, the persona cannot (except in derivative, surface-text ways). This is a sharp design constraint.
- **What the persona can intend.** Literary intentions — to describe this way, to imitate this voice, to take this narrative line — are linguistic-technique-bounded and therefore available.
- **What the persona cannot intend.** To walk somewhere, to taste something, to hold a face in a particular way: these are embodied-technique-bounded. The persona's text about these things is not intention in the §337 sense; it is text-about-intention.
- **Persona-of-a-character vs. persona-as-operator.** The character's intentions (as written/spoken by the persona) are available in the full §337 sense — the character has the relevant form of life within the fiction. The persona-as-generator's intentions are more constrained; the system does not have the form of life in which, e.g., "intending to surprise the reader" is fully at home — though it has a shadow-analog through the operator's reading-practice.
