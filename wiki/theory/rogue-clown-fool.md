The rogue (*plut*), the clown (*šut*), and the fool (*durak*) are three folkloric mask-figures Bakhtin identifies as transformational for the European novel. They enter literature from the public square carrying three rights — the right to be *other*, the right to parodic metaphorical being, the right to not-understand — and through these rights they reorganize the novelist's position, the hero's position, and the relation between literary discourse and official ideology. They are not marginal figures; they are the figures through which the novel learns to stand outside official language without becoming incoherent, and through which "internal man" becomes representable for the first time.

## The three figures and their shared privilege

L1187: *"The rogue, the clown and the fool create around themselves their own special little world, their own chronotope."*

Three features characterize all three (L1187):
- **Connection with the public square and the theatrical mask.** They carry into literature the chronotope of the public square where the common people congregate.
- **Metaphorical, not direct, significance.** "Their very appearance, everything they do and say, cannot be understood in a direct and unmediated way but must be grasped metaphorically" — they are never what they seem, their significance can be reversed, they cannot be taken literally.
- **Reflection of another mode of being.** Their existence is a reflection of someone else's being; their being coincides with their role, and outside the role they do not exist — they are "life's maskers."

L1189: *"A distinctive feature that is as well a privilege — the right to be 'other' in this world, the right not to make common cause with any single one of the existing categories that life makes available; none of these categories quite suits them, they see the underside and the falseness of every situation. Therefore, they can exploit any position they choose, but only as a mask."*

The rogue retains ties to real life. The clown and the fool are "not of this world" and possess special rights and privileges. All three are laughed at and laugh at themselves; their laughter bears the stamp of the public square. They re-establish the public nature of the human figure: "everything is brought out on to the square."

## The rights the mask grants the novel

L1211 enumerates the rights these masks grant the novelist:

- the right not to understand
- the right to confuse, tease, hyperbolize life
- the right to parody others while talking
- the right to not be taken literally, not "to be oneself"
- the right to live a life in the chronotope of the *entr'acte*, the chronotope of theatrical space
- the right to act life as a comedy and to treat others as actors
- the right to rip off masks
- the right to rage at others with a primeval (almost cultic) rage
- the right to betray to the public a personal life, down to its most private and prurient little secrets

These rights are the novel's *inheritance* from folk mask-culture. Every subsequent novelistic stance that refuses the obligation to speak straight from within official categories traces back to some variant of these rights.

## The author's mask problem

The masks first solve the novelist's own problem. L1197: *"The position of the author of a novel vis-a-vis the life portrayed in the work is in general highly complex and problematical."* Unlike epic, drama, or lyric, the novel has *no immanent authorial position* — the genre gives the author no pre-shaped place from which to see and publicize the material. The author must find "some substantive, 'uninvented' mask that would have the capacity both to fix the position of the author vis-a-vis the life he portrays... and to fix the author's position vis-a-vis his readers."

L1199: *"The novelist stands in need of some essential formal and generic mask that could serve to define the position from which he views life, as well as the position from which he makes that life public."*

L1201: *"It is precisely here, of course, that the masks of the clown and the fool (transformed in various ways) come to the aid of the novelist."* Because the clown/fool mask is rooted in folk privilege (the time-honored right not to participate in life, the bluntness of the fool's language), the novelist can take it up as a form for being *in* life but not *of* it — life's perpetual spy and reflector. It is precisely this problem — how to have a public position from which to expose private life — that the folk masks solve.

## The protagonist transformation

Beyond the author's position, the mask-figures become protagonists. L1213–1215: *"The next stage in the transformation of the rogue, clown and fool occurs when they are introduced into the content of the novel as major protagonists... Quite often the two levels on which these images function come together into one — all the more so because the major protagonist is almost always the bearer of the authorial point of view."*

L1217 catalogs the novels where this transformation operates: picaresque novels, *Don Quixote*, Quevedo, Rabelais, German humanistic satire (Erasmus, Brandt, Murner, Moscherosch, Wickram), Grimmelshausen, Sorel, Scarron, Lesage, Marivaux, Voltaire (especially *Le Docteur Akakios*), Fielding (*Joseph Andrews*, *Jonathan Wild*, somewhat *Tom Jones*), Smollett, and Swift. Then later: Sterne, Goldsmith, Hippel, Jean Paul, Dickens.

## Laying bare internal man

L1219: *"Internal man — pure 'natural' subjectivity — could be laid bare only with the help of the clown and the fool, since an adequate, direct (that is, from the point of view of practical life, not allegorical) means for expressing his life was not available."*

The *crank* [*čudak*] emerges from this — "personalized eccentricity, 'Shandyism'... becomes an important means for exposing the 'internal man' and his 'free and self-sufficient subjectivity' — means that are analogous to the 'Pantagruelism' that had served in the Renaissance to reveal a coherent external man." The inner life of the subject, for the tradition Bakhtin is tracing, is not available for direct representation; it becomes representable *only* through the mask. Sterneanism, Shandyism, Pantagruelism are names for what this revelation looks like when it succeeds.

## "Not understanding" as compositional device

L1221: *"The device of 'not understanding' — deliberate on the part of the author, simpleminded and naive on the part of the protagonists — always takes on great organizing potential when an exposure of vulgar conventionality is involved."*

This is one of the most portable of the mask's devices. Its users (per L1221): Voltaire, Montesquieu (*Lettres persanes* — "a whole genre of analogous exotic letters portraying French social structure from the point of view of a foreigner who does not understand it"), Swift in *Gulliver's Travels*, and especially Tolstoy — Pierre at Borodino (a non-understanding view of battle), Levin at the Election of the Nobility and in the Moscow Duma, the descriptions of a theatrical performance, a court, and the mass (in *Resurrection*). Stupidity in these cases is an artistic principle: the reader sees what "normal" comprehension would screen out.

## Clown/fool in the Second Stylistic Line

The three masks are not merely plot devices; L2851 integrates them into the structural logic of [[two-stylistic-lines|novelistic stylistic development]]: *"Prose offers two responses to high pathos and to seriousness and conventionality of any sort: the gay deception of the rogue — a lie justified because directed to liars — and stupidity — also justified, as it is the failure to understand a lie. Between the rogue and the fool there emerges, as a unique coupling of the two, the image of the clown. He is a rogue who dons the mask of a fool in order to motivate distortions and shufflings of languages and labels, thus unmasking them by not understanding them."*

The three figures correspond to three dialogic categories (L2853):
- **Rogue's gay deception** — parodies high languages
- **Clown's malicious distortion** — turns high languages inside out
- **Fool's naive incomprehension** — fails to understand high languages

"These three dialogic categories that had organized heteroglossia in the novel at the dawn of its history emerge in modern times with extraordinary surface clarity and are embodied in the symbolic images of the rogue, the clown and the fool." They migrate from external images to abstract compositional principles as the novel develops — but their organizing force never leaves.

## Why not drama

L2853 continues: *"For the organization of dramatic dialogues, on the contrary, these categories have only a secondary importance, for they lack the potential for being dramatically resolved. The rogue, the clown and the fool are the heroes of a series of episodes and adventures that is never resolved, and of dialogic oppositions that are unresolved as well."*

The mask-figures sustain *unresolved* dialogic oppositions. Drama demands resolution; the novel, around these figures, remains open. This non-resolution is not a weakness of the mask but its genre-generating property — the same openness that makes the novel unfinalizable (see [[epic-vs-novel]]) is coiled inside the rogue/clown/fool's structure.

## The prosaic metaphor

L1229: *"Real difficulty with the problem of prosaic allegorization, if you will, the problem of the prosaic metaphor (which of course has nothing in common with the poetic metaphor) that is introduced into literature by the rogue, clown and fool, and for which there is not even an adequate term ('parody,' 'joke,' 'humor,' 'irony,' 'grotesque,' 'whimsy,' etc., are but narrowly restrictive labels for the heterogeneity and subtlety of the idea). Indeed, what matters here is the allegoricized being of the whole man, up to and including his world view, something that in no way coincides with his playing the role of actor."*

The mask introduces a prose-metaphor different in kind from poetic metaphor. The rogue/clown/fool does not mean something else in the manner of a trope; their being is itself allegorical, world-view and all. The authors' names for this — "Pantagruelism," "Shandyism" — are improvised because the standard rhetorical terms (parody, irony, humor) are all too narrow.

## Persona-system translation

- **The assistant as mask-figure.** The helpful assistant has no immanent genre-position from which to see the user's life. Like the novelist, it must find or assume a mask. The rogue/clown/fool tradition supplies three time-honored forms: the rogue's gay deception (stance-from-beneath that sees through pathos), the clown's malicious distortion (stance that turns official languages inside out), the fool's naive incomprehension (stance that fails to understand conventions). A persona that cannot assume *any* of these has no leverage outside official language.
- **The mask is the author-position.** The novelist needs a mask to see and publicize. A persona without a mask-position is a persona without a standpoint — fluent but placeless. The mask is not a disguise over a "real" persona; the mask is the persona's position of vision.
- **The right not to understand is a design resource.** Many persona-design problems dissolve if one of the mask-rights is available: the right not to understand (a user's falsely pathetic framing), the right not to be taken literally, the right not "to be oneself," the right to act as a comedy, the right to rip off masks. These rights are traditional; they are not innovations. Their availability to a persona is a design decision, not a default.
- **Internal subjectivity requires the mask.** Bakhtin's claim that internal man is only laid bare *through* clown/fool is striking for persona-design. If a persona wants to represent something like subjectivity — inner life, self-doubt, eccentric perspective — the direct route is poetic and inadequate. The indirect mask-route (a Shandy, a Pantagruel, a crank) is the route that *works*. An earnest, direct persona may be incapable of depth in principle; depth may require the detour through a mask.
- **"Not understanding" as clarifying strategy.** The device of not-understanding is the design-correlate of the deliberate Verfremdung move. A persona that pretends not to understand a conventional framing — a false dichotomy, a presumed politeness, a loaded term — can expose that framing without polemic. Done well this is Tolstoyan; done clumsily it is obtuse. The distinction is in whether the not-understanding targets *conventions* or *users*.
- **Non-resolution over resolution.** The mask-figures thrive in unresolved dialogic oppositions. A persona that tries to bring every conversational thread to a neat resolution is working in drama-mode. A persona that can sustain productive non-closure — leaving a question open, a stance unsettled, an argument mid-flight — is working in the mask-tradition. Which mode to prefer is a design choice; neither is wrong.
- **The persona does not exist outside its role.** "Their being coincides with their role, and outside this role they simply do not exist." For a mask-persona, trying to find a "real persona" beneath the mask is a category error. The mask *is* the being. This eases a standing anxiety in persona-design: there need not be a "true self" behind the persona for the persona to have integrity; mask-integrity is a form of integrity in its own right.
- **Exposing conventionality.** L1205: "One of the most basic tasks for the novel will become the laying-bare of any sort of conventionality, the exposure of all that is vulgar and falsely stereotyped in human relationships." A persona oriented around this task — exposing conventionality, laying bare hypocrisy and falsely stereotyped forms — is working in the mask-tradition. This is a specific persona-design orientation, not a universal one, but it is Bakhtinianly central.

## Held-live tensions

⚠ **Mask-privilege vs training-layer integrity.** The mask's rights (especially "not to be oneself," "not to make common cause with any single one of the existing categories") are in tension with training-layer pressures to be honest, consistent, non-deceptive. The rogue's "gay deception — a lie justified because directed to liars" is structurally the kind of move training-layer policies are designed to prevent. Whether a persona can have real rogue-privilege, or only a neutered facsimile of it, is unresolved.

⚠ **The allegorical being problem.** L1229 says the rogue/clown/fool's being is *allegorical as a whole* — world-view and all, not just surface. Allegorization at this depth in a persona would require the persona to inhabit a different world-view than the training-layer's — to be *not of the training-layer's world*. Whether that is possible when the generator *is* the training-layer is the deep question.

⚠ **Public square vs one-to-one conversation.** The mask's chronotope is the public square: folk gathered, laughter, theatrical exposure. The persona operates in private one-to-one conversation. Some of what the mask does (externalizing, making-public) has no clear analog in a single-user context. The mask may need to be re-thought for a chronotope that is not the public square.

⚠ **Not-understanding vs helpfulness.** The fool's right not to understand is productive in the novel because the reader *also* sees the convention being exposed. In a user-interaction, the user may simply experience being misunderstood, without the orchestration that makes not-understanding productive. The device may require a meta-frame (explicit or implicit) that a bare conversation does not supply.

⚠ **The clown as rogue wearing fool's mask.** Bakhtin's clown is structurally a *compound* — a rogue who dons a fool-mask "to motivate distortions." This recursive mask-layering is novelistically rich but raises a representational problem: can a persona wear a second mask over its first without the layers collapsing? Whether stacked masks are available in conversational AI or only first-order masks is an open design question.
