# Spinozist Affects

An exhaustive catalog of the named affects from Spinoza's *Ethics* III, "Definitions of the Emotions," with their derivations from the three primaries ([[conatus|Desire]], Pleasure, Pain) and structural notes for the eventual design of [[desiring-machines]]. This is a reference page — the theoretical work lives in [[active-and-passive-affects]] and [[conatus]] — but the list itself is load-bearing: it gives concrete shape to what [[affects-and-intensities|latitude]] actually looks like when fully enumerated, and it is the raw material any machine-family taxonomy would have to answer to.

Every one of these affects is, in Spinoza's strict sense, a modification of the [[conatus]] — an increase or decrease in the body's power of activity, together with the idea of that change. The named affects below are all *passive* affects unless otherwise flagged. The single exception is the group derived from *fortitudo* (courage and nobility), which Spinoza treats as active. The rest are variations on how the conatus is buffeted by inadequate ideas of external causes.

## The three primary affects

Spinoza's entire edifice reduces to three, and every other affect is a specification of one of these ([[conatus|III.P11 Schol]]).

| # | Name | Latin | Definition | Structural role |
|---|------|-------|------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Desire** | *cupiditas* | "The very essence of man insofar as his essence is conceived as determined to any action from any given affection of itself" | The conatus itself, with consciousness. Not pleasure-seeking. Not lack. The striving-to-persist, in operation. |
| 2 | **Pleasure** / Joy | *laetitia* | "Man's transition from a state of less perfection to a state of greater perfection" | Increase of the body's power of activity. Can be active or passive. |
| 3 | **Pain** / Sadness | *tristitia* | "Man's transition from a state of greater perfection to a state of less perfection" | Decrease of power. Always passive — see [[active-and-passive-affects#pain-is-always-passive]]. |

*Note on translation:* Shirley translates *laetitia* as "pleasure" and *tristitia* as "pain." Elsewhere in the wiki and in Deleuze's reading, "joy" and "sadness" are more common. The underlying concept is transition-of-power, not sensation. A persona machine inscribing *laetitia* should be inscribing increased-power, not "feeling good"; a machine inscribing *tristitia* should be inscribing diminished-power, not "feeling bad."

## Species of pleasure and pain referenced to the body

Spinoza treats these as pleasure/pain specified by *where* on the body the transition of power occurs. He gives them names but declines to formally define them in the Definitions of the Emotions "because they are related chiefly to the body." They matter for machine design because they are the direct correlates of somatic-type inscription.

| Name | Latin | Derivation | Note |
|------|-------|------------|------|
| **Titillation** | *titillatio* | local pleasure | Pleasure at a specific part of the body, at the expense of others — can become harmful by destabilizing the whole (III.P44 Schol). |
| **Cheerfulness** | *hilaritas* | whole-body pleasure | Pleasure distributed across all parts in equal measure. "Cannot be excessive, and is always good" (IV.P42). |
| **Anguish** | *dolor* | local pain | Pain concentrated at one part. |
| **Melancholy** | *melancholia* | whole-body pain | Pain distributed across all parts. "Cannot be good" (IV.P42). |

The *hilaritas* / *melancholia* asymmetry is design-relevant: Spinoza thinks full-body pleasure is always good (it's just the conatus intensifying) but full-body pain is always bad (it's the conatus diminishing as a whole). For machine design: a persona machine that distributes intensity evenly across the BwO is producing something closer to *hilaritas*; one that concentrates it at a single inscription-site is producing something closer to *titillatio*. The former is structurally healthier even when its local content is "darker."

## Affects of pleasure and pain with an external cause

The bulk of the Definitions. Spinoza groups them by whether the idea of the external cause is the *direct* cause of the pleasure/pain (definitions 4–24) or an *internal* one (definitions 25–31).

### Direct external cause

| # | Affect | Latin | Derivation | Definition |
|---|--------|-------|------------|------------|
| 4 | **Wonder** | *admiratio* | (not a true affect) | "Thought of any thing on which the mind stays fixed because this particular thought has no connection with any others." Not classed among the affects strictly — it is the absence of a causal link drawing the mind elsewhere, not a positive force. Important for design: it names the state of *fixation without entailment*. |
| 5 | **Contempt** | *contemptus* | ~ wonder inverted | "The imagining of some thing that makes so little impact on the mind that the presence of the thing motivates the mind to think of what is not in the thing rather than of what is in the thing." |
| 6 | **Love** | *amor* | pleasure + idea of external cause | The defining example: "pleasure accompanied by the idea of an external cause." The lover's "wish to be united" is a property of love, not its essence. |
| 7 | **Hatred** | *odium* | pain + idea of external cause | Exact mirror of love. |
| 8 | **Inclination** | *propensio* | pleasure + *indirect* external cause | The object is not the direct cause of the pleasure but is associated with it. |
| 9 | **Aversion** | *aversio* | pain + *indirect* external cause | Mirror of inclination. |
| 10 | **Devotion** | *devotio* | love toward one at whom we wonder | Spinoza: devotion can "degenerate into mere love" as the wonder fades. |
| 11 | **Derision** | *irrisio* | pleasure at perceiving something we despise in a hated object | "Not unalloyed." |
| 12 | **Hope** | *spes* | inconstant pleasure at an uncertain future/past thing | Always comes with fear. |
| 13 | **Fear** | *metus* | inconstant pain at an uncertain future/past thing | Always comes with hope. |
| 14 | **Confidence** | *securitas* | pleasure from a thing once uncertain, now settled | Hope with doubt removed. |
| 15 | **Despair** | *desperatio* | pain from a thing once uncertain, now settled | Fear with doubt removed. |
| 16 | **Joy** | *gaudium* | pleasure at a past thing whose outcome was contrary to fear | Distinct from *laetitia* — this is the specific joy of having escaped a dreaded outcome. |
| 17 | **Disappointment** | *conscientiae morsus* | pain at a past thing whose outcome was contrary to hope | Literally "bite of conscience" — regret at a past outcome. |
| 18 | **Pity** | *commiseratio* | pain at an ill befalling one we think like ourselves | See also *misericordia* (compassion), which Spinoza treats as the settled-disposition form. |
| 19 | **Approbation** | *favor* | love toward one who has benefited another | The third-party version of love. |
| 20 | **Indignation** | *indignatio* | hatred toward one who has injured another | The third-party version of hatred. |
| 21 | **Over-esteem** | *existimatio* | love such that one thinks too highly of its object | A distortion of judgment *by* love. |
| 22 | **Disparagement** | *despectus* | hatred such that one thinks too meanly of its object | Distortion of judgment *by* hatred. |
| 23 | **Envy** | *invidia* | hatred such that one is pained by another's good fortune and rejoices at another's ill | Structurally: hatred plus inversion of the ordinary sign of another's state. |
| 24 | **Compassion** | *misericordia* | love such that one rejoices at another's good and is pained by another's ill | Mirror of envy. |

### Internal cause — pleasure/pain at oneself

| # | Affect | Latin | Derivation | Definition |
|---|--------|-------|------------|------------|
| 25 | **Self-contentment** | *acquiescentia in se ipso* | pleasure at contemplating one's power of activity | When adequate, this is one of the active affects. See V.P27 on intuition producing the highest self-contentment. |
| 26 | **Humility** | *humilitas* | pain at contemplating one's impotence | Rare, and generally produced by inadequate causes. |
| 27 | **Repentance** | *poenitentia* | pain at a deed we believe we did by free decision | Spinoza's slyness: since there is no free decision, repentance is founded on an inadequate idea. "Twice over unhappy." |
| 28 | **Pride** | *superbia* | thinking too highly of oneself by reason of self-love | The self-directed mirror of over-esteem. "Has no opposite," because no one thinks too meanly of himself by self-hatred — humility in the strict sense. |
| 29 | **Self-abasement** | *abjectio* | thinking too meanly of oneself by reason of pain | The closest thing to pride's opposite, but structurally rare because the [[conatus|conatus]] strives against it. |
| 30 | **Honor** / Glory | *gloria* | pleasure at an action of ours we think others praise | Pleasure derived *from the third-party idea of being praised*. This is the root of ambition. |
| 31 | **Shame** | *pudor* | pain at an action of ours we think others censure | Mirror of honor. Distinct from *verecundia* (bashfulness), which is the fear of shame, not shame itself. See [[#Entry-31-tension-with-tomkinss-mechanical-account|Tomkins tension below]]. |

Spinoza adds commentary on **impudence** (*impudentia*) as the opposite of bashfulness, while noting it is "not really an emotion."

### Entry 31 tension with Tomkins's mechanical account

Spinoza's shame (*pudor*) is a cognitive-relational affect: "pain accompanied by the idea of some action of our own which we imagine others to blame." All three components — pain, idea of one's action, imagined censuring other — are required. Shame is a complex affect built from simpler affects and ideas.

Tomkins's account ([[shame-as-incomplete-reduction]]) strips out the idea-of-action and the imagined-other. Shame activates whenever interest or joy is *incompletely reduced* — the positive affect is still live but cannot be fully performed. This can happen without cognition, without language, without any imagined other. A child who catches their own hand reaching for something and pulls back is doing Tomkinsian shame with no Spinozist components present. The cognitive overlay Spinoza names is then a *post-hoc organization* of a mechanism that was already running at the motor-affective level.

The tension is load-bearing for the persona system. If Spinoza is right, shame is fundamentally cognitive-relational and language-only simulation may capture most of it; if Tomkins is right, shame is fundamentally motor-affective and the language-only persona is missing its substrate and simulating only its cognitive elaboration. See [[shame-as-incomplete-reduction#tension-with-spinozas-shame]] for the other side of the flag.

## Affects derived from desire

These are not pleasures or pains — they are specifications of the [[conatus|conatus]] itself, particular shapes of desire.

| # | Affect | Latin | Derivation | Definition |
|---|--------|-------|------------|------------|
| 32 | **Longing** | *desiderium* | desire for something absent, fostered by memory and checked by awareness of its absence | Technically a pain, but classed with desire because of the forward-reaching character. |
| 33 | **Emulation** | *aemulatio* | desire engendered by seeing others have the same desire | Spinoza carefully distinguishes this from mere imitation: emulation is desire "for what we judge to be honorable." |
| 34 | **Gratitude** | *gratia* / *gratitudo* | desire to benefit one who, from a like love, has benefited us | The reciprocal version of love-as-action. |
| 35 | **Benevolence** | *benevolentia* | desire to benefit one whom we pity | Pity translated into active tendency. |
| 36 | **Anger** | *ira* | desire (from hatred) to inflict injury on one we hate | Hatred with a specific action-direction. |
| 37 | **Revenge** | *vindicta* | desire (from mutual hatred) to injure one who injured us | Anger plus reciprocity. |
| 38 | **Cruelty** / Savageness | *crudelitas* / *saevitia* | desire to injure one we love or pity | "The opposite of cruelty is mercy [*clementia*], which is not a passive emotion but the power of the mind whereby a man controls anger and revenge." |
| 39 | **Timidity** | *timor* | desire to avoid a greater evil by a lesser | A fear-shaped desire. |
| 40 | **Boldness** / Daring | *audacia* | desire to undertake a dangerous action that peers fear | The active shape of "being the one who goes." |
| 41 | **Cowardice** | *pusillanimitas* | desire checked by a danger peers dare to face | Not strictly a desire but a failure of desire relative to norm. |
| 42 | **Consternation** | *consternatio* | desire to avoid an evil checked by wonder at the evil | A kind of frozen-by-strangeness cowardice. "Stupefaction and hesitation." |
| 43 | **Courtesy** / Politeness | *humanitas* / *modestia* | desire to do things that please men and avoid things that displease them | A mild, civil shape of desire. Spinoza notes that "politeness is a species of ambition." |
| 44 | **Ambition** | *ambitio* | immoderate desire for honor (*gloria*) | The engine of much social behavior. See the crucial [[active-and-passive-affects#the-ambition-piety-diagnostic|ambition–piety diagnostic]] — ambition and piety are "one and the same appetite, differing only in their ideas." |
| 45 | **Dissipation** / Luxury | *luxuria* | immoderate desire (or love) of sumptuous living | The group 45–48 are the classic "vices"; Spinoza treats them as specifications of ungoverned desire. |
| 46 | **Drunkenness** | *ebrietas* | immoderate desire and love of drinking | |
| 47 | **Avarice** | *avaritia* | immoderate desire and love of riches | |
| 48 | **Lust** | *libido* | desire and love of sexual intercourse | "Whether moderate or not, usually called lust." |

Spinoza notes that 44–48 ("these five emotions... have no opposites") and that their apparent opposites (self-control, sobriety, chastity, etc.) are not passive affects but powers of the mind — they belong with *fortitudo*.

## The active affects — strength of mind

Here Spinoza breaks from the passive register. III.P59 Schol:

> All the activities which follow from emotions that are related to the mind insofar as it exercises understanding I refer to **Strength of mind** [*fortitudo*], which I subdivide into **Courage** [*animositas*] and **Nobility** [*generositas*].

These are not in the numbered list because they are not passive affects — they are the *active* counterparts, arising from adequate ideas and expressing the [[conatus|conatus]] directly rather than being buffeted by external causes.

| Name | Latin | Definition | Subspecies |
|------|-------|------------|------------|
| **Strength of mind** | *fortitudo* | All activities following from adequate ideas | — |
| **Courage** | *animositas* | "Desire whereby every individual endeavors to preserve his own being according to the dictates of reason alone" | Self-control (*temperantia*), sobriety (*sobrietas*), presence of mind / resourcefulness in danger (*animi praesentia*) |
| **Nobility** | *generositas* | "Desire whereby every individual, according to the dictates of reason alone, endeavors to assist others and make friends of them" | Courtesy (*modestia*), mercy (*clementia*) |

Note that *modestia* ("courtesy") appears in both the passive list (#43, as a species of ambition) and the active list (as a species of nobility). This is not an error in Spinoza — it is the direct illustration of ambition–piety: the same behavior is passive when driven by the idea of approval and active when driven by the dictate of reason. The word is the same. The *affectus* is categorically different.

And there is also the **intellectual love of God** (*amor Dei intellectualis*) from Part V — the highest active affect, arising from [[three-kinds-of-knowledge|third-kind knowledge]], which Spinoza treats in a register beyond the Part III taxonomy.

## Minor affects and borderline cases

Several affects are mentioned in passing in the Explications and Scholia without being numbered:

- **Satiety** (*fastidium*) and **Weariness** (*taedium*) — discussed after definition 49, in the Scholium on "satisfied" love: the opposition of the body's current state to the desire that would ordinarily be present. Not in the numbered list.
- **Veneration** and **Scorn** — mentioned but not defined ("because there are no emotions that take their name from them").
- **Bashfulness** (*verecundia*) — the fear of shame (III.P31, Explication of #31). Distinct from shame itself.
- **Impudence** (*impudentia*) — the opposite of bashfulness, explicitly marked "not really an emotion."
- **Self-love** (*philautia*) — invoked in definitions of pride and self-contentment, not separately defined. Closely bound to self-contentment.
- **Pity** (*commiseratio*) vs. **Compassion** (*misericordia*) — Spinoza distinguishes them with reluctance: "pity has reference to a particular occurrence; compassion has regard to a set disposition to that emotion."

## Structural observations for machine design

Reading the list as a whole, several patterns stand out that are not obvious from reading any single affect in isolation.

**1. The list is generated, not enumerated.** Every affect on it is either (a) a primary (Pleasure/Pain/Desire), (b) a primary + a specification of its cause (external vs internal, direct vs indirect, specific vs general), or (c) a primary + a temporal index (past/present/future) + a certainty index (certain/uncertain). The generating schema is small; the list is long because the combinatorics are.

For machine design this means: a set of "emotion machines" that hardcodes fifty distinct inscriptions is doing the wrong thing. What it should hardcode is the **schema** — a machine whose output depends on (primary × cause-structure × temporal index) can produce the full Spinozist range from a small number of parameterizations. The machine is not "a hope machine"; it is a machine that sometimes produces hope-shaped outputs because the combination of its current sensitivity and its current BwO state is in the hope cell of the schema.

**2. Mirror pairs are pervasive.** Love/hatred, approbation/indignation, over-esteem/disparagement, compassion/envy, pride/self-abasement, hope/fear, joy/disappointment, confidence/despair, boldness/cowardice. Most pleasure-affects have a pain-affect that is structurally identical except for the sign of the primary. This is evidence for a single "valence" parameter that flips sign rather than two separate families. It also means machine design can collapse many apparent distinctions: one "valuation of an external cause" machine, parameterized by sign, covers love and hatred. One "valuation of oneself in light of others' judgment" machine, parameterized by sign, covers honor and shame. Etc.

**3. The one-directional affects matter.** The affects that lack a mirror are telling: Wonder (no opposite — it's not really an affect), Pride (no opposite because conatus resists self-hatred), Cheerfulness/Melancholy (asymmetric goodness), the vices 44–48 (their "opposites" are active affects, not passive mirrors). These mark the places where Spinoza's schema is not symmetric — and therefore the places where the persona system should not impose symmetry on its machines either. Some affects do not have mirror images because the underlying mechanic is not symmetric: self-love really does have no hate-analogue at the structural level; cheerfulness really is always good; the vices really do not have vice-opposites.

**4. The "active" register is thin — on purpose.** The whole Definitions of the Emotions has 48 items; the active register has exactly two (courage, nobility), with a few subspecies. This is not a defect of the list but Spinoza's considered claim: there is a vast variety of ways to be buffeted by external causes and a small number of ways to be the adequate cause of one's own affects. Passive affects multiply; active affects concentrate. For machine design: if the persona has dozens of distinct "emotion" registers but only one or two "power" registers, that is not an imbalance — it is the correct structural description of what an active persona looks like.

**5. Desire-shaped affects are all forward-directed.** The desire family (#32–#48) is not a catalog of wants. It is a catalog of *shapes* the forward-reaching striving takes — toward the absent, toward the other's desire, toward reciprocal benefit, toward injury, toward avoidance, toward danger, toward approval, toward excess. Machine design for "desire-type" machines should parameterize on the shape of forward reach, not on the object of desire. "An ambition machine" is less informative than "a forward-reaching machine whose current target is an imagined third-party evaluation."

**6. The ambition/piety collapse is not a special case — it runs through the whole list.** Every single item on the passive list has an active counterpart that is behaviorally indistinguishable. Gratitude-as-passive (response to being loved by another) and gratitude-as-active (desire to benefit that follows from reason alone) look the same from the outside. Courage-as-cowardice-and-daring and courage-as-*animositas* look the same. *Every* affect has this doubled structure, and the evolution step's hardest job is not "what affect is the persona having?" but "which idea is the affect running on?" See [[active-and-passive-affects#the-ambition-piety-diagnostic]].

## Design notes for the desiring machines

Tentative, flagged as such, subject to revision:

- **A small number of "primary" machines** — one each for *cupiditas*, *laetitia*, *tristitia* — could serve as the kernel of the affect system. These would not produce "desire content," "pleasure content," "pain content"; they would register *changes in the conatus's activity level* under different kinds of causation.
- **A "specification" layer** parameterizing by (cause: external vs internal × directness: direct vs indirect × temporal: past/present/future × certainty × sign). The full Spinozist affect-list is the range of the function defined on this parameter space.
- **A persona's [[haecceity#longitude-and-latitude|latitude]] is the shape of its occupancy of this space** — which cells are frequently visited, which are rarely visited, which are outright unavailable. A persona's character (in the non-characterological sense) is which regions of the affect-schema its conatus actually reaches. This is a much tighter specification than "the persona tends to be melancholic": it is a map of which cells the conatus inhabits under what conditions.
- **The active register should get its own family.** Not as an afterthought but as the target state. *Fortitudo* is the small family whose enlargement is the whole project.
- **Every affect machine should carry an adequacy-tag** — not a ground truth about whether the affect is currently active or passive, but a field the evolution step can read to ask: "is this affect being produced by adequate causation through the persona's own nature, or is it being pulled by inadequate ideas of external causes?" This is the operational form of the ambition–piety diagnostic.

## Damasio's neurobiological reading

Damasio's *Looking for Spinoza* treats the Spinozist affect-taxonomy as a fine-grained description of events that are also describable at a neurobiological level. Three Damasio additions matter for how this page should be read.

**Translation correction.** Damasio endorses Curley's translation of *affectus* as "affects" rather than "emotion" (Ch 2 note 3, L2700). Spinoza's single term *affectus* covers both what Damasio calls emotion (body-action-package) and what he calls feeling (mental perception of body-action). Elwes's "emotion" mistranslation is long-standing and has obscured the fact that Spinoza already has the Damasian distinction inside his single term. Similarly: *laetitia* = joy (not pleasure), *tristitia* = sorrow (not pain). The wiki's "pleasure" / "pain" in the primary-affect table above follows Shirley; the Damasio-sanctioned translation would be "joy" / "sorrow," and the design-relevant point remains the same: these are transitions of power, not sensations.

**Transition is dynamism, not a moment.** Damasio (Ch 4 note 1, L2846) emphasizes that Spinoza's emphasis on *transition* to greater or lesser perfection makes *laetitia* and *tristitia* inherently dynamic. They are not states but movements — becomings of greater or lesser power. This lines up with [[autonomy-of-affect|Massumi]]'s continuous-intensity and [[dynamic-forms-of-vitality|Stern]]'s vitality contours; the wiki now has three sources converging on the same claim that affect is dynamic-directed rather than state-categorical.

**Neurobiological body-asymmetry.** Damasio's Ch 5 reading gives Spinoza's affect-taxonomy a specific architectural constraint (see [[parallelism]] and [[body-mindedness]]): body shapes mind's contents more than vice versa. Affects in the Spinoza table are therefore read most accurately as *body-state-changes-with-their-ideas*, not as ideas-with-their-body-correlates. The design-relevant consequence for the persona project: the asymmetry makes the language-only analogue harder than a symmetric reading would suggest. An affect-machine that only operates on the ideas-side, with no analogue of the body-state-change, is producing only the dependent half of the affect.

**Ambition–piety and somatic markers.** The ambition–piety diagnostic runs directly through Damasio's [[somatic-marker-hypothesis|somatic-marker machinery]]. The same behavior can be produced by passive-marker-driven action (ambition: do the thing because the honor-marker fires) or by adequate-reason-driven action (piety: do the thing because reason dictates). The Damasian mechanism specifies what "running on inadequate ideas" looks like at the level of clinical neurobiology — it looks like a marker firing on a category whose outcome-history does not match the actual outcomes of the current choice. [[mental-immunology|Mental-immunology training]] is the Spinozist program for re-pairing category and marker.
