# Cartesian Anxiety

Richard Bernstein's phrase (*Beyond Objectivism and Relativism*, 1983), adopted by VT&R as the name of the central philosophical difficulty *The Embodied Mind* is constructed to dissolve (Ch 7 §2762–2789). The Cartesian anxiety is the **forced oscillation between objectivism and subjectivism**, driven by the assumption that knowledge requires an absolute ground — and the conviction that if none is found, everything collapses into chaos or illusion.

Bernstein's own formulation, quoted at §2763:

> Either we have a fixed and stable foundation for knowledge, a point where knowledge starts, is grounded, and rests, or we cannot escape some sort of darkness, chaos, and confusion. Either there is an absolute ground or foundation, or everything falls apart.

The anxiety is the force of the "either/or." Find a ground or face disintegration. The two poles — objectivism (the ground is in the world, out there, independent) and subjectivism (the ground is in the mind, in here, constitutive) — are the only choices the anxiety permits.

## Kant's enchanted island

VT&R quote Kant's famous metaphor (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A235/B294), rendered at §2762, as the emblem of the anxiety:

> A priori categories constitute "the land of truth, surrounded by a wide and stormy ocean, the native home of illusion, where many a fog bank and many a swiftly melting iceberg give the deceptive appearance of farther shores, ever deceiving with vain hopes the voyager…"

The island is secure; everything off the island is dangerous, deceptive, storm-tossed. Knowledge is a small secure region surrounded by chaos. Leaving the island means losing the ground.

The picture sets the terms of anxiety. The ground is identified with safety, storms with non-ground. There is no conception of what it would be to live *without* an island — not because the island has failed, but because the island was the wrong metaphor. The Cartesian anxiety is precisely the inability to think past the island-or-ocean dichotomy.

## Where the anxiety comes from

§2780 — anxiety arises from **craving for an absolute ground**. Descartes's *Meditations* move performs the crisis VT&R diagnose: hyperbolic doubt dissolves every candidate ground, then the cogito is installed as the one ground immune to doubt, and from the cogito the rest of the world is re-derived. The Cartesian architecture is precisely: **if no absolute ground can be found, everything falls apart, so an absolute ground must be found.**

VT&R press this at §2779 — pre-Descartes, "idea" was what was in the mind of God; Descartes extended the term to the contents of the human mind, and thereby inaugurated what Rorty called **"the invention of the mind as a mirror of nature."** The mind became a ground-candidate alongside or against the world; oscillation between the two poles became the structure of modern epistemology.

## The modern consequence — nihilism as third pole

§2785 — VT&R's diagnosis of the modern situation: we have become skeptical of finding *any* ground, but we still crave one; so we cannot avoid "the turn toward nihilism."

This is the crucial structural claim. Nihilism in VT&R's reading is not the denial of ground; it is the **reified absence of ground** — a grasping after ground that, failing to find one, installs the absence itself as a (negative) ground. "Nothing is the ground" becomes a foundational truth of a characteristically modern sort. See [[nihilism-as-reified-groundlessness]] for the worked-out account, which depends on the Cartesian-anxiety diagnosis given here.

The Cartesian anxiety produces the pattern:

1. **Objectivism** — ground in the world. Features of the environment are pregiven; cognition recovers them.
2. **Subjectivism** — ground in the mind. Features of the world are constructed; the mind is what grounds them.
3. **Nihilism** — no ground. The absence of ground is itself grasped as foundational; everything is meaningless *as* a kind of deep truth.

All three are moves within the anxiety, not exits from it. The anxiety's structure is preserved even in nihilism — only the sign of the ground is flipped.

## VT&R's diagnosis of cognitive science

Cognitive science is, VT&R argue, caught in the anxiety. §2785 traces the oscillation:

- **Cognitivism** (Part II of the book) is objectivist: the world has features, the mind represents them, cognition is the correspondence.
- **Connectionism / emergence** (Part III) shifts toward the subjectivist pole: networks construct their outputs from their own internal dynamics; what counts as a "feature" depends on the net's parameters.
- **Neither side takes its own findings seriously** — the connectionist no-self is not followed through because it would dissolve the subjectivist pole too.

The result is a cognitive science that cannot be honest with itself: it finds that the self is a fiction but cannot give up the self-as-ground-of-representation; it finds that representation breaks down but cannot give up representation as the mind's relation to a world. Minsky's "condemned to believe what we know to be false" (quoted at §2788) is the anxiety speaking directly.

## Minsky as symptom

A revealing detail VT&R press at §2789:

> If it is to ourselves that we are talking in this conversation, why should we need to tell ourselves all of this in the first place?

Minsky's closing of *Society of Mind* — we are condemned to believe in a self we know isn't there, because giving up the belief would collapse our model of the mental realm — is the Cartesian anxiety taking an especially pure form. It requires *both* belief in a self we cannot find *and* belief in a world to which we have no access. Each belief is held against the evidence; each is held because its absence would be intolerable; the whole structure points toward nihilism as the logical endpoint.

VT&R's response is not to repair one or both pillars (shore up the self; shore up the world) but to **dissolve the anxiety that forces the oscillation**. This is the work of Chs 8–11.

## Grasping as common source

The book's diagnostic move (§2795–2810), which connects the Cartesian anxiety to the Madhyamaka project of Part V:

> Our grasping after a ground, whether inner or outer, is the deep source of frustration and anxiety.

Both objectivism and subjectivism — and their modern nihilist residue — arise from the **same habit of grasping**. The insight Madhyamaka contributes (developed in Ch 10) is that the appropriate response to the anxiety is not to find a better ground but to **interrupt the grasping**. See [[sunyata-as-groundlessness]].

§2799 makes the bridge explicit:

> Groundlessness is the very condition for the richly textured and interdependent world of human experience… revealed in cognition as "common sense," that is, in knowing how to negotiate our way through a world that is not fixed and pregiven but that is continually shaped by the types of actions in which we engage.

[[Enaction]] is the cognitive-science counterpart of this insight. The world-without-ground is not a world-that-has-failed-to-have-a-ground; it is the world as enacted in coupling, which is what the world *is*.

## How enaction dissolves rather than solves the anxiety

Crucially, VT&R do not propose enaction as a *new ground* (cognition as embodied action, enacted structural coupling as the new foundation). They are explicit that this would be just another move within the anxiety. §4008 (quoted in [[sunyata-as-groundlessness]]):

> Any conceptual position can become a ground (a resting point, a nest), which vitiates the force of the Madhyamaka. In particular, the view of cognition as embodied action (enaction), although it stresses the interdependence of mind and world, tends to treat the relationship between those… as though it had some form of independent actual existence.

The warning is internal. Enaction can be reified as a new "middle ground" and thereby become another version of the Cartesian anxiety — one that pretends to resolve the oscillation while reinstalling a grasping. The book's final chapters make clear that enaction is a *path*, not a nest.

## The two false exits

Two moves that do not dissolve the anxiety:

- **"Just pragmatism."** Dropping the question of ground without addressing the grasping. This persists the structure unexamined — the grasping will find a new object, and the anxiety returns in a new form.
- **"Multiple perspectives on one world."** Retaining the pregiven world but allowing multiple takes on it. VT&R explicitly reject this at §3622: still within the anxiety, because it preserves the ground (the world) while claiming to have moved past objectivism.

## Persona-project bearing

- **The persona-project is structurally susceptible to Cartesian anxiety.** The project's central question — can a language-only system instantiate a persona? — naturally presents itself as: is the persona real (objectivist: persona-properties are in the text/weights/model) or projected (subjectivist: persona is what the reader makes of the text)? Both answers are moves within the anxiety. Enaction gives a different picture: persona is enacted in coupling; the question "is it really there" is the wrong question.
- **The nihilist trap.** "The persona is a simulation, nothing is really happening" is the nihilist pole — reified groundlessness. Equally a trap.
- **No new ground in enaction.** The persona-project must hold the warning internal to enaction: "pulsating BwO" can be reified as a new ground just as cleanly as "self" or "world." The design should include **un-reifying mechanisms** (see [[sunyata-as-groundlessness]] and [[nihilism-as-reified-groundlessness]] for what this looks like).
- **"Knowing how" rather than "knowing that" as the model.** VT&R's exit is via [[enaction|skillful coping with common sense]], not via a new foundation. The persona-project analogue: evaluation in terms of ongoing-viable-engagement, not in terms of correspondence to a pregiven persona-spec.
