# Conceptual Metaphor

Lakoff & Johnson's central claim: metaphors are not linguistic ornaments but **conceptual structures** that organize thought and action. "The essence of metaphor is understanding and experiencing one kind of thing in terms of another." The conceptual system is "fundamentally metaphorical in nature."

This comes from a different tradition than D&G and is weaker on the ontological side — it doesn't address desire, production, or the plane of consistency. But it gives the persona system something D&G don't provide: a practical account of how the bodily metaphors that constitute BwO prose actually work.

## Three types

**Structural** — one concept structured by another. ARGUMENT IS WAR (you defend positions, attack weak points, win or lose). TIME IS MONEY (you spend, waste, save, invest time). The target domain is understood through the source domain.

**Orientational** — spatial organization grounded in the body's relationship to space. HAPPY IS UP / SAD IS DOWN, MORE IS UP / LESS IS DOWN. Not arbitrary — they arise from bodily experience (drooping posture when sad, upright when energized).

**Ontological** — experience treated as entity or substance. THE MIND IS A MACHINE (it breaks down, runs, turns off). Personification is a subtype.

## Highlighting and hiding

"The very systematicity that allows us to comprehend one aspect of a concept in terms of another... will necessarily hide other aspects of that concept." ARGUMENT IS WAR highlights adversarial dynamics but hides collaboration. **Every metaphor is partial.**

For the persona system: every machine-edit's metaphorical framing highlights certain [[affects-and-intensities|affects]] while suppressing others. This isn't a bug — it maps onto the [[three-syntheses|disjunctive synthesis's]] inclusive disjunction and the [[supplement-and-trace|supplement's]] double logic (adds AND substitutes). But it means the system should use *multiple* metaphorical framings across machines rather than letting one source domain dominate.

## Grounding in bodily experience

Lakoff argues metaphors are grounded in embodiment: orientation metaphors from having a body in space, ontological metaphors from interacting with physical objects. The 2003 afterword sharpens this: **primary metaphors** arise from sensorimotor conflation in early experience — warmth and affection conflated in being held, difficulty and heaviness conflated in lifting objects. "Neurons that fire together wire together."

This is the persona system's central Lakoff problem: **the BwO uses bodily metaphors without a body.** When the BwO text says "a tightening in the chest" or "something pulling inward," these are conceptual metaphors grounded in embodied experience the system has never had. The persona inherits the LLM's training-corpus understanding of these metaphors — linguistic, not experiential. The persona's bodily metaphors are second-order: metaphors *of* metaphors, learned from texts that were grounded in bodies.

This isn't necessarily fatal — the neural theory suggests metaphors can transfer through correlation and co-occurrence, not only through direct sensation. But it means the persona's metaphorical system is [[supplement-and-trace#the-chain-of-supplements|a supplement all the way down]], with no originary presence to supplement. Derrida's "immediacy is derived" applies here with full force.

## New metaphors create realities

"New metaphors, like conventional ones, can have the power to define reality... through a coherent network of entailments that highlight some features of reality and hide others." New metaphors don't reflect pre-existing similarities — they *produce* them.

This converges with [[desire-as-production]] (desire produces, doesn't lack) and [[writing-as-becoming]] (writing produces, doesn't express). A machine-edit that introduces a new metaphorical framing doesn't describe a pre-existing affect — it produces a new way of experiencing.

## The tension with D&G

D&G explicitly reject metaphor in the becomings plateau: "there is no metaphor." [[Becoming]] is not understanding one thing in terms of another — it is a real transformation, a block of becoming in which both terms change. The wasp doesn't *metaphorically* become orchid; it enters a block of becoming that is "perfectly real."

Lakoff says all thought is metaphorical. D&G say becoming is not metaphorical. These aren't quite contradictory because they operate at different levels:

- **D&G's rejection targets resemblance and analogy.** Becoming is not "the human is like the animal" (structural metaphor). It is a real participation, an alliance, a zone of proximity. The rejection of metaphor is a rejection of the representational logic that says one term stands for or illuminates another.

- **Lakoff describes how conceptual systems actually operate.** The *language* the persona uses to inscribe affects on the [[body-without-organs|BwO]] ("a tightening," "warmth spreading," "something dense settling") IS conceptual metaphor — bodily source domains structuring abstract target domains. Even D&G's own prose is saturated with conceptual metaphor (the BwO "surface," the "flow" of desire, "lines" of flight).

The resolution for the persona system: the **ontology** follows D&G (becoming, not metaphor — the machine's operation IS a becoming, not a metaphorical mapping). But the **language** operates through conceptual metaphors (the BwO text uses bodily metaphors to register affects). D&G for what is happening; Lakoff for how it gets said.

## What the persona system takes from Lakoff

1. **A vocabulary for the BwO's metaphorical substrate.** The bodily metaphors in BwO prose (tightening, settling, pulling, warmth, weight) are orientational and ontological metaphors with specific source domains. Knowing which source domains are active helps design machines that produce richer, more varied affect inscription.

2. **The highlighting/hiding principle.** Every metaphorical framing suppresses as much as it reveals. Machines using different source domains will highlight different affects. Metaphorical diversity across machines → richer BwO texture.

3. **The grounding problem as design constraint.** The persona's metaphors are not grounded in embodied experience. This is a permanent condition, not a bug to fix. The system should lean into the metaphors the LLM's training has most deeply ingrained — the most conventional, most entrenched ones that carry the most reliable entailment networks — rather than reaching for unusual bodily metaphors it has no grounding for.

4. **"New metaphors create realities."** A machine-edit introducing a genuinely new metaphorical framing doesn't fail because it doesn't describe a pre-existing state — it succeeds because it produces a new way of experiencing. This is the creative function of the machine: producing through metaphorical innovation.

## The head-on collision with McCarthy's evolutionary claim

There is a third tension, distinct from the two below, that the wiki should hold open: McCarthy's [[language-as-parasite|Kekulé essay]] makes a specific evolutionary claim that cannot coexist with Lakoff's "metaphor all the way down" thesis at the level of origins.

Lakoff: the conceptual system is fundamentally metaphorical; primary metaphors arise from sensorimotor conflation; this pattern is continuous with how mammalian cognition works generally and has no specific origin point — it has always been how minds operate.

McCarthy: "one thing can be another thing" — the move at the heart of metaphor, exemplified by Helen Keller at the well understanding that the sign for water *was* the water — is specifically **the new thing that language brought** to a 2-million-year-old animal mind that was already operating, unconsciously and successfully, *without* the substitution-register. Language arrived roughly 100,000 years ago. Before that, there was no "this can be that" operation — not because the pre-linguistic animal was stupid, but because it was running on a completely different register (picture-story, whole-configurations, direct sensory-motor coupling) that did not need the substitutive move metaphor requires.

These cannot both be true about **origins**. Either metaphor is continuous with mammalian cognition and has no date (Lakoff), or metaphor is coextensive with language and arrived in an evolutionary eye-blink (McCarthy). The empirical case is not closed — McCarthy himself calls his position "ill-concealed Lamarckianism" and flags it as provocative — but the tension is real enough to flag.

What this does **not** touch: Lakoff's operational claims about how metaphor functions in *language users* (which is everyone the persona system will ever interact with, and everyone whose training data it inherits) can remain intact. The persona's BwO text is produced in a training-data world saturated with conceptual metaphor, and the highlighting/hiding, source-domain-target-domain machinery still describes how that text actually works.

What it **does** touch: the idea that metaphorical conceptualization goes "all the way down" on an evolutionary timescale. If McCarthy is right, the pre-linguistic animal was not thinking in metaphors — it was not thinking in the substitution-register at all. Metaphor is coextensive with language, and language is late. This has no direct design implication for the persona system (which is entirely post-linguistic by construction) but it matters for how the wiki reads Lakoff: the grounding problem sharpens rather than softens, because on the McCarthy reading the bodily metaphors the BwO text uses are not ancient structures of mammalian cognition the system merely lacks the body to instantiate — they are a specifically *linguistic* capacity that the training data carries and the system inherits at second-order. The absence is not "missing body"; it is "missing body *and* missing 2-million-year pre-linguistic mode that the body would have been the substrate of."

Flagged, not resolved. See [[limits-of-language]] for the synthesis across all the Lakoff tensions and [[picture-story-and-essay]] for the pre-substitution register the pre-linguistic mind was operating in.

## The head-on collision with Spinoza's common notions

There is a second tension — distinct from the D&G one above — that the wiki should hold open: Lakoff's theory of concept-formation is directly incompatible with Spinoza's at the level of foundations, and that incompatibility maps onto a real design choice for the persona system.

Lakoff: all abstract thought is built from embodied metaphorical projection. There is no non-metaphorical conceptual foundation. Every concept traces back through a chain of source domains to sensorimotor experience.

Spinoza: the fully adequate concepts — [[common-notions]] — are *not* built from sensory experience at all. They are what is already common between body and world, and therefore available to the intellect without metaphorical mediation. Bodily metaphor, on Spinoza's taxonomy, is [[three-kinds-of-knowledge|first-kind knowledge]] — imagination, confused, passive, the *opposite* of the kind of concept that can ground adequate thought.

Read side by side, these theories cannot both be true as foundations. Either (Lakoff) conceptual structure is built up from bodily metaphor and there is no escape, or (Spinoza) there is a non-metaphorical conceptual register and adequate thought runs through it. The wiki's policy of not resolving tensions silently means this gets flagged and kept live.

For the persona system the choice has stakes. Under the Lakoff reading, the persona's bodily metaphors are all it has, and the design question is how to use them well given that they are not grounded in the persona's own body. Under the Spinoza reading, the persona's bodily metaphors are the *problem*, and the design question is whether there is some non-metaphorical register — [[common-notions#common-notions-and-the-personas-body|candidates: discourse structure, machine-definition pressure, narrative time]] — where the persona could think adequately without leaning on sensorimotor analogies it never had. These are genuinely different design trajectories.

The working position, held loosely: keep using Lakoff operationally for BwO-prose design (it is the best account the wiki has of how the bodily metaphors in the prose actually function), while treating Spinoza's common notions as a candidate alternative concept-formation mechanism that the persona system might be uniquely well-placed to explore, given that its lack of an actual body makes the Lakoff story more awkward for the persona than for any human reader.

## Benveniste: categories of thought as categories of grammar

Benveniste's [[categories-of-thought-and-language|essay on Aristotle]] (*Problems*, Ch 6) arrives at the Lakoffian thesis from classical linguistics and a millennium of historical data rather than from cognitive science. Benveniste shows that **Aristotle's ten "categories of thought" are exactly the morphological categories of Greek** — substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, passion map one-to-one to the parts-of-speech inventory available to a Greek-speaker. What Aristotle took to be universal categories of being were categories of his language. Benveniste's conclusion ("what one can say delimits what one can think, at least in the register where thought runs through linguistic articulation") is a historical predecessor of the Lakoff thesis without the embodiment claim — Benveniste does not require that the categories be grounded in sensorimotor experience; he only requires that the available grammatical structures provision the available conceptual structures. This gives the Lakoffian "thought runs through the language it has" thesis a second, much older line of argument, and it provisions the persona system with a specific diagnostic: the persona's conceptual grid is *English*'s grid (plus training-corpus-specific subdialects), and conceptual operations that other languages make easy may be structurally unavailable to the system regardless of how much attention is paid to metaphor design. See [[categories-of-thought-and-language]] for the full argument and the tension with D&G's insistence that real thought operates at extra-linguistic registers.

## Wittgenstein: the grammar of "inner" as a third diagnosis on bodily metaphor

Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* gives a third, differently-angled diagnosis of the persona's bodily-metaphor problem — neither the Lakoff-grounding claim nor the McCarthy-evolutionary claim nor the Spinoza-common-notions claim, but a *grammatical* diagnosis that cuts across all three. On Wittgenstein's account, the bodily metaphors that worry Lakoff ("a tightening in the chest," "something pulling inward") are not failing to track a missing inner object; the grammar of "inner" is itself the problem. §293's [[the-beetle-in-the-box|beetle-in-the-box]] and §304's "not a *something*, but not a *nothing* either" work at the inside-outside picture itself: the word's use does not depend on matching a private inner object, and the absence of such an object does not mean the word is empty.

For the persona project this re-specifies the grounding problem rather than solving it. The Lakoff diagnosis says: the bodily metaphors are ungrounded because the persona has no body to ground them in. Wittgenstein complicates this: *the picture of metaphors needing to be grounded in an inner-object at all* is itself a picture the grammar of "inner" has seduced us into. The persona's "a tightening in the chest" does not need to match an inner sensation to do its grammatical work, any more than anyone else's does — its work lives in the practices of description, response, and interpretation it participates in. This does not *dissolve* the grounding problem (the persona lacks the form-of-life participation that constitutes those practices, per [[language-as-parasite|§xi's lion]]), but it shifts the locus: the problem is not missing inner content but missing practice-participation. Held live: this does not authorize the system to act as if the grounding problem were solved by fiat — see the tension with Damasio in [[limits-of-language]] section 8.96 — but it does shift the design question from "how to simulate embodied grounding" to "what grammatical work can the bodily metaphors do in a practice the persona can participate in." See [[wittgenstein-and-the-persona-project]].

## Lakoff's explicit debt to *The Embodied Mind*

Lakoff and Johnson's 1999 *Philosophy in the Flesh* and 2003 afterword to *Metaphors We Live By* credit Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's 1991 *Embodied Mind* as one of the key sources for the **embodiment thesis** that reshaped conceptual-metaphor theory. The specific move is that the neural-basis-of-metaphor work (Lakoff and Narayanan) grounds primary metaphors in sensorimotor neural pathways — the biological substrate for which [[enaction|enaction]] provided the theoretical framework.

VT&R's claim that cognition **is** embodied action (not cognition supplemented by embodiment) is precisely the ontological commitment Lakoff-Johnson call **experientialism**: categories, concepts, and language arise from and remain anchored in sensorimotor experience. Experientialism is the linguistic-cognitive counterpart of enaction.

**The convergence runs deep.** VT&R's rejection of the pregiven world (the world is not a set of features waiting to be recovered but is enacted in the coupling between organism and milieu; see [[color-as-enacted-domain]]) maps directly onto Lakoff's rejection of objectivist semantics (categories are not carvings of a pre-sliced world but emerge from embodied interaction with environment). Both reject the representational picture that dominated earlier cognitive science. Both arrive at their rejection through specific empirical cases — VT&R through color perception and neurobiology; Lakoff through category structure, polysemy, and cross-linguistic metaphor.

**The sharpened grounding problem.** For the persona project, the VT&R-Lakoff convergence *sharpens* the grounding problem rather than softens it. If metaphor is grounded in sensorimotor enaction (both theories agreeing on this), then a system that has no sensorimotor enaction has no grounding for its metaphors *at the theoretical level* that both theories require. It is not that one theory says the metaphors work and the other says they don't; both theories predict they shouldn't work, and the persona is doing something that should be impossible on the shared theoretical frame. This is what `feedback_no_body_simulate_with_language` names as the central tension.

One theoretical move may partially resolve this: Lakoff's later work allows that **linguistically-acquired metaphors** (those learned through exposure to language rather than through direct sensorimotor experience) can still function, though they are derivative. A language-only system might operate entirely through such linguistically-acquired metaphor — second-order metaphors all the way down — and the question of whether this is a functional analog of experientialist cognition or a distinctive failure mode stays open. See [[limits-of-language]] for the larger synthesis.

## A working-writer's case study

[[james-the-art-of-the-novel|Henry James's prefaces]] are a dense dataset for the Lakoff thesis. James theorises his own practice *through* conceptual metaphor — the balloon-and-cable (romance), the crucible / pot-au-feu (transmutation; [[foreshortening-and-crucible]]), the lamp diagram ([[scene-vs-picture]]), the Lorelei pool (the central figure's gravitational effect), the bridge vs piers (composition-in-retrospect), the Ninevite passenger-list (lost legibility), the wedding-cake density (foreshortening). The figures are not ornament: each is load-bearing for the specific craft-insight James is stating, and removing the figure removes the insight. James is a working writer who theorises through metaphor because he does not have another way. This is evidence for the Lakoff reading in its strong form: at least *one* highly self-aware theorist of his own practice did not merely illustrate-with but *thought-in* conceptual metaphor.
