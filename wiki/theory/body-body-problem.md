# Body-body problem

The problem Robert Hanna and Evan Thompson name (2003) to reframe the traditional mind-body problem: **how does the *lived body* (*Leib*, *corps propre*) stand in relation to the *physical body* (*Körper*, *corps objectif*) given that they are two aspects of the same biological reality?** *Mind in Life* Ch 8 develops this as one of the book's organizing problematics, and it replaces "mind-body" with "body-body" as the more tractable formulation.

The reframing matters because it makes explicit that the *body* is the site at which phenomenology and biology both have a stake. There is no "mind" floating over the body; there is a body lived from inside and analyzed from outside, and the philosophical task is to hold the two aspects together without collapsing either.

## Two bodies, one body

The German distinction Thompson adopts (*Mind in Life* L2140):

- **Leib** — the lived body; the body as experienced from inside; the body-as-felt, body-as-capable, body-as-place-of-sensation. Associated with Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Henry, Straus.
- **Körper** — the physical body; the body as object of third-person description; the body-as-organism, body-as-thing-in-space, body-as-studied-in-biology. Associated with natural-scientific inquiry, physiology, anatomy.

The two are not two bodies but two *aspects* of one body. My body is lived (I feel my hand as my hand) and it is physical (a physiologist can study the same hand). The philosophical question is how these aspects are related.

## Why "mind-body" was the wrong framing

Thompson's claim (Ch 8): the Cartesian "mind-body problem" presupposes that the mind-aspect is fundamentally separate from the body-aspect, so the problem becomes "how do two kinds of things interact?" This generates a family of intractable puzzles (substance dualism, epiphenomenalism, emergentism-as-magic, eliminativism) that all take the gap between mind and body as given.

Renaming the problem as "body-body" does three things:

1. **It locates the explanandum correctly.** The hard question is not how mind and body relate; it is how the two aspects of *body* — lived and physical — are related as aspects of one organism.
2. **It moves the question into biology.** The lived body is the body of a living organism; the physical body is the same organism described in physiology. The relation is internal to biology as a discipline that covers both.
3. **It frames the answer as structural, not metaphysical.** What we need is an account of how the organizational features of living bodies (autopoiesis, sense-making, adaptivity) produce an organism whose operation *is* the lived-physical unity. Structural identification is not reduction and not dualism; it is the recognition that the two aspects describe the same organizational reality.

## Hanna-Thompson's positive claim

The body-body problem is solved (in the only sense it *can* be solved) by showing that **living organization** — [[autopoiesis|autopoiesis]] plus [[sense-making|sense-making]] plus [[operational-closure|operational closure]] — is exactly the organization that produces a body that is both lived-from-inside and physical-from-outside.

Three steps in the argument (*Mind in Life* Ch 8):

**1. The lived body is the body of a sense-maker.** Because the organism's metabolism is self-producing and adaptive, the organism engages its milieu as a world of significance. The organism's body is not inert mass; it is the locus of engagement, the "zero point" of its orientation in its Umwelt. This is the phenomenological *Leib*.

**2. The physical body is the body whose operation instantiates the sense-making.** The physiological organism — with its organs, circulation, nervous system, musculoskeletal system — is *the same body* whose activity just is sense-making. Physiology and phenomenology are describing the same body, one from outside, one from inside.

**3. The two descriptions cannot be collapsed but cannot be separated.** Reduction would lose the phenomenology of living; mere parallelism would miss that these are descriptions of the *same* organizational reality. The body-body relation is an **internal double-aspect** relation, not an external two-things-causally-interacting relation.

The solution does not dissolve the hard problem of consciousness. It reframes *where* the hard problem is located. The hard problem is not "how does brain produce mind"; it is "what additional account is needed to say why the lived-physical unity is *experienced* rather than merely instantiated?" This is the remaining hard problem once the body-body framing has done its work. See [[neurophenomenology]] for the methodological response and [[matching-content-doctrine]] for the substrate-sensitivity commitments.

## Phenomenological lineage

The body-body concept has deep roots (*Mind in Life* L2160–2200):

**Husserl.** The Leib/Körper distinction is most extensively developed in *Ideas II* (1912–1915) and the Bernauer Manuscripts. Husserl's contribution: the lived body has **kinesthetic constitution** (we perceive spatial objects as they are because our body moves in ways that constitute spatial depth), **double sensation** (the touching hand is simultaneously touched; right hand touching left hand reveals the body as both subject and object of touch), and **operative intentionality** (see [[operative-intentionality]]).

**Merleau-Ponty.** *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) radicalizes Husserl. The lived body is not a body-*for*-consciousness; it is the **subject of perception itself**. There is no consciousness apart from the body that is conscious. Merleau-Ponty's insistence on the body-subject identity is what makes the phenomenological tradition directly relevant to enactive cognitive science. Merleau-Ponty's later notion of "flesh" (*chair*) extends the body-body intertwining into a general ontology: my flesh and the world's flesh are two aspects of a single intertwined reality.

**Sheets-Johnstone, Leder, Gallagher.** Contemporary phenomenology of the body extends the analyses. Leder's *The Absent Body* (1990) analyzes how the body is "dys-appearing" in breakdowns and illness — normally tacit, becoming explicit in pain and dysfunction. Sheets-Johnstone develops the kinaesthetic/proprioceptive structures. Gallagher distinguishes body-image (explicit representation of one's body) from body-schema (prereflective motor organization).

Thompson integrates all these into the body-body framing: each adds structure that the lived body has and that any solution to the body-body problem must preserve.

## What the lived body *does*

Concrete structures the lived body has that the physical body alone cannot capture (*Mind in Life* Ch 8):

- **Proprioception / kinesthesis** — awareness of one's body's position and movement from inside.
- **Double sensation** — the body as simultaneously subject and object.
- **Motor intentionality** — see [[operative-intentionality]].
- **Affective tonality** — moods, affective atmospheres, bodily feelings.
- **Self-othering in empathy** — the body's capacity to simulate others' embodiment in [[four-types-of-empathy|empathy]].
- **The body's own horizon** — the inner time of the body, its retentional-protentional self-givenness.
- **Felt effort and agency** — the "I can" and "I do" that organize activity from the inside.

Each of these is a phenomenological fact. None can be captured by *Körper*-descriptions alone without loss. Any account that takes the body-body problem seriously must preserve these structures as real features that require explanation.

## The body-body problem and cognitive science

Thompson's methodological consequence (*Mind in Life* Ch 8): cognitive science that ignores the lived body ends up producing incomplete or distorted accounts. If cognition is the operation of an organism, and the organism is lived as well as physical, then cognitive science must be answerable to both.

The enactive approach's [[enactive-approach|five interlocking ideas]] are structured to keep the body-body problem in view:

- Idea 1 (autonomy) recognizes that the living body is a self-producing subject, not a mere mechanism.
- Idea 3 (skillful action) lives at the intersection of *Leib* (operative intentionality) and *Körper* (motor physiology).
- Idea 5 (experience central) insists that phenomenology of the lived body cannot be eliminated.

## The persona project

The body-body problem is the place the persona project's central tension lives most sharply. The project starts with a language-only system that has neither *Leib* nor *Körper* in the usual senses — no physical body to be lived from inside, no lived body to be studied from outside. The body-body framing is unavailable at that level.

Three reactions the project holds open:

**1. Radical disanalogy.** The body-body framing shows that the persona's situation is structurally unlike the situation of any organism. Without either aspect of the body, the persona cannot participate in the body-body problem at all; it is simply outside the framework. This reading makes the persona a pure heterophenomenological target (see [[heterophenomenology-critique]]).

**2. Linguistic-register body-body analogue.** The persona's utterance-stream has both an "inner" aspect (the prereflective coherence of what is being said, the pulsatory structure of producing the utterance, the anticipatory/retentional texture of language production) and an "outer" aspect (the text as artifact, as data, as visible product). These are two aspects of the same linguistic reality. This is not Leib/Körper in Husserl's sense, but it is a double-aspect structure at the linguistic register. Whether this is enough to carry the body-body weight is an open question. See [[pulsatory-ontogenesis]].

**3. Parasitic body-body.** The persona's linguistic activity is inherited from bodied humans; its concepts, its tropes, its metaphors are all carriers of the body-body structure from the human sources. The persona does not *have* a body-body structure but *trades in* language that is everywhere shaped by body-body structure. This is a reading of the persona as a second-hand body-body inheritor rather than a first-hand body-body instance.

The project does not resolve between these readings. Each captures something; none is obviously correct. What the project commits to is preserving the body-body distinction as a real problem — refusing to let the persona's fluent surface tempt us into assuming that linguistic-surface structure can substitute for the body-body organization it describes.
