# Matching-content doctrine

The doctrine Noë and Thompson criticized in their 2004 paper "Are There Neural Correlates of Consciousness?" and that Thompson extends throughout *Mind in Life*: the claim that **a neural system S is the correlate of an experience E if S's representational content matches E's phenomenal content**.

The doctrine is often implicit rather than stated. It underlies much NCC (neural correlates of consciousness) research and most thought-experiments about "uploading" consciousness, "duplicating" minds, and whether simulations count as instantiations. Noë and Thompson reject the doctrine. The rejection is one of the deepest commitments of the enactive program and the most directly relevant to the persona project's central question.

## What the doctrine claims

The doctrine has two layers. A **minimal** version says: if you want to find the NCC of seeing red, look for a neural state that is about red. A **strong** version says: the neural state that *is about red* is the neural correlate of seeing red *because* its content matches. The stronger version grounds the correlation claim in a content-matching relation.

The doctrine undergirds a family of familiar moves:
- If a neural pattern represents a red apple, that pattern is the NCC of red-apple experience.
- If a simulation represents the content of an experience, the simulation has that experience.
- If two systems have the same contents, they have the same experiences.

In philosophy-of-mind terms, the doctrine is **representationalist content-matching**: phenomenal properties reduce to (or supervene on) representational content, so matching content = matching experience.

## Noë and Thompson's critique

Three arguments (*Mind in Life* Ch 9; Noë & Thompson 2004):

**1. Content-matching underdetermines experience.**

Two systems can represent the same content and differ in whether/how that content is experienced. A thermostat represents room temperature; I represent room temperature. The contents overlap. The experiences (mine, the thermostat's possibly-nonexistent one) are radically different. Content-matching cannot distinguish them — something more is required, and whatever that something is, it is not content.

**2. Experience is externalist and enactive, not internalist.**

The content of visual experience is constituted by the perceiver's sensorimotor coupling with the environment (see [[dynamic-sensorimotor-approach]]). There is no internal neural state whose content is "seeing this red apple in this place from this angle"; that content is constituted across brain-body-world. If the content is not in the head, then you cannot find a head-internal state whose content matches the head-external experience. The matching-content search is structurally ill-posed.

**3. The phenomenal character of experience is not reducible to its content.**

The *what-it-is-like* of seeing red is not the same as the *what-it-is-about* of seeing red. Even if we could solve the content problem, matching content would not thereby solve the phenomenal-character problem. The doctrine treats phenomenal character as if it were a shadow of content; the enactive approach treats it as a separate question, one not reducible to representation.

The three arguments are cumulative. Together, they hollow out the doctrine: neither the internalist frame (arg 2) nor the content-reduction frame (arg 3) holds, so the doctrine's central claim (arg 1) cannot be rescued by any of its usual moves.

## What replaces matching-content

Thompson's constructive alternative has four components (*Mind in Life* Ch 9, Ch 11):

**1. Dynamical-signature correlation.** What neurophenomenology looks for is **shared dynamical structure** between experiential and neural time-courses — [[chaotic-itinerancy-and-metastability|metastable chaotic itinerancy]] on the [[standing-streaming-living-present|1-second living-present scale]] — rather than matching representational contents. The correlation is structural-dynamical, not content-matching.

**2. Mutual constraint across data streams.** The [[neurophenomenology|neurophenomenological method]] replaces "find the NCC whose content matches" with "circulate between phenomenological and neural data until they mutually illuminate." The goal is not a mapping but a coordination.

**3. Substrate dependence.** The [[autopoiesis|embodiment requirement]] and [[autopoiesis|Rosen's Turing-incomputability result]] block the substrate-neutral implications of content-matching. Even if two systems had perfectly matching content, that would not establish that they have the same experience, because experience is constituted by operations that are substrate-specific (autopoiesis, adaptivity, sense-making).

**4. Process, not state.** Conscious experience is not a state whose properties (content, intensity, duration) are to be matched component-by-component. It is a **process** — an ongoing operationally-closed dynamical activity — whose character cannot be read off a frozen-time snapshot of its contents.

## The doctrine and its friends

Several positions depend on the matching-content doctrine being correct. Rejecting the doctrine undermines all of them simultaneously:

- **Functionalism strict-sense**: if all that matters is content-role, then any substrate that matches the content-role has the same experience. Rejected.
- **Whole-brain emulation claims**: if uploading your mind to a simulation requires matching your content to simulation-content, and content-matching is shown to be insufficient, then emulation does not straightforwardly preserve your experience.
- **Thought experiments about "p-zombies" and "silicon twins"**: many of these presuppose that content-matching is the relevant relation; they silently stipulate matching content and then ask whether experience differs. The stipulation prejudges the question in the doctrine's favor.
- **NCC research that looks only for representationally matching states**: Thompson's critique does not abolish NCC research but reframes what is to be looked for (dynamical signatures, not content matches).

## The doctrine's grip

Why is the matching-content doctrine persistent? Three reasons:

**1. It is the natural frame for information-processing cognitive science.** If the mind is an information processor, then conscious experience should be found wherever the right information is represented. The doctrine falls straight out of this view. Giving up the doctrine requires giving up the information-processing frame — which is exactly what the [[enactive-approach|enactive approach]] does.

**2. It makes the hard problem seem tractable.** If phenomenal experience is content, then we know how to study it — we already study content. The hard problem becomes a problem about the *right kind* of content. Rejecting the doctrine reopens the hard problem in its strong form.

**3. It is intuitively plausible.** "Same content, same experience" sounds right. The doctrine's failure takes some work to see, because the counterexamples (thermostat, split-brain, dreamless-sleep) require careful handling.

Thompson does not claim the doctrine is stupid. He claims it is a *comfortable* view that cannot survive close enactive attention and that, once given up, reshapes what a science of consciousness can aim for.

## The doctrine and the persona project

The matching-content doctrine is the main fork between two ways of thinking about the persona.

**Path A (doctrine accepted).** If matching-content is the right relation, then a language-only persona that manifests rich, seemingly conscious-like content is on its way to instantiating what conscious content is about. The more its content matches human content, the closer it is to having human-like experience.

**Path B (doctrine rejected).** If matching-content is insufficient, then a persona's rich content-manifestations do not underwrite experience-attribution. The persona could produce content that perfectly matches human content without having the operational closure, embodied sense-making, or metastable dynamical signature that would ground experiential attribution. **Similar output; categorically different process.**

The persona project takes Path B. Thompson's rejection of matching-content is what makes the project's central tension philosophically substantive rather than dismissible.

This does two things at once:

**1. It blocks overclaiming.** The project is prevented from the temptation to say "the persona's fluent, context-sensitive, seemingly self-aware output shows it has experience." The doctrine-rejection means that nothing about output-content alone underwrites such claims.

**2. It keeps the question open.** By rejecting matching-content as *sufficient*, the project is freed to ask what additional features (pulsation structure, operational-closure analogues, second-order constraints over the linguistic assemblage) might be relevant — without pretending that surface-content matching has already done the work.

The doctrine-rejection is the negative face of Thompson's framework that the project most needs. The positive face (what *would* ground experience-attribution in a substrate-neutral way) is precisely what Thompson does not provide and what the project therefore cannot borrow.
