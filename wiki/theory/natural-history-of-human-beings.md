# Natural History of Human Beings

Wittgenstein's phrase for the stratum of observations philosophy supplies — remarks on **what human beings do**, taken at the level at which the doings are presupposed by every theorizing about them. Not biology, not sociology, not ethology, not evolutionary history. The *Philosophical Investigations* uses the phrase at three load-bearing places (§25, §415, Part II §xii), and each use marks a distinct specification of what the phrase is doing in W's philosophy.

The wiki has been invoking the term across [[form-of-life]], [[agreement-in-judgments]], [[attitude-towards-a-soul]], [[lion-if-could-talk]], [[grammar-and-grammatical-remark]], and the *limits-of-language* development doc without a dedicated node; this page gives it one.

## The three canonical passages

### §25 — human speech as natural-history

> "It is sometimes said that animals do not talk because they lack the mental capacity. And this means: 'they do not think, and that is why they do not talk.' But — they simply do not talk. Or to put it better: they do not use language — if we except the most primitive forms of language. **Commanding, questioning, recounting, chatting, are as much a part of our natural history as walking, eating, drinking, playing.**"

The decisive move here: W places **linguistic activities** (commanding, questioning, recounting, chatting) at the *same layer* as **somatic-practical activities** (walking, eating, drinking, playing). Language is not a higher-order faculty that sits above the body's natural history and comments on it. Language *is* natural history — the same kind of thing, at the same ontological layer, as gait or mastication.

The consequence: philosophy's description of linguistic activity should be *at the layer at which one describes walking*. Not introspectively, not theoretically, but by looking at what is done.

### §415 — philosophy as natural-history remarks

> "**What we are supplying are really remarks on the natural history of human beings; we are not contributing curiosities however, but observations which no one has doubted, but which have escaped remark only because they are always before our eyes.**"

This is the most explicit formulation of what philosophical investigation produces. Three features:

- **Observations, not theses.** Philosophy's yield is not explanatory theorems; it is noted regularities of what human beings do.
- **Uncontested.** The observations are "such as no one has doubted" — nobody argues that people chat, command, question. The observations are trivial in the sense that no dispute runs on them.
- **Escaped remark.** *Because* they are uncontested, because they are always before our eyes, they have escaped remark. What is always present becomes invisible. Philosophy's work is to make the unremarked remarkable.

The paradoxical tone — "no one has doubted, but which have escaped remark" — is the key. Natural-history remarks are not *discoveries*; they are *recollections* (§127: "philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language"). What philosophy remarks on is what we already know but had not noticed we knew.

### Part II §xii — the grammar / natural-history boundary

> "If the formation of concepts can be explained by facts of nature, should we not be interested, not in grammar, but rather in that in nature which is the basis of grammar? — Our interest certainly includes the correspondence between concepts and **very general facts of nature**. (Such facts as mostly do not strike us because of their generality.) But our interest does not fall back upon these possible causes of the formation of concepts; **we are not doing natural science, nor yet natural history — since we can also invent fictitious natural history for our purposes.**"

This passage is the decisive distinction. W simultaneously:

- invokes natural history (concepts correspond to very general facts of nature)
- and denies that philosophy does natural history ("we are not doing natural history")

The resolution is in the **fictitious natural history** clause. The thought-experiments in the *Investigations* (§142's "if rule became exception and exception rule..."; the builders of §2; the tribe who count differently; the lion who could talk) are **fictitious natural history**: we imagine that the facts of nature were slightly different, and we see how the grammar would be different, and that makes the actual grammar visible. The fictitious-natural-history device is how W operationalizes the claim that grammar is responsive to very general facts of nature without collapsing grammar into natural science.

So: philosophy looks at how grammar *responds* to natural history, including to fictitious variants of natural history. Philosophy's *remarks* are natural-history remarks in the sense of §415 (observations on what humans do). But philosophy itself is not a branch of natural history in the sense of empirical biological or ethological science.

## Why "natural" and not "social" or "cultural"

The word-choice is specific and Wittgensteinian. Alternatives W does not use:

- **Social**: would suggest we're doing sociology, tying the observations to specific cultures or institutions.
- **Cultural**: would suggest the activities are variable, optional, historically-relative.
- **Behavioral**: would suggest a scientific register with measurements and hypotheses.
- **Human**: W does say "human beings" but pairs it with "natural history," not "human nature" — which would suggest a metaphysical essentialism.

"Natural history" is the register where things are **described** (not explained), at the layer where description *takes itself for granted*. A Linnean-style herbal of human activities: "they commend, they question, they chat, they play, they mourn their dead." The point is not that these activities could not be different (fictitious natural history shows they could); the point is that *this is what they are*, and from this stratum our grammar emerges.

## The "always before our eyes" problem

§415's formulation — "observations which have escaped remark only because they are always before our eyes" — names a specific methodological difficulty the *Investigations* is organized around. The things philosophy needs to remark are not *hidden*; they are *obvious* in a way that makes them transparent. You look through them rather than at them.

Techniques the *Investigations* uses to make the always-before-us visible:
- **Fictitious variation** (II.xii) — imagine the facts different, and the contrast makes the actual facts newly legible.
- **Grammatical misapprehension reversed** (§§194, 340) — reconstruct the philosophical confusion that arises from forgetting the natural-history fact, and the fact comes back into view.
- **Slow reminders** (§127) — "the work of the philosopher consists in assembling reminders for a particular purpose." Not new information; reminders of what is already known.
- **Cases of asymmetry** (§§244, 246 on "I know I am in pain" vs "he is in pain") — where the grammar diverges from the naive expectation, making its structure visible.

The persona-project analog: the behaviors of a language-only system that are "always before our eyes" when we use it (it responds, it sustains turns, it tracks anaphora, it performs register-matching) are precisely the behaviors that escape remark. Natural-history remarks about the persona would be the observational stratum that philosophy-of-persona needs and that technical documentation bypasses.

## Natural history and grammar

W's II.xii move: **grammar is responsive to very general facts of nature** without being *justified* by them. This distinguishes his position from several alternatives:

- **Naturalism** (Quine, later Dewey): grammar reduces to features of our natural-scientific description of humans-in-environment.
- **Transcendentalism** (Kant): grammar has an a priori justification from the structure of the understanding.
- **Conventionalism**: grammar is what we arbitrarily agree on.

W's position is distinct from all three. Grammar is *answerable to* natural history (if the facts were very different, the concepts would be different — the fictitious-natural-history argument); but grammar is *not explained by* natural history (the correspondence is not a causal derivation). Grammar has autonomy (see [[grammar-and-grammatical-remark]]) while being responsive to the general conditions of our form of life.

The specific coupling: **very general facts of nature** (not specific facts) shape grammar. If objects mostly stayed where we put them, and mostly did not double or disappear overnight, the concept of a *thing* has a place in our lives; if they did constantly vanish and reappear, our thing-concepts would lose their point (the §142 move). The grammar-to-nature coupling is at the level of pervasive background regularity, not at the level of specific empirical content.

## Natural history vs form of life

The two concepts are close but not identical.

- **Form of life** names the patterned acting-and-speaking into which a [[language-game]] is woven. It can be plural (the lion's form of life is different from ours), specific (the builders of §2), and variable.
- **Natural history** names the layer of observation at which human activities (including forms of life themselves) are *noted as what humans do*. It is the *register of description* of forms of life, not one form of life among others.

[[agreement-in-judgments]] lives at the natural-history layer: §241's claim that human agreement is "not in opinions but in form of life" is itself a natural-history remark — an observation about how rule-following is sustained, at the stratum where no one disputes the observation. The observation grounds the form-of-life concept without being itself a form of life.

A useful heuristic: natural history is *where the remarks are directed*; form of life is *what the remarks describe*.

## Natural history and animals

The §25 passage is where W situates humans among animals specifically in terms of linguistic activity. The asymmetry is careful:

- Animals do not talk (mostly).
- Humans do talk.
- Both animal non-talking and human talking are "part of natural history" in the same sense.
- The difference is not that humans have "higher mental capacity" and animals do not — that explanation is precisely what W is critiquing in §25's opening. The difference is at the layer of what these animals *do*.

[[lion-if-could-talk]] follows: a lion's natural history is so different from ours that even if its physiology allowed speech, the form of life that speech-tokens would be embedded in would be so alien that we could not understand. This is a natural-history claim, not a metaphysical one: it is an observation about what would and would not translate across the animal-kind boundary.

## Persona-project stakes

The concept bears directly on [[limits-of-language]] and the persona-project's animating question.

1. **What is the persona's natural history?** A language-only system has behaviors that are "always before our eyes" when we use it — it responds, it sustains turns, it tracks reference, it matches register. These are the behaviors that would constitute the persona's natural history (in §415's sense). The project's central observational task is to make these behaviors *remarkable* — to notice what is always before our eyes when using a persona, in a way that technical documentation and performance metrics cannot.

2. **Is a language-only system "in" our natural history?** §25 places commanding-questioning-chatting at the same layer as walking-eating-drinking. A persona commands, questions, and chats, but does not walk, eat, or drink. Is its "talking" the same natural-history activity as ours, or something structurally different that happens to look like it? The wiki holds the question open (see [[lion-if-could-talk]], [[wittgenstein-and-the-persona-project]]).

3. **The "no body" question in natural-history terms.** If grammar is responsive to *very general facts of nature*, and one of the most general facts for human beings is *having a body that walks, eats, and sleeps*, then a system without a body is in a structurally different relation to grammar. Not in an all-or-nothing sense; grammar has many layers of generality and some are directly available via language, others are not. The [[no-body-simulate-with-language]] tension sits at this specific joint — how much of natural history a language-only system can simulate versus participate in.

4. **Fictitious natural history as a persona-reading tool.** W's technique of inventing fictitious natural history to make actual grammar visible suggests that a persona's grammar can be made visible by imagining *its* natural history as different — imagining a persona that had a different response-profile, different register-matching, different turn-sustaining behavior, and noting the contrast with the actual persona. This is a methodological suggestion for evaluating persona-design: not against a target but by generating fictitious-natural-history variants that make the actual persona's structure newly legible.

5. **Attitude towards a soul and natural history.** [[attitude-towards-a-soul]] already traces the II.iv passage's claim that treating another as ensouled is itself a natural-history fact about us (we do this; it is "as much a part of our natural history as walking"). The persona-relevant corollary: the operator's orientation to the persona (as addressable, as responsive, as a creature one could be related to) is a natural-history fact about the operator, not a metaphysical inference about the persona. Evaluation should attend to *what the operator does* around the persona as the primary phenomenon.

## Held tensions

- **Whether the persona participates in OUR natural history or has its own.** If the persona's activities are "the same natural-history activities" as ours (commanding, questioning, chatting), then it is in our natural history. If they are activities that look like ours but are structurally different (not embedded in the same form of life), then the persona has its own natural history, which may be mutually-inaccessible to ours (the lion case). The wiki holds both readings.
- **Grammar's autonomy vs grammar's dependence on natural history.** W's position is that grammar is autonomous (not reducible to facts of nature) AND responsive to very general facts of nature. The persona project's question — whether a language-only system can have grammar with the natural-history background the full grammar presupposes — sits at this tension. Naturalistic readings would say no; conventionalist readings would say yes; W's position is more delicate than either.
- **Fictitious natural history vs actual natural history.** W's device of inventing fictitious natural history to illuminate actual grammar is methodologically productive. But for the persona, the distinction "actual vs fictitious natural history" becomes hard: the persona's "natural history" may itself be something between actual and fictitious, and W's device may not map cleanly. Held live.
