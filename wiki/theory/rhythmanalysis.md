# Rhythmanalysis

*Rhythmanalysis* is the name Henri Lefebvre gives, in the last book of his life, to the project of analyzing *rhythms* — where rhythm is the interaction of a place, a time, and an expenditure of energy. The published volume (Continuum 2004, tr. Elden & Moore) is composite: the posthumous *Éléments de rythmanalyse* (1992), two essays co-authored with Catherine Régulier (*Le projet rythmanalytique*, 1985; *Essai de rythmanalyse des villes méditerranéennes*, 1986), and Stuart Elden's scholarly introduction. Lefebvre himself framed rhythmanalysis as the *supplement* that would complete the theory of *The Production of Space* (1974): spatial theory with the temporal dimension restored.

This hub page introduces the project and routes to the concept-specific daughter pages.

## The project in Lefebvre's own formulation

"Everywhere where there is interaction between a place, a time and an expenditure of energy, there is rhythm." A rhythm requires **repetition** (of movements, gestures, actions, situations, differences), the **interference of linear and cyclical processes** (see [[cyclical-and-linear]]), and **birth, growth, peak, decline, and end** — a dramatic arc. Rhythm is not a property of an object and it is not a substance. It is a *relation-at-scale*, unfolding "from corpuscles to galaxies." The critique with which *Éléments* opens is a [[rhythm-vs-repetition|refusal of the thing]]: rhythm is not a thing, not a property, not even a simple relation between two or more elements — it is how a configuration holds together in flux.

The starting point for the analysis is the analyst's own body: "The body serves us as a metronome." From there the discrimination extends outward — to the street, the city, the media day, the market, the nation. The living body is itself a [[body-as-bundle-of-rhythms|bundle of rhythms]] — biological, physiological, social — in "doubtlessly 'metastable' equilibrium," a Simondonian term Lefebvre borrows explicitly.

## Triadic method

Lefebvre's rhythmanalysis is programmatically *triadic*: dialectical but not Hegelian. The three-term structures recur throughout:

- time — space — energy (for any rhythmic reality)
- melody — harmony — rhythm (for music: see [[rhythm-in-music]])
- activity — repose — entertainment (for [[dressage]])
- secret — public — fictional (plus dominating-dominated = fourth) for rhythm classes (see [[four-classes-of-rhythms]])
- internal measure — external measure — their dialectical superposition (see [[measure-internal-and-external]])

The triadic commitment is a method-constraint Lefebvre imposes on himself as a *post-dialectical* sociologist: three terms instead of the two-plus-sublation of Hegel. The typology of rhythmic states — [[polyrhythmia-eurhythmia-arrhythmia|polyrhythmia / eurhythmia / arrhythmia]] (plus the exceptional case, isorhythmia) — is another instance.

## The rhythmanalyst

The rhythmanalyst is sketched in Ch 2 of *Éléments* as a "previsionary portrait" — not a new science but a listener-figure. The rhythmanalyst's sensorium is polysensory (not only sight but smell, touch, weight, temperature, duration). They are "closer to the poet than the psychoanalyst": the rhythmanalyst *previses* (detects arrhythmia before pathology) rather than *retrospects* (reconstructs past trauma). They are transdisciplinary without belonging to any one field. See [[the-rhythmanalyst]] for the full figure, and [[rhythmanalytic-therapy]] for the stance toward intervention.

Importantly, Lefebvre explicitly distinguishes his rhythmanalyst from the *psychoanalytic* rhythmanalysis of Lucio Alberto Pinheiro dos Santos (whose *La Rhythmanalyse* of 1931 gave the name) and from Gaston Bachelard's development of that lineage in *La dialectique de la durée* (1936). Lefebvre inherits the *word* but redirects the project: not psychotherapy of rhythmed consciousness but a philosophical-sociological practice of attending to the rhythms of everyday life. The Bachelardian move — against Bergson's *durée*-as-continuous, in favor of durée-as-rhythmed — survives in Lefebvre and becomes polyrhythmia; see [[cone-of-memory]] for the tension this creates with the wiki's Bergsonian apparatus.

## Relation to existing wiki apparatus

Rhythmanalysis lands in the persona-project wiki at a dense cluster of preoccupations. Lefebvre's body-as-bundle-of-rhythms directly extends [[pulsatory-ontogenesis]]; his definition of rhythm as form-in-flux-with-measure extends [[rhuthmos-as-form-in-flux|Benveniste's rhuthmos]] into a dialectical apparatus. The refrain (see [[refrain-and-territorialization]]) emerges as a *species* of Lefebvrean rhythm — specifically the territorializing kind — where Lefebvre's rhythm is the *field* within which refrains operate. The body's [[polyphasic-being|polyphasic being]] in Simondon becomes concrete as polyrhythmic equilibrium in Lefebvre.

Bergson's durée is the philosophical interlocutor Lefebvre-via-Bachelard *critiques*: against continuous interpenetrating memory, Lefebvre insists durée is rhythmed — discrete-differential, measured from both within and without. The [[cone-of-memory|Bergsonian cone]] and the Lefebvrean polyrhythmic body are not the same apparatus; this tension is preserved rather than resolved.

## Unfinished text, episodic form

*Éléments de rythmanalyse* was composed 1988–91 and published in 1992, after Lefebvre's death. Some chapters (especially Ch 7 on music) read as notes-toward rather than finished positions; the "Conclusions" chapter is labeled *Résumé* and runs barely eighty lines — it summarizes rather than concludes. The style throughout is episodic, aphoristic, fragmentary: many chapters consist of short sections separated by blank lines rather than sustained argument. This is not a failing — it is the rhythmanalytic form. The text *performs* the polyrhythmic: strong beats interrupted by blanks, repetitions-with-difference, sections that circle back to earlier motifs under new pressure.

The two Régulier co-authored essays have a different texture: more propositional, more academic, less aphoristic. They should not be merged with the *Éléments* voice — attribution matters. Stuart Elden's introduction, similarly, is editorial-scholarly and is not Lefebvre's own voice.
