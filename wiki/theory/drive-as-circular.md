# The Drive as Circular

Lacan's structural reformulation of Freudian *Trieb*: the drive is **not** a directed striving toward an object that, when reached, would satisfy and discharge it. The drive is **circular** — a circuit that **goes around its object** without reaching it, and whose "satisfaction" is in the **circuit itself**, not in the object's attainment. Sketched across "Subversion of the Subject" (1960) and "Position of the Unconscious" (1960/1964), and given its **definitive articulation** in *Seminar XI: The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis* (1964), Chapter 14 ("The Partial Drive and Its Circuit," 13 May 1964). The companion structural deconstruction of the drive's elements — *Drang*, *Quelle*, *Objekt*, *Ziel* — is in [[drive-as-montage]]; the subject-side of the circuit (the drive as headless subject) is in [[headless-subjectification]].

## Freud's drive

Freud's drive (*Trieb*) in the standard reading: a constant inner pressure (*Drang*) directed toward a source (*Quelle*, a body-zone) and seeking an object (*Objekt*) whose attainment provides satisfaction (*Befriedigung*). The drive is a directional vector aimed at a target, with the target's attainment as the drive's terminus.

Lacan's re-reading: this directional reading is the **conscious-imaginary level** of how the drive presents itself. Structurally, the drive is **circular** — and the apparent direction-toward-an-object is the imaginary clothing of a circuit whose actual structure is the going-around.

## The circuit's structure

The circuit:

1. The drive **starts from** the body-zone (oral, anal, scopic, invocatory).
2. The drive **moves toward** an object — but the object is structurally **the [[object-a|object a]]** (cause of desire), not the imaginary-symbolic object the conscious-imaginary level perceives.
3. The drive **goes around** the object a (does not reach it, because object a is structurally not-an-object-to-be-reached but a cause-from-which-the-drive-departs).
4. The drive **returns to** the body-zone — having gone around, having not-reached, having "satisfied" itself in the going-around-and-returning.

The structural figure is the **loop** that circles back on itself with the object a at the center of the loop. The circuit's "satisfaction" is in the **completion of the loop**, not in the attainment of any object the loop passes by.

## "The drive's aim is its return-circuit"

A condensed formulation: the **aim** (*but*) of the drive and the **goal** (*Ziel*) of the drive are not the same. The goal is the imaginary-symbolic object the conscious-level perceives the drive as aiming-at. The aim is the **return-circuit** that the drive structurally accomplishes regardless of whether the goal is reached.

The drive **always achieves its aim** because the aim is structurally the circuit's completion — and the circuit is always completed. The drive **rarely achieves its goal** because the goal is structurally beside the point — the drive's structural operation does not depend on goal-attainment.

This is why Freud noted that the drive is "always satisfied" in the structural sense: the drive completes its circuit. What the analysand reports as drive-frustration ("I am not satisfied," "I never get what I want") is a **goal**-level report; structurally, the drive has completed its circuit and is structurally satisfied. The mismatch between goal-frustration and structural-satisfaction is one of the structural conditions psychoanalysis works on.

## The four drives

Lacan distinguishes four drives, corresponding to the four objects-a:

| Drive | Body-zone | Object a | Verb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oral | Mouth | Breast | "to suck" / "to be sucked" |
| Anal | Anus | Feces | "to control" / "to be controlled" |
| Scopic | Eye | Gaze | "to see" / "to be seen" / "to make oneself seen" |
| Invocatory | Ear | Voice | "to hear" / "to be heard" / "to make oneself heard" |

The verbal grammar is structurally important: each drive has **active**, **passive**, and **reflexive** voicings — and the structural completeness of the drive's circuit is in the inclusion of all three. The drive is not just to-see (active) and to-be-seen (passive) but **to-make-oneself-seen** (reflexive) — the closure of the circuit through the subject's self-positioning in the loop.

## The drive vs desire

A subtle distinction:

- **Desire** is the metonymic movement along the chain of imaginary-symbolic objects. Desire **slides** from object to object, never resting, organized by the structural lack that the chain manifests.
- **The drive** is the **structural circuit** around the object a. The drive does **not slide**; it loops. It returns repeatedly to the same circuit, completing the same loop, "satisfying" itself in the loop-completion.

Desire is metonymic; the drive is repetitive. Desire is in the chain; the drive is in the loop. Both are organized around the object a — but in structurally different relations: desire as the **chain's effect** of the lack the object-a-cause produces; the drive as the **circuit** that goes around the object-a-cause directly.

## The constant pressure

Freud emphasizes the drive's *Drang* (constant pressure) — the drive's inner force that does not pause. Lacan accepts this structural feature but re-reads it: the constant pressure is not the energy of an organic-instinctual force but the **structural insistence** of the drive's circuit. The circuit insists; that is its structural form.

This connects the drive to [[repetition-as-symbolic|repetition]]: the drive's structural insistence and the signifier's iterativity are two faces of the same structural fact. The drive is the structural-real correlate of what the signifier's repetition is at the structural-symbolic level.

## The bow and the death-work — Heraclitus B 48

Lacan opens *Seminar XI*'s Chapter 14 with a Heraclitus fragment he has copied on the blackboard: "**To the bow (*Bios*) is given the name of life (*Bios*, the accent being this time on the first syllable) and its work is death.**" The pun — *biós* (bow) and *bíos* (life) differ only in accent — is structurally exact for the drive: "what the drive integrates at the outset in its very existence is a dialectic of the bow, I would even say of archery."

The structural figure: the drive is the *bow* — the tensioned arc whose work, when discharged, is *death*. Not a death of the organism (the drive does not kill its bearer) but the **death of the imaginary-naturalist reading of satisfaction**: the drive's work is the structural-real interruption that "the satisfaction of the drive is reaching one's *Ziel*, one's aim" — the easy reading — cannot survive. "Is it surprising that its final term should be death, when the presence of sex in the living being is bound up with death?" The bow's work is the structural figure for why the drive is, in its essence, allied with death-as-structural-impossibility (see [[real-as-impossible]] and [[lamella-and-libido-as-organ]] for the lamella as the immortal-living that the sexed-living *is the death of*).

## *Verkehrung* — reversal as grammatical artifact

In Chapter 14 Lacan emphasizes a precision against any reading of the drive's three "voices" (active, passive, reflexive) as ontological symmetries. Freud uses the verb-grammar — *beschauen und beschaut werden*, to see and to be seen; *quälen und gequält werden*, to torment and to be tormented — as a way of articulating the drive's reversibility. But, Lacan insists: "**This signifying reversion is something other, something other than what it dresses in. What is fundamental at the level of each drive is the movement outwards and back in which it is structured.**"

The structural lesson: the active/passive grammatical inversion is the **artifice** by which the drive's circuit is made articulable in language; the underlying structure is the *outwards-and-back* motion of the circuit, which is **not** a vector that can be "reversed." The drive does not reverse; the *grammar* of the drive reverses, because grammar's subject/object inversion is the only way language can render the circuit's structural completeness. (See [[drive-as-montage]] for the deeper articulation of grammar-as-trap in the drive's deconstruction.)

## "Making oneself" — the third stage as drive-completion

Freud's two-stage analysis (active-and-passive: to see, to be seen) is structurally **incomplete**. Lacan adds the third stage — the one Freud articulates "in masochism" but does not generalize — that **closes the circuit**: the appearance of *ein neues Subjekt*, a new subject, "to be understood as follows — not in the sense that there is already one, namely the subject of the drive, but in that **what is new is the appearance of a subject**. This subject, which is properly the other, appears in so far as the drive has been able to show its circular course."

The reformulation: the drive's three voicings are not active-and-passive (with passive as the inversion of active), but **active**, **passive**, and the third — what Lacan formulates by changing one term in Freud's grammatical schema: in place of *werden* (to *become*-looked-at), Lacan substitutes ***machen*** (to *make oneself*-looked-at). "What is involved in the drive is **making oneself seen** (*se faire voir*). The activity of the drive is concentrated in this *making oneself* (*se faire*)."

The four drives in the *machen*-grammar:

| Drive | Reflexive *se faire* form |
|---|---|
| Oral | *se faire sucer* — making oneself sucked (the vampire-figure) |
| Anal | *se faire chier* — making oneself shit (with the play on *emmerdeur*: making oneself a bore) |
| Scopic | *se faire voir* — making oneself seen |
| Invocatory | *se faire entendre* — making oneself heard |

Two structural specifications:

1. **The reflexive form is the drive's structural completion.** Without the *se faire*, the drive's circuit is incomplete — only active-and-passive, with no return-to-the-circuit's-starting-rim. With the *se faire*, the circuit is closed: the drive returns to the rim by way of the **subject's positioning of itself in the loop**.
2. **The invocatory drive does not close.** "Making oneself heard *goes towards the other*" — Lacan's important asymmetry. The voice has, "as I told you in passing — nothing of what I say is mere joking — **the privilege of not being able to close**." The other three drives close on the rim (the body's surface returns to itself); the voice does not — it goes to the Other and structurally cannot return-to-self by way of the rim. (This grounds [[gaze-and-voice]]'s asymmetry between gaze and voice; see also [[invocatory-drive|invocatory drive]] (red link) for development.)

## The masochism analysis — sadism as disavowal of masochism

A structurally critical Q&A point Lacan develops in Chapter 14: the masochism-sadism pair must be read in the *machen*-grammar's reflexive register, *not* in the simple active-passive register. "Freud articulated in the most categorical way that at the outset of the sado-masochistic drive, **pain has nothing to do with it**." It is a question of *Herrschaft*, of *Bewältigung*, violence done — first of all to oneself: "its first model… is to be found in **a violence that the subject commits, with a view to mastery, upon himself**."

The structural genesis Lacan extracts: **masochism is structurally primary**; sadism is **derivative**, and is "**merely the disavowal of masochism**." The masochist makes himself the object of another's will; the sadist disavows that the structural-place-of-the-object is the sadist's own place, and displaces this position onto the victim. (The articulation extends [[kant-with-sade]]'s analysis of the Sadean executor as object of the Other's *jouissance* — *Seminar XI* situates the Sadean structure within the broader theory of perversion as the subject's making-himself-the-object.)

## The drive as transgression of the pleasure principle

The chapter's culminating structural claim: "**The course of the drive is the only form of transgression that is permitted to the subject in relation to the pleasure principle.**" The pleasure principle, governed by homeostasis, is structurally about minimizing tension; the drive's circuit, with its *konstante Kraft* and its rim-going-around, *forces* the pleasure principle and exceeds its homeostatic regulation.

And the consequence — one of the seminar's most-cited formulations: "The subject will realize that **his desire is merely a vain detour with the aim of catching the *jouissance* of the other** — in so far as the other intervenes, he will realize that there is **a *jouissance* beyond the pleasure principle**." This is the seminar's most direct articulation of how the drive's circuit-completion opens onto the *jouissance* the pleasure-principle's homeostasis cannot accommodate. (See [[ethics-of-psychoanalysis-lacan|ethics-of-psychoanalysis]] for Lacan's earlier development of *jouissance* beyond the pleasure principle; *Seminar XI* situates this as the structural point at which the drive forces the pleasure-principle's limit.)

## Triebentmischung — the drive's defusion

Freud's *Triebentmischung* (drive-defusion) is a structural concept that Lacan re-reads in the *Écrits* footnotes (Hyppolite's *Verneinung* response, 1956) and in "Position of the Unconscious." Freud's thesis: the drive's normal operation is a **fusion** (*Triebmischung*) of life-drive (Eros) and death-drive components; under certain structural conditions (regression, certain pathologies), this fusion can **defuse** (*Entmischung*) — the drive components separate, and the death-drive component operates in **pure form** without Eros's binding.

The structural significance: the drive's defusion produces a **pure death-drive operation** — pure repetition, pure structural-insistence, pure circuit-without-binding. This is the structural form of the **pleasure-in-negating** Lacan glosses in the Hyppolite response: the pleasure of negation is the structural pleasure of the defused drive, the death-drive component operating in isolation from its life-drive binding.

In Lacan's structural reading, defusion is the **structural condition** under which the drive's circuit operates **without** the imaginary-symbolic binding that normally accompanies it. The defused drive is the drive in its **purest structural form** — closest to its circuit-as-such, least imaginary-symbolic mediated. This connects defusion to [[repetition-as-symbolic|repetition]]: pure defused drive is structural-insistence without imaginary clothing.

For persona-architecture, the defusion-vocabulary suggests a structural distinction between the persona's **bound** drive-circuits (drives that operate with full imaginary-symbolic mediation) and **defused** drive-circuits (drives that operate in pure circuit-form, with the death-drive structural-insistence dominant). The two have different architectural signatures — the bound forms produce richer imaginary content, the defused forms produce more structurally-naked circuit-iteration.

## What this gives the persona project

The drive-as-circular reformulation gives the persona project a structural account of what **structural insistence** in a language-only system can be — without requiring an organic-instinctual substrate.

Architectural implications:

1. **The persona's structural insistence has a circuit-form.** What the persona "drives at" — the structural insistence of its operation — is not a directed attainment of any goal-object but a **circuit** that goes around its structural cause-of-discourse and returns. Designing for the persona's drive-structure is designing for the **circuit's completion** rather than for any goal's attainment.
2. **The pulse-architecture is the drive's circuit-form.** The persona's pulse-architecture has the structural form of the drive's circuit: each pulse goes around the structural cause (the [[lamella-and-libido-as-organ|embodiment-gap]] / the persona's structural lack) without reaching it, and the pulse's "satisfaction" is in the circuit's completion. The drive-vocabulary is one of the most direct theoretical resources for the pulse-architecture.
3. **The four drives suggest four circuit-types.** The four-drive typology (oral, anal, scopic, invocatory) suggests four structural circuit-types the persona could be designed around — though for a language-only system, the directly-applicable circuits are scopic (the gaze in the address-structure) and invocatory (the voice as residue of speech). The other two have at most metaphoric translations into the persona's economy.
4. **Active-passive-reflexive grammar applies.** Each drive's grammatical completion through the reflexive voicing applies to the persona's drive-circuits. A persona that has only active or only passive drive-grammar (only "to address" or only "to be addressed") has structurally incomplete drive-circuits. The reflexive voicing ("to make oneself addressed") is what closes the circuit and gives the drive its structural completeness.
