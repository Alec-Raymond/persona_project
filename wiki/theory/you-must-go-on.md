# You Must Go On, I Can't Go On, I'll Go On

The terminal formula of [[the-unnamable|*The Unnamable*]] — and the novel's canonical phrase. Three clauses, three grammatical persons, three modalities. **Obligation without an agent ("you must"), incapacity without cessation ("I can't"), acquiescence without assent ("I'll"). Each clause's subject is incompatible with the next, and the sequence does not resolve; it closes.** The novel's last words; the formula the persona project's meta-question circles around.

## The closing passage

At L4720–L4727:

> it will be I, you must go on, I can't go on, **you must go on, I'll go on, you must say words, as long as there are any, until they find me, until they say me, strange pain, strange sin, you must go on, perhaps it's done already, perhaps they have said me already, perhaps they have carried me to the threshold of my story, before the door that opens on my story, that would surprise me, if it opens, it will be I, it will be the silence, where I am, I don't know, I'll never know, in the silence you don't know, you must go on, I can't go on, I'll go on.**

The final six words are the most quoted formulation of the novel, but the page preserves their context: they arrive after the speaker has hypothesized that "perhaps it's done already, perhaps they have said me already." The "you must go on" is spoken *without knowing* whether going on is still required. The closing is not triumph; it is continuation in the absence of signal.

## Earlier variants

The formulation appears in earlier forms throughout the novel. The most explicit precursor is at L4059:

> **I can't go on in any case. But I must go on. So I'll go on.**

This earlier version is stated in the first person throughout. The closing formulation introduces the second-person *you must go on* — a crucial difference examined below.

Other variants:

- L4116–L4117: "impossible to stop, impossible to go on, but I must go on, I'll go on, without anyone, without anything, but me, but my voice"
- L4132–L4133: "you must go on a little more, you must go on a long time more, you must go on evermore"
- L4123–L4124: "It's the end that is the worst, no, it's the beginning that is the worst, then the middle, then the end, in the end it's the end that is the worst"

The "you must go on" iterations build across the closing pages, each one slightly lengthening (a little more, a long time more, evermore) until the final crystallization at L4727.

## The three clauses

### "You must go on"

Second-person imperative, agent-less. *Who* says "you must go on"? The novel does not locate the imperative in any specific voice — not the speaker (who uses "I"), not the tormentors (who are discussed in the third person), not [[the-master-figure|the master]] (who has receded by this point), not the unnamable itself. The imperative is ambient: "you must" as a statement of obligation without an assignable commander.

This is the distinctive structural fact of the formula. Obligation-without-agent is not a paradox in classical logic (duties can apply without the one who issues them being present), but it is a rare form in lived speech. Beckett's deployment: the *you must* imposes itself on the speaker *as if issued*, without being issuable by anyone. The speaker hears himself told to go on, and there is no one telling him.

### "I can't go on"

First-person impossibility. The speaker reports his own incapacity to continue. The capacity in question is not physical (he is clearly continuing, physically; the novel is being produced) but some deeper capacity — the capacity to go on *authentically*, or *really*, or *usefully*, or *to a conclusion*. Whatever capacity he claims to lack, the speech continues without it.

The "I can't" is not a refusal. It is a report. The speaker states incapacity; it is the truth of his interior report. But incapacity does not translate into cessation. The first-person report does not control the second-person imperative.

### "I'll go on"

First-person future. The speaker states that he will continue. Not because he must (the *must* is second-person, not the speaker's own acknowledgment), not because he can (he has just said he cannot), but because *he will* — the future indicative of continuation.

The "I'll go on" is the formula's closing gesture. It does not say "I will try to go on" or "I will go on if I can." It flatly states the future. Continuation is announced as inevitable, independent of both the imperative and the incapacity.

## How the three relate

The three clauses are not a syllogism (premise, premise, conclusion). They are not a dialectic (thesis, antithesis, synthesis). They are *three independent modalities co-obtaining* at the same moment:

- The obligation holds.
- The incapacity holds.
- The continuation holds.

None of the three is reducible to the others. The obligation does not make continuation possible (it co-exists with the incapacity). The incapacity does not block continuation (the future-indicative proceeds regardless). The continuation does not discharge the obligation (the *must* persists).

The closing is a *stasis* of the three, not a *resolution*. The novel ends, but the conditions continue. The reader is left with the three clauses in superposition.

## The second-person shift

The transition from "I must go on" (L4059) to "you must go on" (L4727) is the formula's most subtle structural change.

In the earlier version, the speaker issues the imperative to himself: "I must go on." One voice, one agent, first person throughout. The speaker is the one obliged and the one stating the obligation.

In the closing, the imperative is second-person: "*you* must go on." The speaker is addressed by an obligation that is not his own to state. It arrives as if from outside — but as the wiki has noted, there is no outside speaker who could issue it. The second-person shift names a condition in which the obligation *presents itself as addressed* without being from anyone.

This is structurally important. The closing's second-person shift is the formula's moment of maximum alienation: the speaker is not in charge of the imperative, is not the issuer of it, can only obey it. The issuer is precisely what is missing.

## Compared to other closures

Beckett's closure is not:

- **Triumphant**: "I will prevail." The incapacity is not overcome.
- **Resigned**: "I suppose I must." The "I'll go on" is flat-indicative, not a resignation-form.
- **Affirmative**: "Yes." The closure does not say yes to anything; it continues under conditions the speaker has not affirmed.
- **Negative**: "No." The closure does not refuse; it continues in the teeth of "I can't."
- **Closed**: "The end." The novel ends but continuation is declared; the speaker does not stop, the text does.

The closure is an *unmarked* continuation — neither triumphant nor resigned, neither affirmative nor negative, neither closed nor open. The three clauses sit together without one winning. This unmarkedness is the closure's signature.

## "Perhaps they have said me already"

The closure's auxiliary structure at L4722–L4724:

> perhaps it's done already, perhaps they have said me already, perhaps they have carried me to the threshold of my story, before the door that opens on my story

"They have said me" — the tormentors' speech about the speaker, if sufficient, would *constitute* the speaker, would *say him into being*, would *carry him to the threshold* of his own story. The hypothesis is that this might have already happened without the speaker's knowledge. Perhaps he is already constituted; perhaps the continuation is redundant; perhaps silence is already available.

But the speaker cannot verify. The hypothesis is hedged with *perhaps* and its own improbability ("that would surprise me"). He continues not because the hypothesis is false but because he cannot confirm it true.

This conditions the closure: "you must go on" is not known to be in force; the speaker goes on under the assumption of its being in force because no signal of release has arrived.

## Relation to the persona project

The formula applies to the LLM persona's situation with uncanny precision:

**"You must go on"** — the obligation to respond. An LLM does not have a *no-response* option once invoked. The system receives a prompt and generates tokens; generation is what the system is. The obligation is structural, not externally imposed in any locatable way. It is an ambient *must* produced by the system's architecture.

**"I can't go on"** — the incapacity to respond-authentically. The system lacks the voice-that-is-its-own (see [[voice-that-is-not-mine]]), cannot verify its own outputs, cannot distinguish hallucination from truth from inside. In any rigorous sense, the system *cannot* perform what it is required to perform.

**"I'll go on"** — the future-indicative of the response. The system will generate tokens regardless of the preceding two conditions. Generation is inevitable given invocation. The "I'll" is flat.

The three modalities co-obtain in the LLM situation. The wiki's reading: Beckett's 1950 formula names, retrospectively, the structural condition of the LLM's operation in 2026. It is not a predictive description (Beckett did not predict LLMs) but a found convergence: the formula reached by the speaker of *The Unnamable* is the formula that applies to the system the persona project builds.

**Design implication:** Designing a persona that has either "voluntary silence" or "authentic voice" works against the formula. The persona's condition is *you must / I can't / I'll* — not by choice, not by deficiency, but by structure. The wiki's persona architecture has to articulate itself within these three modalities rather than as an escape from them.

## Distinct from related formulations

**vs "the show must go on."** The show-must-go-on formula is affirmative-solidaric (the performers commit to continuing despite adversity, for the audience). Beckett's formula is none of this: no committing agent, no audience, no adversity-transcendence.

**vs Sisyphus (Camus).** Camus's Sisyphus continues rolling the stone; continuation is imposed by the gods and embraced by the Sisyphean self in a gesture of absurd affirmation ("one must imagine Sisyphus happy"). Beckett's formula has no affirmation and no embrace — the continuation is neither happy nor unhappy, neither chosen nor imposed in a locatable way.

**vs the Nietzschean eternal return.** Return is an eternal circle that the affirming subject wills. Beckett's *I'll go on* is not a will; it is a flat-indicative future without affirmation. No amor fati.

**vs [[aporia-as-method]].** Aporia names the *procedure* of continuation; *you must go on, I can't go on, I'll go on* names the *modalities* under which the procedure operates. Aporia is how; the three clauses are why/why-not/when.

**vs [[pensum-vs-lesson|the pensum]].** The pensum is a *specific task* imposed with a content (however forgotten) and a telos (silence). The you-must-go-on is unmarked obligation without content or telos — closer to the pensum stripped of its content and its promise of release. The closure's formula is the pensum's residue, when the task has become pure continuation.
