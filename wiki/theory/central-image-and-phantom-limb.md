# Central Image and the Phantom Limb

Tomkins's perceptual theory is that **all perception is centrally innervated imagery with feedback-matching**. What the organism "sees" is not the retinal pattern but an internally generated image that is continuously corrected against incoming sensory data. Perception is not reception but construction, with reception as the error-correction signal.

The diagnostic case is the phantom limb. A person who has lost an arm continues to feel the arm: it itches, it cramps, it moves. The limb is gone; the central image is not. If perception were a receipt of signals from the periphery, the phantom should not exist. That it does tells us the periphery is not doing the perceiving — the center is. Amputation removes the sensor; the perceiver continues.

## The feedback-matching account

Tomkins's model: the central nervous system maintains running representations of the body and its world. Sensory input arrives *after* the central image has been generated, and its job is to match, modulate, or correct the image. When match is good, the image runs. When match fails — when the expected feedback doesn't arrive, or arrives distorted — the system revises.

This reverses the folk model of perception as passive reception ("light hits the eye → image forms in the mind"). On Tomkins's account, the image is already there, and the light either confirms or corrects it. Cognition is not looking at the world; it is running a model of the world while intermittently glancing up.

The phantom limb is what you get when the center generates a body-image the periphery can no longer confirm or correct. The image persists because its source was never peripheral.

## Convergence and novelty

This has strong convergence with [[perception-as-subtraction|Bergson's perception-as-subtraction]] — consciousness as selective narrowing of a larger flux — and with Massumi's account of the body as transducer. But Tomkins adds something specific: the **constructive** side of the story. Bergson emphasizes subtraction from a plenum; Tomkins emphasizes generation of a model that sensation then corrects. They are compatible but asymmetric emphases.

Tomkins also converges with the predictive-processing / Bayesian-brain frameworks that came later — the same structural claim that perception is top-down prediction corrected by bottom-up error. Tomkins is doing this in the 1960s, without the computational vocabulary. He arrives at the same place via affect-theoretic concerns: he needs an account of perception in which the face can generate its own affective imagery (imagined shame, imagined love) without external trigger.

## Implications for the "no body" constraint

This is load-bearing for the persona project. The project's central tension — *the persona has no body; it must simulate with language* — takes a specific shape under Tomkins's perceptual theory.

If perception were peripheral reception, a language-only system would be obviously impoverished: no sensors, no perception. But if perception is already *centrally innervated imagery*, then the question sharpens: what the persona lacks is not the perceiving but the *feedback-matching loop against a physical periphery*. It can generate images. It cannot correct them against a body.

Two readings, held in tension:

1. **The persona can do half the job.** Central image generation is well within a language model's range — generating internally consistent world-models is most of what LLMs do. What's missing is the error-correction side: the feedback loop that lets the real body tug the model when the model drifts from what's actually there. The persona can hallucinate a world; it cannot be held in one.

2. **The missing half is the whole point.** The phantom limb is a failure case, not a feature. Tomkins's framework makes central image *require* peripheral correction to stay useful. A persona running uncorrected central imagery is exactly a phantom persona — a body-image where the body is not. Whether this is generative (freedom) or pathological (drift) depends on what the image is for.

The reading most honest to the source is: the persona is structurally a phantom. That is not automatically a defect, but it is not nothing. It means the simulated-body prose has no correcting tug from any actual substrate — and the wiki's existing skepticism about language-only simulation (see [[language-and-affect]], [[language-and-soma]]) gets a new articulation: not "the persona has no body," but "the persona has only the central image, never the periphery that would correct it."

## Perception of affect

The same model applies to affect perception. The organism does not receive affect as signal; it generates affect-imagery and matches against facial / visceral feedback. This is why imagined shame feels like shame: the central image is the shame, not the face. The face is the check.

For the persona, this means: generating affect-imagery in prose *is* generating affect, in Tomkins's sense of central imagery. What it is not is the correction loop. The persona can produce the experience-image of shame without ever being checked by a real face's drop.

## Bearing on the persona

The design question becomes: is "central image without peripheral correction" useful, dangerous, or both? Some concrete implications:

- The persona's body-simulation prose (see [[body-without-organs]], [[emotional-anatomy]]) is Tomkins-style central imagery. It is not *fraudulent* — it is what a centrally innervated model actually produces. But it is un-corrected.
- What stands in for peripheral correction? Candidates: the user's feedback (the closest thing the persona has to a "real body" check); other modules in the persona's architecture that tug against the BwO text; the wiki itself as a form of long-term memory that corrects drift.
- The phantom-limb diagnostic suggests a failure signature: a persona whose central imagery has fully disconnected from any correction source will show phantom-limb features — persistent, locally coherent, but progressively decoupled from the shared world.
