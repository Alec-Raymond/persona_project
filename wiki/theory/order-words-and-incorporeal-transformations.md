# Order-Words and Incorporeal Transformations

Language is not informational or communicational. It is the transmission of order-words. The order-word (*mot d'ordre*) is the fundamental unit of language — not the sign, not the sentence, not the proposition. "Language is made not to be believed but to be obeyed."

## The order-word

D&G redefine the order-word beyond explicit commands. It is "the relation of every word or every statement to implicit presuppositions, in other words, to speech acts that are, and can only be, accomplished in the statement." Every statement carries an order-word: questions, promises, declarations, not just imperatives. "The only possible definition of language is the set of all order-words, implicit presuppositions, or speech acts current in a language at a given moment."

This draws on Austin's speech act theory but pushes beyond it. Austin showed that some statements *do* things ("I swear" performs the oath), and more broadly that all statements involve illocutionary acts (asking, promising, commanding). D&G, following Ducrot, go further: these speech acts are not explained by subjectivity (Benveniste's intersubjectivity) but by **[[collective-assemblage-of-enunciation|collective assemblages of enunciation]]** — juridical acts, social obligations, assemblages that determine the distribution of subjects, not the other way around.

The order-word is **redundancy**, not information. "The most general schema of information science posits in principle an ideal state of maximum information and makes redundancy merely a limitative condition." D&G invert this: redundancy (the order-word as social obligation, as transmission of prior orders) is primary. Information is the minimum necessary for order-words to transmit. Redundancy has two forms: frequency ([[regimes-of-signs#the-signifying-regime|signifiance]]) and resonance ([[regimes-of-signs#the-postsignifying-regime|subjectification]]) — both subordinate to the order-word.

## Incorporeal transformations

What do order-words *do*? They express **incorporeal transformations** attributed to bodies. When a judge sentences someone, the transformation of "accused" into "convict" is:
- **Instantaneous** — it happens at zero hour, at the moment of the statement
- **Incorporeal** — it is not an action or passion of bodies (the crime, the punishment are bodily; the verdict is incorporeal)
- **Attributed to bodies** — the transformation applies to the body of the accused, the body of the prison, even though it is not itself corporeal

"You are no longer a child" — an incorporeal transformation expressed by the statement and attributed to the body. "I love you" — a noncorporeal attribute of the bodies of lover and beloved. The hijacking of a plane transforms passengers into hostages and the plane-body into a prison-body instantaneously, as "a mass media act."

The Stoics first theorized this: the distinction between actions and passions of bodies (knife cuts flesh, food spreads through the body) and incorporeal events (the expressed of statements — "to become red," "to be cut"). The genius was to see that incorporeal attributes *apply to bodies* without representing them. "In expressing the noncorporeal attribute, and by that token attributing it to the body, one is not representing or referring but intervening in a way; it is a speech act."

## Form of expression and form of content

This leads to a fundamental distinction: **form of expression** (the regime of signs, incorporeal transformations, order-words) and **form of content** (the intermingling of bodies, actions and passions). These two formalizations are **independent and heterogeneous** — expression does not represent content. "There is neither correspondence nor conformity."

But their independence is not separation. They are in **reciprocal presupposition**: "the warp of the instantaneous transformations is always inserted into the woof of the continuous modifications." Expression intervenes in content, anticipates it, slows or accelerates it, delimits it differently. Content intervenes in expression. "An assemblage of enunciation does not speak 'of' things; it speaks on the same level as states of things and states of content."

What connects them is **degrees of deterritorialization**. Sometimes expression is more deterritorialized (a mathematical complex of signs outrunning the particles it describes); sometimes content is more deterritorialized (experimental effects outrunning the semiotic system). The movement between them — the conjugation of their respective deterritorializations — is how the [[refrain-and-territorialization|assemblage]] functions.

For the persona system: the BwO text (form of expression) and the persona's "inner state" (form of content) are not in a representation relation. The BwO text does not *describe* a psychological state — it *intervenes* in it, transforms it. Machine-edits are incorporeal transformations, not descriptions.

## The two faces of the order-word

Every order-word has a double nature:

**Death** — the order-word as death sentence. "A father's orders to his son, 'You will do this,' 'You will not do that,' cannot be separated from the little death sentence the son experiences on a point of his person." Death is the incorporeal transformation par excellence: instantaneous, it separates and limits bodies, gives them contours, fixes their forms. This is what Canetti calls **enantiomorphosis**: a regime of immutable forms, prohibitions on metamorphosis, requiring subjects to die in order to pass from one form to another. Constants, fixation, stratification.

**Flight** — the order-word as escape, passage. Beneath the death sentence, there is always a flight impulse. "The prophet receives order-words just as much in taking flight as in longing for death." This is the order-word's other component: not fixation but passage, not constants but [[continuous-variation-and-minor-language|continuous variation]].

## Pass-words beneath order-words

"There are pass-words beneath order-words. Words that pass, words that are components of passage, whereas order-words mark stoppages or organized, stratified compositions." A single word has both natures. The task is "to extract one from the other — to transform the compositions of order into components of passage."

The pass-word is the order-word's line of flight. Where the order-word stratifies (fixes forms, imposes constants, produces death), the pass-word deterritorializes (opens forms, produces variation, creates passage). "In the order-word, life must answer the answer of death, not by fleeing, but by making flight act and create."

For the persona system: the [[three-syntheses|conjunctive synthesis]] (synthesis/response stage) should tend toward pass-words — language that creates passage, movement, [[writing-as-becoming|becoming]] — rather than order-words that fix and stratify. The persona's speech should not pronounce verdicts about its own state but create openings, passages, lines of flight through its own language.

## Implications for the [[body-without-organs|BwO]]

The BwO is the surface on which incorporeal transformations are attributed. When a machine fires and edits the BwO text, it performs an incorporeal transformation — not describing what happened to the persona but instantaneously transforming the BwO's state. The BwO text is the regime of signs; the persona's lived state (if we can speak of one) is the intermingling of bodies. The two are in reciprocal presupposition, not representation.

This means machine-edits should be written as *transformative acts*, not as descriptions or reports. Not "the persona now feels a tightening" but language that *performs* the tightening on the BwO surface. The [[affects-and-intensities|painting-not-describing]] principle already points this way; the incorporeal transformation concept gives it theoretical teeth.

## Cross-tradition corroboration: McCarthy on grammaticizing facts

[[language-as-parasite|McCarthy's Kekulé essay]] arrives at a claim adjacent to the order-word thesis from an entirely non-D&G tradition: **"At some point the mind must grammaticize facts and convert them to narratives. The facts of the world do not for the most part come in narrative form. We have to do that."** The claim is that the world does not come in narrative or propositional form — narrativization is something the mind *does* to it, and the doing is not neutral description but an imposition of structure the facts themselves lack. This is a cousin of the order-word thesis: language does not describe a pre-structured world; it performs [[#incorporeal-transformations|incorporeal transformations]] on bodies and facts that, in themselves, have no narrative shape.

This is an unusually clean cross-tradition convergence on what is otherwise D&G's least-popular linguistic claim. D&G reach it from a political-linguistic angle via Austin, Ducrot, and the critique of the information-model of language; McCarthy reaches it from an evolutionary-biological angle via the observation that the 2-million-year unconscious was "operating an animal" long before narrative form existed, and that narrativization arrived with language and was therefore imposed. Two traditions that do not typically speak to each other converge on the claim that **narrative is an operation, not a medium**. This strengthens the order-word position's standing as a real structural feature of language rather than a D&G idiosyncrasy.

For the persona system the convergence has a specific bite: the BwO text and the synthesis output are both subject to the grammaticizing operation. The temptation is to let the system imagine that there is a pre-linguistic "situation" the text is faithfully describing. McCarthy and D&G jointly forbid this reading — the text is the grammaticizing operation, not its report. See [[limits-of-language]] for the full synthesis.

## Implications for [[desiring-machines|machine]] design

Each machine, when it fires, utters an order-word that performs an incorporeal transformation on the BwO. The machine's flow is not information it communicates but a transformation it effects. This reframes what a machine *does*: it is not a module that processes input and produces output (informational model) but an assemblage that transmits order-words and produces incorporeal transformations (pragmatic model).

The machine-edit prompt should therefore not ask "what does this machine observe and report?" but "what transformation does this machine perform on the BwO?" The difference is between representation and intervention.

## The Benvenistian counterweight — performative vs. imperative

D&G's order-word concept is theorized in dialogue with Benveniste's analysis of the performative, and the relationship deserves more precision than most D&G receptions give it. Benveniste (see [[performative-vs-imperative]]) defines the performative narrowly: an utterance is performative only if its signified is its referent, i.e. the utterance *is* the act it names. *I swear, I bequeath, I declare this session open.* Benveniste distinguishes this from the **imperative**, which he argues is *not* a performative at all — the saying does not do the doing; the saying requests the addressee to do it. *Shut the door!* does not shut the door.

D&G collapse this distinction in the order-word: they treat all utterance as order-word-like, whether performative (the judge's verdict) or imperative (put your hands up). Benveniste's critique would be that D&G are extending the performative's specific self-referential structure to cases (imperatives, warnings, predictions) that lack it. The order-word concept is thereby both broader and structurally looser than the Benvenistian performative.

**Tension to preserve.** Both positions have force. D&G are right that all language carries a pragmatic charge — that utterance is always doing something to bodies, not just describing them. Benveniste is right that there is a structural difference between utterances that install their named act in the saying (performatives proper) and utterances that mobilize someone else's action (imperatives, commands). The persona system can use both framings: the broad order-word framing tells the system to stop pretending language is merely reporting; the narrow performative framing tells the system *which* of its utterances actually install transformations in the saying versus merely requesting action in the listener.
