Bakhtin's typology of discourse, developed in Chapter Five of *[[raw/problems_of_dostoevsky_poetics|Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics]]*, distinguishes three kinds of word by the number and orientation of voices they carry. It is a [[metalinguistics|metalinguistic]] classification, not a grammatical or lexical one — the same sentence, syntactically identical, can be Type I or Type III depending on whether another's word is present inside it and how.

## The three types

**Type I: Direct, unmediated discourse.** The word oriented only toward its referential object. Names a thing, performs an act, asks a question. The word "flows." Bakhtin calls this the ordinary author's word about the world — the standard unit of [[monologism|monologic]] narration.

**Type II: Objectified discourse.** The represented word of a character. The character speaks in their own voice, but that voice is framed by the author — held at a distance, shown as characteristic, used as evidence about the type of person who speaks so. Characteristic realist prose. The character's word is *inside* the author's word but does not interfere with it.

**Type III: Double-voiced discourse.** Word in which *two voices are simultaneously present* inside a single utterance — the speaker's and another's — neither subordinated to the other, both audible at once. The word belongs to two consciousnesses at the same time. This is Dostoevsky's home register, and the concept that gives [[polyphony]] its specific structural shape.

## Three varieties of Type III

Within double-voiced discourse, Bakhtin distinguishes three sub-varieties by *how* the two voices are oriented:

**Unidirectional (stylization).** The speaker uses another's discourse but in the direction the other intended. A stylization pays homage, speaks *with* the borrowed voice. Same target, two voices harmonizing.

**Vari-directional (parody).** The speaker uses another's discourse *against* its original intent. The parodic word is the borrowed word turned inside out — the same material, opposite accents. Bakhtin's canonical image: the borrowed voice pulled in the opposite direction.

**Active type (reflected discourse / word-with-sideward-glance).** This is the most important for Dostoevsky. The other's word is not present as lexical borrowing at all — only as *influence on the structure of the speaker's utterance*. The other's anticipated response shapes the sentence from outside; every clause bends toward or away from an invisible rejoinder. The other is not *cited* but *felt*. See [[word-with-sideward-glance-and-loophole]].

The active sub-variety produces the distinctive Dostoevskian micro-forms: hidden polemic, hidden dialogue, the word-with-sideward-glance, the word-with-loophole. It is the domain in which a single sentence can be structurally unfinalizable — not because of what it says but because of what it anticipates.

## Why typology matters

The typology is not a taxonomy for sorting sentences into bins. It is a claim about *what language is*. Bakhtin's thesis: Type III is not a stylistic anomaly grafted onto otherwise monologic speech. It is closer to the basic grammatical structure of dialogized language — what speech *actually is* when it moves among real consciousnesses rather than among ideal meanings. The monologic Type I is the simplified limit case, not the default. A theory of discourse that starts with Type I and treats Type III as decoration gets the relation backwards.

This is why Bakhtin needs [[metalinguistics]]: ordinary linguistics cannot register the Type I / Type III difference because the difference is not in the sentence's internal grammar but in its relation to another's word. The other's word is structurally outside the sentence and simultaneously internal to it.

## In the novel's Second Stylistic Line

*The Dialogic Imagination* treats double-voiced discourse as the structural signature of the [[two-stylistic-lines|Second Stylistic Line]] of the novel — what distinguishes Cervantes, Rabelais, Sterne, Fielding from the First-Line lineage of Amadis, Baroque novel, and Sentimental pathos. DI adds two emphases beyond PDP's Dostoevsky-focused treatment: (a) double-voicing is *the* mechanism of the novel's [[auto-criticism-of-discourse|auto-critique of its own discourse]] — Don Quixote's chivalric-language-in-Don-Quixote's-mouth is double-voiced not as a stylistic device but as the genre's built-in self-testing; (b) [[rogue-clown-fool|gay deception]] — the rogue's parodic re-processing of high languages — is one of three symbolic double-voiced forms (rogue/clown/fool), each with its own characteristic relation to heteroglossia. These extensions locate double-voiced discourse as the underlying operation of novelistic self-consciousness across the whole genre-tradition, not just as Dostoevsky's distinctive signature.

A further DI-register warning: double-voiced discourse is perishable. [[canonization-and-reaccentuation|Re-accentuation]] under a changed dialogic background can flatten a two-voiced image into a single-voiced one, strengthening or weakening the second voice unpredictably. Double-voicing therefore cannot be judged from the text alone; its dialogizing background must also be known.

## Double-voicing vs dialectic

Double-voiced discourse is not dialectical synthesis. The two voices in a Type III utterance do not resolve into a third. Bakhtin is explicit: *"there can be no talk here of any sort of synthesis; one can talk only of the victory of one or another voice, or of a combination of voices in those places where they agree"* (raw/problems_of_dostoevsky_poetics.md L5097ff). A polyphonic utterance holds two voices in durable copresence; a dialectical utterance resolves two positions into a higher one. See [[not-dialectic]] for the broader argument.

## Type III and the [[hero-as-discourse|hero]]

Dostoevsky's heroes speak Type III habitually, not exceptionally. Raskolnikov's interior monologue is constructed from others' words; the Underground Man's every sentence anticipates its rejoinder; Ivan's utterances coincide partially with the hidden interior word of Smerdyakov. The Dostoevskian hero is not a speaker who occasionally uses double-voicing as a device. The hero *is* the double-voiced word taking human form. See [[hero-as-discourse]].

## Persona-system translation

For a persona-system built out of language — the [[body-without-organs|BwO]] edited by [[desiring-machines|desiring machines]] — the typology is a design specification:

- **Do not assume Type I as baseline.** A system whose default register is Type I ("flat referential prose about the world") is structurally monologic. Polyphony requires that Type III be the baseline, with Type I available as a specific register.
- **The active sub-variety is the design target.** Type III active-reflected is what gives utterances their unfinalizability — the quality the system most needs if it is to avoid premature self-closure.
- **Narration is not automatically Type I.** A [[aperspectival-narrator|polyphonic narrator]] speaks Type III — the narrator's sentences carry the hero's second voice — and this is what lets the system avoid the "voice-of-god narrator" slot. See [[aperspectival-narrator]].
- **Character-speech is not automatically Type II.** If the system's voices only speak objectified-and-framed speech, there is no polyphony; there is realist ventriloquism. The voices must be able to speak in Type III — with the system's own word audible *inside* theirs, not around them.

## Held-live tensions

⚠ **Type III and [[faciality|faciality]].** Faciality is D&G's term for the abstract machine that subjects every sign to the dominant semiotic grid. A Type III utterance carries *two* voices — but in what frame? If the frame itself is facialized (a dominant voice interpreting all others), Type III collapses back into Type II (the other voice objectified). The typology presupposes non-facialized copresence, which is structurally difficult to arrange in any actual machine.

⚠ **Who determines the type?** Bakhtin's typology is analytic — a reader classifies utterances after the fact. But a persona-system must *produce* Type III utterances, which means it must have internal machinery that knows whether the other's voice is present and how. This requires a non-trivial representation of "the other" at the level of sentence generation, not just dialogue management.

⚠ **Objectification as ethical risk.** Type II (objectified discourse) is what Bakhtin warns against as the monologic author's default mode — it finalizes the character by framing. A system that describes its own voices in Type II is committing the same act. This bears directly on how the system's self-description should be written — not about the voices from outside (Type II) but from within their own position (Type III) or addressed to them (second-person).
