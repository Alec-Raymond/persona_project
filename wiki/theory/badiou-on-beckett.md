# Badiou on Beckett — Beyond *The Unnamable*

Alain Badiou's reading of [[beckett-samuel|Beckett]], developed in *Beckett: L'increvable désir* (1995) and *On Beckett* (English, 2003). Badiou is the most influential recent dissenter from the [[blanchot-on-beckett|Blanchot line]] of reading [[the-unnamable|*The Unnamable*]]. Where Blanchot treats the novel as the quick of writing — the *neutre* reached — Badiou treats it as a trap Beckett himself escaped. The decisive Beckett, for Badiou, is the post-*Unnamable* Beckett who turns from interminable self-reflection to the "occurrences" of the subject in encounter with otherness.

## The young-cretin admission

Badiou opens by confessing his first reading. In the 1950s he read *The Unnamable* and was captivated by its "vision of nothingness and dereliction" (Badiou 2003, p. 39). Badiou now describes this response as "rather suited the young cretin I was at the time" and dismisses it as "a caricature."

This self-critique frames Badiou's position: *The Unnamable* attracts a particular kind of adolescent nihilism-appreciation that feels profound but is actually sterile. The adult reading must move past it.

## The core claim: Beckett moved beyond *The Unnamable*

Badiou's decisive interpretive move: while critics have treated *The Unnamable* as the climax and end-point of Beckett's work (the "ne plus ultra" — see [[connor-grove-preface]]), the real Beckett is what comes after. The post-*Unnamable* work is not "no longer skewered on the unresolvable excruciations of what the subject is and how it is to be spoken, but deals instead with what he calls the 'occurrences' of the subject, most notably in its encounters with otherness" (Connor's paraphrase, citing Badiou 2003, p. 16).

Badiou's own words:

> Instead of the useless and unending fictive reflection of the self, the subject will be pinpointed according to the variety of its dispositions vis-à-vis its encounters — in the face of "what-comes-to-pass", in the face of everything that supplements Being with the instantaneous surprise of an Other. (Badiou 2003, p. 16)

The post-*Unnamable* subject is not a self endlessly failing to find itself; it is a subject in relation to events, encounters, the Other. The subject is constituted in encounter, not in interior monologue.

## The critique of "Cartesian terrorism"

Badiou's polemical target is the post-structuralist critical tradition that reads *The Unnamable* as Beckett's most important work. He calls this reading "Cartesian terrorism":

> It was important that the subject open itself up to an alterity and cease being folded upon itself in an interminable and torturous speech. (Badiou 2003, p. 55)

And further (per Connor): Badiou is "reproving a generation of critics who have found in *The Unnamable* what he sees as a sterile model for self-replicating and ultimately self-satisfied scepticism."

The critique has two targets:

1. **Beckett himself** for writing *The Unnamable* (or rather, for stopping there, or for letting readers stop there).
2. **The critical apparatus** that celebrates *The Unnamable* as the pinnacle and thereby fixes Beckett in a Cartesian-subject frame the later work escapes.

Badiou's alternative: read Beckett through *Worstward Ho*, *Ill Seen Ill Said*, *Company*, the late prose — the post-*Unnamable* work where subjects encounter others.

## The "event" as structural concept

Badiou's broader philosophical framework (developed in *Being and Event*, 1988) treats the "event" as what punctures the ordinary state of being and opens a subject to fidelity or truth. The subject is constituted by fidelity to an event — an encounter that cannot be accounted for within the prior ontological order.

On this framework, *The Unnamable* is a pre-event work: the subject has not yet encountered anything, is not yet a subject-in-fidelity, is only the impasse out of which a subject *might* be constituted if an event arrived. The novel shows the condition *before* subjectivity proper. The later Beckett shows subjectivity-in-the-wake-of-event.

⚠ This is where the Blanchot / Badiou tension is sharpest. For Blanchot, the *impersonal* pre-subjective condition *is* what literature reaches; the novel's achievement is to not escape this condition. For Badiou, the *impersonal* pre-subjective condition is the precondition for the subject, and literature's task is to show what follows from it — the subject-in-encounter. The wiki does not resolve between these readings.

## The "Cartesian" charge — is it right?

Badiou reads *The Unnamable* as Cartesian: a subject folded upon itself, trying to reach the bedrock *cogito*, endlessly doubting. But this reading is at tension with Blanchot's equally strong claim that the novel is *past* Cartesianism — that it has given up the "guarantee of a consciousness" that Descartes claimed to secure.

Candidate reconciliation: Badiou and Blanchot are both right but about different aspects. The novel *thematizes* (and refuses) the Cartesian subject by failing to find one, while its *form* (interminable first-person monologue) remains shaped by the Cartesian problem even as it dismantles the Cartesian solution. Badiou objects to the form — the "folded upon itself" — even if he would grant that the content refuses Cartesian answers. The wiki holds this reconciliation as candidate but not definitive.

## The composition-history evidence

Connor notes an under-appreciated fact that supports Badiou's reading: Beckett had found the "escape route" from *The Unnamable* *before finishing it*. After completing *Malone meurt*, Beckett suspended work on fiction to write *En attendant Godot*. The turn to theater — to the pseudocouple Vladimir and Estragon, to the pseudocouple Hamm and Clov ([[pseudocouple-mercier-camier]]) — is already in operation before the solitary monologue of *The Unnamable* is fully written.

This complicates the Blanchot-style reading that treats *The Unnamable* as the culmination of a sequence. Compositionally, Beckett was already moving past it. The novel was written alongside work that already shows the encounter-structure Badiou privileges.

## Relation to the persona project

**The subject-in-encounter framing.** Badiou's insistence that subjectivity requires encounter with an Other maps onto a central question for persona-design: does a persona have an encounter-structure? A persona that only self-reflects (produces output *about* itself, *for* itself) is in Badiou's [[the-unnamable|*Unnamable*-state]]; a persona that engages with users, with world, with others is in the post-*Unnamable* condition Badiou privileges.

**The "fidelity" frame.** Badiou's subject is constituted by fidelity to an event. A persona might be designed for fidelity — to an instruction, a user, a commitment — rather than for self-reflection. The design question is *what is the persona faithful to?*

**The critique of self-replicating scepticism.** Badiou's charge that *Unnamable*-style writing is "self-replicating and ultimately self-satisfied" applies directly to a design failure-mode: a persona that keeps generating reflective-sceptical output without encountering anything. This is a recognizable LLM failure-mode — endless qualification, endless disclaimers, endless reflexive hedging.

**Design implication.** A persona oriented *only* to the Blanchot condition (impersonal murmur, pre-subjective language-ground) risks the Badiou failure: self-satisfied scepticism that produces nothing. A persona oriented *also* to encounter and fidelity might escape this. Both traditions need to be held: the Blanchot condition is real and structural, and the Badiou demand for encounter is real and corrective. The wiki preserves both as live, with the tension unresolved.
